Correlation, diversification and liquidity

Why diversification fails when it is needed most

Where we are

After following one position along its path in Chapter 1E, this chapter puts positions together and asks when they protect each other. It then prices the exit, before closing with the six equations the rest of the book is written in.

What you need first
  • Variance, standard deviation and the fat tails of real returns (Chapter 1B).
  • The Sharpe ratio, excess return per unit of volatility (Chapter 1D).
  • Drawdown, margin and forced liquidation (Chapter 1E).

1 · Correlation: moving together

Correlation measures how two return series move together over a sample. It is the Pearson coefficient of clinical statistics, computed on returns. For returns XXX and YYY with standard deviations σX\sigma_XσX​ and σY\sigma_YσY​, it is

ρ=Cov⁡(X,Y)σX σY \rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} ρ=σX​σY​Cov(X,Y)​ (1F.1)

where the covariance Cov⁡(X,Y)=E[(X−μX)(Y−μY)]\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−μX​)(Y−μY​)] is the average product of the two returns' deviations from their means. Dividing by the standard deviations puts ρ\rhoρ on a scale from −1 to +1. Two hypothetical assets with volatilities of 20% and 10% and a covariance of −0.006 have ρ=−0.006/(0.20×0.10)=−0.3\rho = -0.006/(0.20 \times 0.10) = -0.3ρ=−0.006/(0.20×0.10)=−0.3. SPX and Treasury bonds had a correlation of roughly −0.3 from 2000 to 2020, meaning they tended to diversify each other.

2 · Portfolio variance: why diversification works

If you hold a fraction w1w_1w1​ of your capital in one asset and w2w_2w2​ in another, with volatilities σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and σ2\sigma_2σ2​ and correlation ρ\rhoρ, the portfolio's variance is

σp2=w12σ12+w22σ22+2 w1w2 ρ σ1σ2 \begin{aligned} \sigma_p^2 = {}& w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 \\ &+ 2\,w_1 w_2\,\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 \end{aligned} σp2​=​w12​σ12​+w22​σ22​+2w1​w2​ρσ1​σ2​​ (1F.2)

Beside each asset's own risk in the first two terms, the third term holds their interaction, which takes its sign and its size from ρ\rhoρ.

σp2=E[(w1a+w2b)2]=w12 E[a2]+w22 E[b2]+2 w1w2 E[ab] \begin{aligned} \sigma_p^2 &= \mathbb{E}\big[(w_1 a + w_2 b)^2\big] \\ &= w_1^2\,\mathbb{E}[a^2] + w_2^2\,\mathbb{E}[b^2] \\ &\quad + 2\,w_1 w_2\,\mathbb{E}[ab] \end{aligned} σp2​​=E[(w1​a+w2​b)2]=w12​E[a2]+w22​E[b2]+2w1​w2​E[ab]​ (1F.3)

Many assets, and why Sharpe ratios do not add

With many assets, collect the weights in a column vector w\mathbf{w}w and the variances and covariances in a covariance matrix Σ\boldsymbol{\Sigma}Σ. Each asset's variance sits on the diagonal and each pair's covariance off it, as in the two-asset case:

Σ=(σ12ρ σ1σ2ρ σ1σ2σ22) \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\ \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix} Σ=(σ12​ρσ1​σ2​​ρσ1​σ2​σ22​​) (1F.5)

For any number of assets, the portfolio variance is then

σp2=w⊤Σ w \sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{w} σp2​=w⊤Σw (1F.6)

Multiplying out the two-asset case gives back (1F.2) term by term. The lesson is that individual Sharpe ratios do not aggregate: covariance dominates. Take two strategies with the same Sharpe ratio SR\text{SR}SR and the same volatility, held half and half. Their mix keeps the same excess return, while (1F.2) shrinks its volatility by a factor of (1+ρ)/2\sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}(1+ρ)/2​. The Sharpe ratio of the mix therefore rises by the inverse of that factor:

SRp=SR 21+ρ \text{SR}_p = \text{SR}\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{1+\rho}} SRp​=SR1+ρ2​​ (1F.7)

At ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0, two strategies with a Sharpe ratio of 0.5 combine to 0.5×1.41=0.710.5 \times 1.41 = 0.710.5×1.41=0.71. At ρ=1\rho = 1ρ=1 they combine to 0.5, with no gain at all. The Sharpe ratio of the mix depends on ρ\rhoρ, which neither strategy's Sharpe ratio contains.

3 · Tail correlation: when it matters

Tail correlation is correlation conditional on extreme events: the same coefficient, computed only over the periods of stress. SPX and Treasury bonds had a tail correlation closer to −0.6 in 2008, when bonds rallied sharply as stocks crashed. In 2022 it was +0.5 as both fell together: the tail correlation flips on regime.

Familiar ground

Restricting a sample to a narrow range weakens a correlation: a biomarker that tracks outcome across all patients tracks it less well within the sickest decile. If returns were jointly normal, the same would hold for them: correlation measured only in the worst stock months would come out smaller than over all months. So when a correlation jumps toward 1 in crashes, conditioning on the crash is not the cause: the dependence in the tail is stronger than a normal model allows. With only a handful of crash months, though, any tail estimate is also noisy.

Most "diversifying" strategies have decent unconditional correlation to equities, say 0.3 to 0.5, but a tail correlation that jumps toward 1.0 in crashes:

StrategyUnconditional correlation with equitiesTail correlation
Betting against beta (BAB) and low volatility, Chapter 10Fabout 0.5about 1.0 in 2008
Quality factor, Chapter 10Fabout 0.5about 0.8 in 2008
Carry trades, Chapter 10Eabout 0.0 to 0.3about 0.7 in liquidity events
Index variance risk premium (VRP), the PUT index, Chapter 10Cabout 0.5about 1.0 in the left tail

The genuine tail-diversifiers are rare:

  • Trend following (Chapter 10D) has about 0.0 unconditional correlation but turns negative in slow drawdowns: its returns were positive in 8 of the 10 largest drawdowns of a 60/40 portfolio.
  • Long Treasuries are regime-conditional, having worked from 2000 to 2020 and broken in 2022.
  • Deep out-of-the-money (OTM) tail puts pay in the left tail but bleed premium in normal regimes.

This is why allocation by individual Sharpe ratio is the wrong framework. Allocation has to be by contribution to the tail behavior of the portfolio. The worked example's last row shows the cost: at a correlation of 1, diversification reduces volatility by nothing.

4 · Liquidity: the cost of getting out

Liquidity is the cost of getting into and out of a position, and it has three components:

  1. The bid-ask spread, the immediate cost of executing. The bid is the highest price a buyer offers, the ask the lowest price a seller asks, and the mid-quote the point halfway between.
  2. Market impact, how much your own order moves the price.
  3. Stress liquidity, how spreads widen in volatile conditions.
Worked example: The spread on a calm day and a stressed one

Step 1: a calm day. Suppose an option is quoted $4.90 bid and $5.10 ask, so its mid is $5.00 and its spread $0.20. Buying at the ask and later selling at the bid costs the whole $0.20, which is 4% of the mid, even if the mid never moves.

Step 2: a stressed day. If stress widens the spread 10 times, to $2.00, selling at the bid lands $1.00 below the mid. That is 20% of the position's value, paid on the day you most want out.

For SPX and XSP options, normal-condition spreads are tight, a few cents on liquid strikes. In stress, spreads can widen 10 to 50 times, so the cost of unwinding a position right when you most need to is enormous. Govindaraj, Li and Zhao (2020) documented pre-earnings bid-ask spreads on single-stock options averaging 31.7% of the mid-quote.

Academic backtests use mids, while live profit and loss (P&L) uses real fills. The gap between paper and reality is mostly here (Chapter 2D and Chapter 13A).

Familiar ground

A backtest filled at the mid is a per-protocol analysis: it counts only perfect execution. Your account is the intention-to-treat result, with every real fill included.

5 · Signal, strategy and allocation

Three different things get called "an edge", and conflating them produces confused thinking:

  • A signal is an observation that updates probabilities, such as the ratio of the VIX to the VIX3M rising above 1.05. The VIX measures the implied volatility of SPX options over the next 30 days and the VIX3M over the next 3 months (Chapter 9A).
  • A strategy is a rule that translates signals into trades, such as "halt put-writing when the signal triggers".
  • An allocation is how much capital each strategy gets, such as "10% of portfolio risk to the put-write sleeve".

On its own, a signal is not tradable. A strategy without an allocation is only a backtest. An allocation without strategies is a portfolio policy. When someone calls something "a great edge", ask whether they are describing a signal, a strategy or an allocation. Most "great edges" are signals dressed up as strategies, with no allocation discipline behind them.

6 · The six equations the book is written in

Learn the meaning, not just the form, of the six equations the book uses again and again.

  1. Expected value (Chapter 1C), where ppp is the win probability, GGG the gain on a win, LLL the loss on a loss and ccc the friction of commissions, taxes and slippage:
E[R]=pG−(1−p)L−c \mathbb{E}[R] = pG - (1-p)L - c E[R]=pG−(1−p)L−c (1F.8)
  1. The Sharpe ratio (Chapter 1D), excess return per unit of volatility, with μ\muμ the mean return, rrr the risk-free rate and σ\sigmaσ the volatility:
SR=μ−rσ \text{SR} = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} SR=σμ−r​ (1F.9)
  1. Tail loss, or expected shortfall (Chapter 1B): the average loss conditional on being in the worst tail. At the 95% level it averages the worst 5% of losses, those beyond the value at risk VaR95\text{VaR}_{95}VaR95​. In the formula below, the loss LLL is the return with its sign reversed. This makes ESα\text{ES}_\alphaESα​ Chapter 1B's expected shortfall written as a positive number.
ESα=E[L∣L>VaRα] \text{ES}_\alpha = \mathbb{E}[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_\alpha] ESα​=E[L∣L>VaRα​] (1F.10)
  1. The retail-realism haircut (Chapter 13A) multiplies a paper Sharpe ratio by one factor hhh for each layer of friction, each typically 0.7 to 0.9. The cumulative haircut is often 0.3 to 0.5: three factors of 0.75 multiply to 0.42, so a paper Sharpe ratio of 0.8 would become about 0.34.

  2. The conviction threshold (Chapter 13B): trade only if the estimated net return μ^net\hat{\mu}_\text{net}μ^​net​ beats a penalty λ\lambdaλ on tail risk plus a penalty κ\kappaκ on execution cost CexecC_\text{exec}Cexec​.

Chapter 1G uses these primitives to explain why edges exist at all, sorting them into four sources: risk premium, behavioral, institutional and microstructure. Chapter 1D has already shown why the Sharpe ratio flatters short-volatility strategies. Chapter 13A uses the haircut equation to derive realistic retail expectations. Without these primitives those chapters would be claims, but with them they are conclusions. The rest of the book assumes the primitives of Chapters 1A to 1F are second nature.

In practice

Before you add a "diversifier", look up its correlation with your equity holdings in the worst months, not across all months. Before you size a position, find its spread on a calm day and ask what it would be on a stressed one. Both numbers matter most when a margin call (Chapter 1E) forces you to sell.

When it fails

Correlations are estimated from the past, and they flip on regime. Long Treasuries diversified equities from 2000 to 2020 and then fell with them in 2022. A tail correlation measured over one crisis may not describe the next.

Common mistake: allocating by each strategy's Sharpe ratio

Ranking strategies one at a time by Sharpe ratio and funding the best ones ignores what decides the portfolio's fate in a crash: how the strategies move together in the left tail. Several strategies that each look diversifying can become one position in a crash.

Decision rule
  • Allocate by each strategy's contribution to the portfolio's tail behavior, not by its own Sharpe ratio.
  • For any options strategy, ask: "Could I close this position in the worst conditions?" If the answer requires the bid to be tight, the answer is "probably not", and you should size accordingly.
  • When you meet a claimed edge, name it first as a signal, a strategy or an allocation.
What to carry forward
  • Correlation is covariance scaled to run from −1 to +1, and portfolio variance is w⊤Σw\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}w⊤Σw.
  • Diversification exists only while correlation is below 1. Most diversifiers' correlation rises toward 1 in crashes.
  • Liquidity is a cost with three parts, of which the stress part decides whether you can get out.
  • A trade needs all three of a signal, a strategy and an allocation, which are different things.

7 · Check yourself

Question 1

What is the difference between unconditional correlation and tail correlation? Why does it matter for portfolio construction?

Answer

Unconditional correlation is measured over every period in a sample, tail correlation only over the extreme ones. A diversifier's value lies in the crash, so the tail figure is the one that matters. The PUT index has about 0.5 unconditional correlation with equities but about 1.0 in the left tail, which makes it no diversifier when you need one.

Question 2

You hold two strategies half and half, each with a Sharpe ratio of 0.6 and the same volatility. What is the Sharpe ratio of the mix if their correlation is 0.5?

Answer

By (1F.7) the mix has SRp=0.62/1.5=0.69\text{SR}_p = 0.6\sqrt{2/1.5} = 0.69SRp​=0.62/1.5​=0.69, against 0.85 if the correlation were 0 and an unchanged 0.6 if it were 1.

Question 3

An option with a mid of $2.00 has a spread of $0.10 on a calm day and 30 times that on a stress day. What does it cost to sell at the bid on each day?

Answer

Selling at the bid costs half the spread below the mid. On the calm day that is $0.05, or 2.5% of the mid. On the stressed day the spread is $3.00, so the cost is $1.50, or 75% of the mid.

Question 4

A newsletter reports that when a certain indicator crosses a level, the index tends to rise over the next week. Is this a signal, a strategy or an allocation, and what is missing?

Answer

It is a signal, an observation that updates probabilities. It lacks both a strategy, the rule for what to buy and when to exit, and an allocation, the capital that rule would get. Until both exist there is nothing to trade.
