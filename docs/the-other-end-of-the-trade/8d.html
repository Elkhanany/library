Part VIII · The whole market · Chapter 8D

Gamma squeezes

How to recognize one

Where we are

Chapter 8C described four episodes after they ignited, and this chapter is its operational counterpart. It shows how to spot a gamma squeeze setup before the feedback runs, which live indicators separate a real squeeze from a noisy uptrend, and when not to chase one despite the headline pattern.

What you need first
  • The three preconditions and the four episodes behind them (Chapter 8C).
  • Gamma exposure and its sign conventions (Chapter 8A).
  • Short interest and short covering (Chapter 8C).
  • A moving average: the average of the last nnn values of a series, recomputed each day as the window moves forward.

1 · The three preconditions, as a checklist

From the case studies of Chapter 8C, three preconditions consistently co-occur: Volmageddon in 2018, March 2020, GME in 2021 and August 2024 all had them. Any one alone is insufficient.

PreconditionWhat it meansHow to measure
Structural concentrationEither the dealer book is heavily short gamma in one direction, or a meaningful share of the float is shorted (single names)Net dealer GEX sign and magnitude; FINRA short-interest reports for single names
Mechanical buying pressureA flow source that requires purchases at progressively higher prices: short covering, dealer delta hedging, leveraged-ETF rebalancingVolume composition, dealer-side flow inference, and the size of leveraged ETFs in the name
Liquidity constraintCounterparty inventory or capital cannot expand to absorb the flow: wide bid-ask spreads, broker margin calls, exchange circuit logicBid-ask spread widening; broker outage signals; price paths that gap up rather than expand continuously

When all three are present, squeeze conditions exist. Whether the squeeze triggers is a question of catalyst: news, positioning around options expiry, or retail coordination. FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, publishes the short-interest figures the first row uses.

The first row also measures GEX by its magnitude, and there the Edge Book disagrees. It keeps only the sign of GEX, as low-confidence regime context, and calls the magnitude too model-dependent to size on (Chapter 10H). It files dealer gamma effects under microstructure edges, which it considers institutional-only (Chapter 1G), and it rejects trading GEX levels for retail accounts (Chapter 10I). Read the row's GEX entry as a sign, checked against the conventions of Chapter 8A, and not as a number to size from.

2 · Four live indicators: P, V, G and D

The four univariate indicators of the SqueezeMetrics framework, P, V, G and D, map onto the preconditions as live, computable signals. The letters are the framework's own names: this G is a ratio of call gamma to total gamma, between 0 and 1, not the destabilizing gamma of Chapter 8B.

P, the price trend, divides the average daily return rrr over 21 days by the average size of a return, ∣r∣|r|∣r∣, over the same days:

P=MA21(r)MA21(∣r∣) P = \frac{\text{MA}_{21}(r)}{\text{MA}_{21}(|r|)} P=MA21​(∣r∣)MA21​(r)​ (8D.1)

An average of returns can never be larger in size than the average of their sizes, so PPP always lies between −1 and +1. It is +1 when every day in the window rose, −1 when every day fell, and near 0 when the ups and downs cancel.

Worked example: Two 21-day windows

Step 1: a steady climb. Suppose that in the window 15 days rise 1% and 6 days fall 0.5%. The average return is (15×1%−6×0.5%)/21=0.571%(15 \times 1\% - 6 \times 0.5\%)/21 = 0.571\%(15×1%−6×0.5%)/21=0.571%, and the average size is (15×1%+6×0.5%)/21=0.857%(15 \times 1\% + 6 \times 0.5\%)/21 = 0.857\%(15×1%+6×0.5%)/21=0.857%. By (8D.1), P=0.571/0.857=0.67P = 0.571/0.857 = 0.67P=0.571/0.857=0.67, above the 0.4 line.

Step 2: a noisy drift. Now suppose 11 days rise 1% and 10 days fall 1%, so the average return is 1%/21=0.048%1\%/21 = 0.048\%1%/21=0.048%, the average size is 1%, and P=0.048P = 0.048P=0.048. The window ended higher, but nothing in it looks like mechanical buying.

V, the volatility trend, takes mad, the average absolute daily move over a recent window, and subtracts its own moving average. If recent moves average 1.5% a day against a moving average of 1.0%, V is +0.5 points: volatility is expanding. A quiet uptrend has P high and V near zero or below. A squeeze has both high, because the price is rising and the market is struggling to absorb the flow.

G, the gamma ratio, computes each strike's gamma at one constant volatility, 20%, so that the skew does not distort the comparison, and weights it by open interest. It then divides the calls' total by the total of calls and puts. A book with 3.0 units of call gamma and 1.5 of put gamma gives 3.0/4.5=0.673.0/4.5 = 0.673.0/4.5=0.67, a call-heavy book past the 0.6 line.

D, the dark ratio, is the share of dark pool volume reported as short sales, averaged over five days. The usual reading rests on how off-exchange trades are reported. A market maker who fills a customer's purchase by selling short reports short volume, so a rising short share can mean rising buying underneath the visible price.

All four are normalized to roughly −1 to +1 with the hyperbolic tangent, applied over a rolling window, so that they can be plotted on one axis:

tanh⁡(x)=ex−e−xex+e−x \tanh(x) = \frac{e^{x} - e^{-x}}{e^{x} + e^{-x}} tanh(x)=ex+e−xex−e−x​ (8D.2)

Near zero, tanh passes a value through almost unchanged; far from zero, it flattens toward ±1. By (8D.2), tanh⁡(0.5)=0.46\tanh(0.5) = 0.46tanh(0.5)=0.46, tanh⁡(1)=0.76\tanh(1) = 0.76tanh(1)=0.76 and tanh⁡(2)=0.96\tanh(2) = 0.96tanh(2)=0.96, so large readings are squeezed together while small ones keep their differences.

The interesting joint signals are rare, because on most days the four diverge. When they align, with P, V, G and D all high, the structural setup for a squeeze is in place. That is the moment to check the catalyst, not to bet ahead of it.

Familiar ground

An alignment of all four indicators is a positive screen, not a diagnosis. When a condition is rare, even a specific screening test produces positives that are mostly false, which is why a positive screen leads to a confirmatory workup rather than to treatment. Here the workup is the catalyst check: news, expiry positioning or retail coordination. Until one of those appears, the alignment has raised the probability of a squeeze, not established it.

3 · Single-name squeezes against index squeezes

The mechanism is identical in the two kinds of squeeze, but the structural conditions differ in kind.

AspectIndex gamma squeeze (Volmageddon, March 2020, August 2024)Single-name gamma squeeze (GME)
Required short-interest concentrationNone: the mechanism runs through dealer hedging aloneCritical: typically above 20% of float, often above 50%
Required catalystA volatility shock, idiosyncratic or macroCoordinated retail call buying, plus a narrative
SpeedHours to daysDays to weeks
AsymmetrySymmetric: positive or negative GEX can blow outStrongly asymmetric: the long side, with short-side squeezes rare
Liquidity constraint works throughMarket-maker capital and market depthBrokerage capital and DTCC margin
ResolutionVolatility fades once dealers' capacity to rebalance is restoredRetail attention fades, short covering completes, and broker restrictions break the loop

Most published "gamma squeeze" commentary conflates the two. The trades are different, the risk profiles are different, and the resolution paths are different. Tag every squeeze read explicitly as single-name or index before you size it.

4 · When to chase, when to fade, when to step aside

Three rules follow from the case studies of Chapter 8C:

  1. Do not chase what is already running. Once GME hit $200, the squeeze was visible. The dealer hedging that drove it from $20 to $200 had largely completed. The path from $200 to $483 was retail fear of missing out (FOMO) and short covering, not gamma, and its risk-reward was much worse.
  2. Liquidity constraints break loops abruptly. Broker restrictions, exchange halts and clearinghouse margin all act as discrete shock absorbers that can collapse the feedback in minutes. Size any squeeze trade on the explicit assumption that 50% of the intraday peak is the realistic exit price under broker-restriction scenarios.
  3. Cover before the catalyst clears. Squeezes resolve when the structural condition resolves: short interest covered, dealer hedge inventory normalized, or retail attention faded. Whichever resolves first defines the exit. Wait for the one you can actually observe, such as broker restrictions, dealer-side rebalancing footprints in the tape or short-interest reports. Do not wait for the one you would prefer, price exhaustion, which you cannot predict ahead of time.
Worked example: Sizing for an exit at half the peak

Step 1: the scenario. Suppose you buy a squeezing stock at $120 and it runs to an intraday peak of $200. The second rule says to plan for an exit at half the peak, $100.

Step 2: the loss to size for. From $120, an exit at $100 loses $20 a share, about 17%, even though the stock traded far above your entry.

Step 3: the size. If you can accept a loss of $2,000 on the trade, you can hold 2,000/20=1002{,}000/20 = 1002,000/20=100 shares. A stop 10% below entry, $12 a share, would have allowed 166 shares, and in the restriction scenario those shares would lose about 166×20=$3,320166 \times 20 = \$3{,}320166×20=$3,320.

In practice

Keep the checklist as a small table and update it as data arrive. Tag each precondition MET when it is measured and present, WATCH when it is building, NO when it is absent, and NEEDS DATA when you cannot measure it. P and V need only daily prices. G needs the option chain with its open interest, and D needs FINRA's dark-pool short volume. A NEEDS DATA tag is not a NO: it means the checklist is incomplete, and an incomplete checklist does not support a trade.

When it fails

The checklist is built from four episodes, read after the fact, and the joint signal is rare. Loops also break abruptly, often in minutes, when a broker restricts buying or an exchange halts trading, and the price can gap through any stop. That is why rule 2 sizes for an exit at half the peak rather than at a stop price.

Decision rule
  • All three preconditions met, no catalyst yet: check the catalyst; do not bet ahead of it.
  • Squeeze already visible and running: do not chase it; the gamma part of the move is largely done.
  • In a squeeze trade: size for an exit at half the intraday peak, and exit when the first observable structural condition resolves.
  • Before any of this: tag the setup as single-name or index, and read any GEX input as a sign, not a size (Chapter 10H).
Common mistake: chasing a visible squeeze

The headline pattern, a stock up several times in days, looks like the setup. By then the dealer hedging that drove it has largely completed, and what remains is attention and short covering, which can stop in minutes. The GME path from $200 to $483 was that phase, with much worse risk-reward than the move before it.

Common mistake: treating an index squeeze like a single-name one

Commentary often uses one word for both kinds of squeeze. An index squeeze needs no short interest, can blow out in either direction and resolves as dealers' capacity to rebalance returns. A single-name squeeze needs heavy short interest and retail call buying, runs on the long side and ends with attention, covering or a broker's restriction. Tag the setup before you size it.

What to carry forward
  • A squeeze needs structural concentration, mechanical buying and a liquidity constraint together, plus a catalyst to trigger it. Any one alone is insufficient.
  • P, V, G and D turn the preconditions into live signals; their alignment is rare and calls for a catalyst check, not a bet.
  • Single-name and index squeezes share a mechanism but differ in short interest, catalyst, speed, asymmetry and resolution.
  • Do not chase what is running, size for an exit at half the intraday peak, and exit on the first structural condition you can observe.

5 · Check yourself

Question 1

Over 21 days a stock rises 0.8% on 14 days and falls 0.4% on 7. What is P, and does it pass the 0.4 line?

Answer

The average return is (14×0.8%−7×0.4%)/21=8.4%/21=0.4%(14 \times 0.8\% - 7 \times 0.4\%)/21 = 8.4\%/21 = 0.4\%(14×0.8%−7×0.4%)/21=8.4%/21=0.4% and the average size is (11.2%+2.8%)/21=0.667%(11.2\% + 2.8\%)/21 = 0.667\%(11.2%+2.8%)/21=0.667%. So P=0.4/0.667=0.6P = 0.4/0.667 = 0.6P=0.4/0.667=0.6, which passes the line.

Question 2

A stock has short interest of 60% of its float and a gamma ratio of 0.7. Its spreads are normal and its price rises steadily without gaps. Do squeeze conditions exist?

Answer

Not yet. Structural concentration is met, and mechanical buying may be building, but there is no sign of a liquidity constraint. Tag that row NO, or WATCH if spreads start to widen, and wait.

Question 3

You bought shares at $80 in a squeezing stock, and the intraday peak is $150. What exit price does the second rule tell you to plan for, and what does it cost you?

Answer

Half the peak, $75, a loss of $5 a share against your entry, although the stock traded almost twice as high. Size the position so that this loss fits your budget.

Question 4

All four indicators are high on the same day. What do you do next, and why?

Answer

Check the catalyst: news, positioning around options expiry or retail coordination. The alignment is a positive screen that raises the probability of a squeeze, not a reason to bet ahead of one.

Question 5

Why tag a squeeze as single-name or index before sizing it?

Answer

The two differ in what they require, how fast they run, which way they can break and how they end. An exit plan that suits one can be wrong for the other: a single-name squeeze can run for days to weeks, while an index squeeze can run its course in hours.
