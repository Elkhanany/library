Part VIII · The whole market · Chapter 8D

Two regimes

When hedging calms the market and when it pushes it

Where we are

Chapters 8A to 8C added every dealer's gamma into one number, gamma exposure, showed that its sign rests on an assumption about who holds what, and drew it strike by strike. This chapter shows what each sign does to prices: calm, mean-reverting markets on one side, trending and volatile ones on the other. It gives a simple model of the feedback and traces the model to the theory it comes from, from the portfolio insurance of 1987 to modern models of market impact. It also says what the model's feedback coefficient means in dollars and index points.

What you need first
  • Gamma exposure (GEX), and the dealers' hedge trade for a given move (Chapter 8A).
  • Why the sign of GEX is an assumption, and the two conventions that fill it in (Chapter 8B).
  • Why a long-gamma hedge trades against a move and a short-gamma hedge trades with it (Chapter 6D).
  • The AR(1) model, in which each return carries a fixed fraction, the weight www, of the one before (Chapter 3B).
  • Realized volatility against implied volatility (Chapter 5E), and the square-root law of market impact (Chapter 2D).

Everything in Parts I to VII compresses to a single regime question: is the dealer book net long gamma or net short gamma? The answer sets the sign of the feedback loop between hedging and prices, and with it the whole character of intraday price action.

In plain terms

A dealer who is long gamma behaves like a thermostat, cooling the room when it warms and heating it when it cools. A dealer who is short gamma behaves like a microphone held too close to its speaker, so that every sound comes back louder. The news that moves prices is the same in both cases. What differs is whether the dealers' hedging pushes back against each move or joins it.

1 · Two regimes, one mechanism

The two regimes are mirror images of each other, row by row:

A toy example shows the mechanism. Suppose GEX is +$5 billion per 1% move. By Chapter 8A's hedge-flow formula, a 1% rally then has dealers selling about $5 billion of index exposure and a 1% fall has them buying about $5 billion, so each move meets a flow against it. If GEX is −$5 billion instead, the same rally has dealers buying about $5 billion and the same fall has them selling about $5 billion. The news, and the first 1% move it caused, are identical in both cases. Only the sign of the response differs.

The table is the Dealer Mechanics Lab's, and the theory later in this chapter adds two rows to it. The sign of dealer gamma changes liquidity as well as volatility, and neither sign makes prices better informed.

The figure below draws six price paths from 100 over 60 steps. Under positive GEX the six paths stay between about 99.5 and 101. Under negative GEX the same shocks spread them from about 98.9 to 102.5, more than twice as wide. In the language of Chapter 3B, each regime makes returns an AR(1) process, with weight w=−0.45w = -0.45w=−0.45 (mean reversion) when dealers are long gamma and w=+0.45w = +0.45w=+0.45 (momentum) when they are short gamma. One mechanism produces both behaviors.

The same fundamentals can therefore produce qualitatively different price behavior, depending on the dealer-positioning regime. That is why regime-conditional strategy selection, the subject of Part XI, matters. The weight of 0.45 is chosen so the difference shows in six paths. It is an illustration, not a measurement, and the measured effects are far smaller, as a later section shows.

3 · The mathematics of feedback

Published GEX is positive when hedging is stabilizing, but the feedback maths reads more naturally with the opposite sign. So define the destabilizing gamma GGG as the negation of published GEX, in whatever units the model uses. It is positive when dealers are net short gamma, so that their pro-trend hedging amplifies moves. It is negative when they are net long gamma, so that their contra-trend hedging damps moves. Familiar ground

The feedback multiplier works like the design effect of a cluster-randomized trial. When outcomes within a cluster are positively correlated, the variance of an estimate is inflated by a factor that grows with the correlation, while a negative correlation would deflate it. Returns work the same way: positive feedback correlates each step with the one before and inflates the variance of the cumulative move, while negative feedback deflates it. The variance ratio test of Chapter 3B measures exactly this inflation.

Instant or lagged: what the regimes do to autocorrelation

The grind box summed its rounds within one instant, and the regime figure spread them over successive steps. Both give the same multiplier for the volatility of the cumulative move, σ/(1−w)\sigma/(1 - w)σ/(1−w). They differ on autocorrelation. If dealers hedge within the instant, each return is larger or smaller than the news, but it carries nothing of the return before: volatility changes and autocorrelation does not. If they hedge one step late, as in the figure, each return carries www times the one before, and the lag-1 autocorrelation is www itself.

The table's rows on mean reversion and momentum are therefore the lagged reading, and two lines of research bear on it. Baltussen, Da, Lammers and Martens (2021) study intraday returns on over 60 futures markets from 1974 to 2020. They find that "the return during the last 30 minutes before the market close is positively predicted by the return during the rest of the day". They link that intraday momentum to the gamma hedging demand of option market makers and leveraged ETFs. The predictive power "reverts over the next days".

Grossman and Zhou (1996) model portfolio insurers in general equilibrium. Like a dealer short gamma, an insurer sells as prices fall and buys as they rise, and a later section returns to the insurers of 1987. Insurers sell to other investors in bad states, and to induce those investors to buy, the risk premium must rise. The authors show that portfolio insurance "increases price volatility, causes mean reversion in asset returns". So the price overshoots within the move and partly comes back afterward. Short gamma means momentum within the move, not necessarily over the days that follow.

The measured sizes are small beside the figure's 0.45. In single stocks, Yang (2015), a job-market paper that was not published, finds that moving from the lowest to the highest quintile of option writers' hedging demand raises daily return autocorrelation from −5.0% to −1.6%. That is a change of 3.4 percentage points of autocorrelation, where the figure's weight of 0.45 is an autocorrelation of 45%. Chapter 8G gives the rest of the measured effects.

4 · The theory behind the formula

The formula is older than the Dealer Mechanics Lab. A line of papers from the 1990s derived it, or close relatives of it, from explicit models of hedgers whose trades move prices. Knowing that literature tells you what the formula assumes and where it stops being true.

Frey and Stremme, and Frey

Frey and Stremme (1997), in Mathematical Finance, split a market into two groups. "Reference traders" are price takers with an ordinary demand for the stock. "Program traders" follow a hedging rule for a book of options they have written, and so must trade whenever the price moves. Setting supply equal to demand gives the price process, and the abstract reports "an explicit expression for the transformation of market volatility" under the hedgers' impact:

It turns out that volatility increases and becomes time and price dependent. The strength of these effects however depends not only on the share of total demand that is due to hedging, but also significantly on the heterogeneity of the distribution of hedged payoffs. Frey and Stremme (1997)

In their logarithmic-demand case, as later papers restate it, the volatility becomes σ/(1−ρSϕS)\sigma/(1 - \rho S \phi_S)σ/(1−ρSϕS​). Here ρ\rhoρ measures how much the hedgers' trading moves the price, and ϕS\phi_SϕS​ is how fast their holding changes with the price. That is the book's σ/(1−κG)\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)σ/(1−κG), with κ=ρ\kappa = \rhoκ=ρ and G=SϕSG = S\phi_SG=SϕS​. The last clause of the abstract matters for the rest of this part. A hedged book spread evenly over many strikes changes volatility everywhere a little, while a book concentrated at one strike changes it a lot near that strike and little elsewhere. That is the theoretical root of strike-local effects, pinning among them (Chapter 8E).

Frey (1998), in Finance and Stochastics, gives the cleanest version. One large trader's holding moves the price directly, and the trader replicates a claim by holding its delta. The attempt produces a nonlinear version of the Black-Scholes equation, whose solution Frey proves exists and is unique.

State Frey's assumptions whenever you quote the formula: impact is linear and permanent, the hedger rebalances continuously to the delta of a known book, and the feedback stays below its critical value. A later section says how each assumption fails.

Other derivations, and a counter-result

Three more papers of the 1990s reach the same shape from different starting points.

  • Platen and Schweizer (1998) add hedgers' demand to noise and speculative demand in an equilibrium model. Hedging makes volatility "endogenously determined by agents' trading behavior", and their simulations "quantitatively substantiate the idea that option price distortions can be induced by feedback effects from hedging strategies". The feedback bends the implied volatility smile and skew of Chapters 5E and 5G, not only realized volatility.
  • Sircar and Papanicolaou (1998) use reference traders and "programme traders" who hedge by Black-Scholes, and derive a nonlinear pricing equation. Their models "do predict increased implied volatilities with minimal assumptions beyond those of the Black-Scholes theory", and reduce to Black-Scholes when the feedback is removed. When hedgers are a small share of the market, the correction to σ\sigmaσ is small, which fits a modest effect on typical days.
  • Schönbucher and Wilmott (2000) derive the modified price process for an illiquid market and simulate the replication of a put, which is portfolio insurance. The family of models they belong to gives volatility of the form σ/(1−ρλ(S)SuSS)\sigma/(1 - \rho\lambda(S) S u_{SS})σ/(1−ρλ(S)SuSS​), rising as the replicating strategy's convexity grows.

Loeper (2018), in The Annals of Applied Probability, gives the rigorous modern version for a hedger whose every trade moves the price linearly. The resulting Black-Scholes equation "is fully non-linear and singular". Loeper shows that it is nonetheless "well posed", and proves the "existence of smooth solutions for a large class of final payoffs". The qualifier "a large class" matters: the equation is still singular, and the results cover payoffs that keep the hedge clear of the singular point.

The sign rule is not a theorem of every model. Basak (1995) models portfolio insurers as optimizing investors in a frictionless general equilibrium. He finds that "the market volatility and risk premium are decreased, and the asset and market price levels increased, by the presence of portfolio insurance". The rule of this chapter comes from models with price impact and limited liquidity. Take those away and the result can reverse.

A newer working paper by Sornette, Ulmann and Wehrli, posted on SSRN, adds the asymmetry the nonlinear table shows. It finds that option hedging creates short-lived directional instabilities. The effect is "strongly asymmetric, with a significantly more pronounced effect on the spot price when the option market maker is short compared to long the option". The damping side is bounded, and the amplifying side is not.

5 · Portfolio insurance and October 1987

Portfolio insurance was a strategy of the 1980s that gave a stock portfolio a floor without buying listed puts. The insurer replicated a put by trading, selling stock or index futures as prices fell and buying as they rose. That is a synthetic long put, and its hedging is a short-gamma program: it trades with every move, like a dealer short gamma. It is the historical case of the formula's amplifying side.

The Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms, known as the Brady Commission, reconstructed the market of October 14 to 20, 1987, from trade records. Its report of January 1988 put the assets under portfolio insurance at about $60 billion to $90 billion, "only 2-3% of equity assets outstanding". On October 19, as summaries of the report give it:

  • Sell programs by three portfolio insurers made up just under $2 billion of the New York Stock Exchange's sales of just under $21 billion.
  • In futures, three portfolio insurers sold the equivalent of $2.8 billion of stock, and portfolio-insurance sales totaled about $6 billion: $4 billion in futures and $2 billion in stock.
  • In the first half hour a few insurers sold futures equivalent to just under $400 million of stock, 28% of public volume. Between 11:40 AM and 2:00 PM they sold about 10,000 futures contracts.
  • Roughly 40% of futures sales by traders other than market makers came from portfolio insurers.

The Commission concluded that futures-based strategies were not the "sole cause" of the fall but "a significant factor in accelerating and exacerbating the declines". One account of the report puts insurance selling at "roughly 15% of total stock and futures sold that day". Rubinstein (1988), a co-founder of a portfolio-insurance firm and so an interested party, estimated "perhaps 12 per cent of the dollar change in net sold positions on October 19". He judged a large causal role unlikely. He also noted that the crash undermined two preconditions of every portfolio-insurance program: low transaction costs and price continuity. Replication fails exactly when prices gap, the same weakness a short-gamma dealer has.

Worked example: A small share of assets, a large share of a day

Step 1: the stock. At $60 billion to $90 billion, insured assets were 2% to 3% of equities.

Step 2: the flow. On October 19 insurers sold about $6 billion. In the first half hour, their futures sales were 28% of public volume.

Step 3: the lesson. A flow that is small against what exists can be large against what trades in a given hour. Hedge flow per 1% move, the unit of GEX, is a flow, and it has to be set against the market's depth at that moment, which is what κ\kappaκ does.

Why so large a fall from so small a share? Gennotte and Leland (1990) put the question in their abstract: "traditional models cannot explain how a relatively small amount of selling could cause so large a price drop". In their model some traders are informed, others infer information from prices, and hedging demand is not fully observed. Markets are then "much less liquid in their model than in traditional models". In the authors' words, "Discontinuities (or 'crashes') can occur even with relatively little hedging". The crash needs the hedging to be unobserved or underestimated. If hedging demand is known, its price impact is much smaller.

Two papers of the same period make the information point from other angles. Grossman (1988) argues that without a traded put there is "less information about the future price volatility associated with current dynamic hedging strategies". As a result, "the stocks' future price volatility can rise because of a current lack of information about the extent to which dynamic hedging strategies are in place". Jacklin, Kleidon and Pfleiderer (1992) show that if the market underestimates dynamic hedging, prices first run above fundamentals, and "the price falls when the amount of portfolio insurance is revealed". Black (1988), in an essay on the crash, noted that futures prices went far below their theoretical values just when portfolio insurers wanted to sell.

The lesson for a GEX reader is double-edged. Listed open interest is visible, so the hedging it implies can be anticipated, which is Grossman's case for traded options. The hedging a public map cannot see, in FLEX, over-the-counter and structured-product books, is the kind that did the damage in these models (Chapter 8B).

Kyle and Obizhaeva, in a paper on large bets and stock market crashes in the Review of Finance, measured how badly a linear intuition failed in 1987. Portfolio insurers sold $10.48 billion of index futures and $3.27 billion of NYSE stocks from October 15 to 20, $13.75 billion gross. "Conventional wisdom" predicted declines of 0.51% net and 0.63% gross. Their invariance model, calibrated on over 400,000 portfolio transition orders, predicted 16.77%. The actual decline was 32%.

6 · Two tests of the sign rule

A measured market: currency options

Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette (2022), in the Journal of International Money and Finance, fit a model of this family to real positions. An earlier version was a Swiss National Bank working paper of 2019. Their model has two aggregate traders, an option market maker and an option market taker, "whose exposures reflect the total outstanding positions of all option traders in the market". The net hedge flow moves the spot rate through "a simple linear permanent impact model". Foreign exchange options trade over the counter, with no public open interest, so the authors rebuilt the market maker's gamma from trade-repository records. Their sample runs from October 21, 2017 to June 30, 2018, in EURUSD and USDJPY.

Spot volatility "is increased (decreased) by a negative (positive) gamma exposure" of the market maker. The rebuilt gamma was negative, because option takers were net long options. In size, "a negative gamma exposure of the OMM of approximately -1000 billion USD" leads "to an absolute increase in volatility of 0.7% in EURUSD and 0.9% in USDJPY". Here OMM is the option market maker, and about −1,000 billion dollars is roughly what the authors' reconstruction shows. The gamma is in the authors' own measure, whose unit the abstract does not state. The increases are absolute: 0.7 and 0.9 points of annualized volatility, under one point in the two largest currency pairs. The 2019 version explains the larger USDJPY effect by "the higher market impact of a transaction in the USDJPY spot market", EURUSD being the more liquid. Impact is the book's κ\kappaκ: the same dealer gamma does more in the thinner market.

A simulated market: liquidity and price discovery

Buis, Pieterse-Bloem, Verschoor and Zwinkels (2024), in the Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control, simulate an order book with an agent-based model. That is a simulation in which many traders follow simple rules and prices emerge from their orders. The authors add delta hedgers with a positive or negative net gamma, and compare normal and stressed conditions. Their findings, in the abstract's words:

  • "the presence of dynamic hedgers enhances market liquidity under normal conditions";
  • "positive gamma helps sustain liquidity in stressed scenarios, while negative gamma depletes it";
  • "an increase in the net gamma positioning of dynamic hedgers reduces volatility and increases market stability, whereas a negative gamma positioning increases volatility and makes the market more prone to failure";
  • "Price discovery typically worsens when dynamic hedgers become more prevalent, regardless of the sign of their positioning."

These are the two rows the regime table lacked. Negative gamma drains liquidity in stress, which is how κ\kappaκ rises in the same step as GGG. Because the two move together, the explosive corner of (8D.1) is reached faster than either alone suggests. And hedging of either sign worsens price discovery, the process by which trading moves prices to what the available information justifies. A damped, pinned market is not a better-informed one. The authors suggest steering hedgers' net gamma as a policy tool, most useful for illiquid instruments. The simulation's background traders place orders by simple random rules, a "zero-intelligence" order book, so the result is a model's, not an estimate.

A market where the dealer's gamma is measured rather than assumed points the same way. Atanasova and co-authors (2025) study the bitcoin options venue Deribit from January 2021 to December 2024. When market makers' aggregate gamma inventory is negative, they "trade in the same direction as BTC price momentum", and option spreads widen. A one-standard-deviation fall in the measure goes with effective spreads 0.12% wider for out-of-the-money calls and 0.19% for puts.

What impact research says

The idea of one number for impact per unit of flow goes back to Kyle (1985). In his model of an informed trader, the market makers' price rises in proportion to net order flow. The slope, known as Kyle's lambda, is the price move per unit of flow, and market depth is its inverse. In that model the slope is set by how much information the flow might carry. Hedge flow is mechanical, and a market that can recognize it has less reason to move far on it, so its impact tends to be smaller and to fade.

Measured impact is not linear in size. The price impact of a metaorder, a large order worked in pieces, grows roughly as the square root of its size, the law of Chapter 2D, (2D.5). In the form the research uses,

I≈Y σDQVD I \approx Y\,\sigma_D\sqrt{\frac{Q}{V_D}} I≈YσD​VD​Q​​ (8D.6)

where III is the move, QQQ the size of the order, VDV_DVD​ the daily volume and σD\sigma_DσD​ the daily volatility, with YYY a constant of order one. Sato and a co-author survey all liquid Tokyo Stock Exchange stocks over eight years. They find the exponent "equal to 1/2 within statistical errors at both the individual stock level and the individual trader level". Bucci and co-authors (2019) find "a clear crossover between a linear market impact regime and a square-root regime" in 8 million US institutional trades. Zarinelli and co-authors (2015) find that the square root fits "only across about two orders of magnitude in order size", a logarithm fitting better across five. They also find that impact relaxes on average to "approximately 2/3 of the peak impact". Tóth and co-authors (2011) trace the law to a latent order book that "vanishes around the current price".

Four more findings bracket λ\lambdaλ for the index:

  • The permanent anchor. Gabaix and Koijen (2021), in an NBER working paper, estimate that "investing $1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5", a price elasticity of demand of 0.2. It is an estimate for persistent flows over a quarter, and it is contested. At an assumed US market value of $55 trillion, $1 billion of persistent flow would add about $5 billion, roughly 1 bp or half an index point at 6,000. Hedge flow that later reverses leaves less than that behind, while its transient impact is larger.
  • Predictable flows. Brøgger (2021) finds that the daily rebalancing of leveraged volatility products is predictable in size, sign and timing. Yet "larger and more predictable flows have smaller price impact coefficients, and ... there are no excess profits from trading ahead of rebalancing flows". A flow everyone can see coming is partly absorbed in advance, which is Gennotte and Leland's point from the other side.
  • Stress. Kyle and Obizhaeva's crash comparisons show how far impact can rise. On May 6, 2010, a seller's program of 75,000 E-mini contracts, about $4.1 billion, coincided with a 5.12% drop in five minutes, from 1,113 to 1,056, against an invariance prediction of 0.61%. In 1987 the actual fall was about twice the prediction, and in 2010 about eight times. The authors read the larger-than-predicted drops as a sign that "rapid selling exacerbates transitory price impact".
  • Depth over time. A working paper by Boyarchenko, Larsen, Venter and Whelan is described in Larsen's 2024 doctoral thesis. It finds that "the depth and dealer number of the S&P 500 futures contract have decreased by 80% over a ten-year period from 2012 to 2022". It is unpublished, and the figure depends on how it defines depth. If it holds, the same GEX moves the index more than it did a decade before, so GEX numbers from different years are not comparable without a depth adjustment.

No published estimate gives the impact of dealer hedge flow in SPX in points per $1 billion. The pinning figure's range of 0 to 3 points per $1 billion spans the calm-day bracket for flows worked over a day. Stress would need values several times higher.

8 · What the formula leaves out

Each of the formula's assumptions fails in a known way, and each failure has a literature:

  1. Linear, permanent impact. Impact is concave and partly transient, as above.
  2. Continuous hedging of a known book. Almgren and Li (2016) find that an optimal hedger "trades smoothly towards the classical Black-Scholes delta, with trading intensity proportional to instantaneous mishedge and inversely proportional to illiquidity". Aubert, Chevalier and Ly Vath (2025) model a market maker whose hedging has transient as well as permanent impact, and who hedges in discrete bursts rather than continuously. Either way the flow is spread out and lumpy, not delivered at each tick.
  3. Everyone else's demand is unchanged. Other traders anticipate predictable flow and trade against it, as Brøgger's result shows, so gross mechanical flow is not net price pressure.
  4. Feedback below its critical value. Near κG=1\kappa G = 1κG=1 the smooth model gives way to gaps and crashes, as in Gennotte and Leland's model and Kyle and Obizhaeva's comparisons.

Two further omissions matter for anyone reading a map of open-interest gamma. The first is new trades. Egebjerg and Kokholm (2024), in an SSRN working paper, split the impact of market makers' hedging in two. The "gamma effect" is rebalancing an unchanged book as spot moves, and the "inventory effect" is hedging new option trades. Studying the gamma effect alone "can underestimate the total effect when the OMM's inventory adjustments reinforce the gamma impact and overestimate it when the adjustments move in the opposite direction". In SPX data, both components "are significant and with impact in line with the model predictions". A burst of customer call buying is hedged at once and moves the index even when the stock of gamma has not changed. A map of yesterday's open interest shows only the first effect.

The second omission is other hedgers. GGG in the formula is everyone who trades with the move, not only option dealers. A leveraged or inverse ETF with assets AAA and multiple LLL trades L(L−1)xAL(L-1)xAL(L−1)xA after a day's return xxx, always with the move (Chapter 8H). Tuzun (2013), in a Federal Reserve working paper, estimated for the fund assets of that time that "A 1% increase in broad stock-market indexes induces LETFs to originate rebalancing flows equivalent to $1.04 billion worth of stock". Volatility-targeting funds and trend followers also trade with moves, on longer lags. Target-date funds trade against them: Parker, Schoar and Sun (2023) find that they move retirement investors into "macrocontrarian strategies that sell stocks after relatively good stock market performance". Dealer GEX is one term in the market's net GGG, and a map of option gamma shows none of the others.

9 · How large the feedback is in practice

The dealer-feedback story is real and quantifiable, but its practical effect is modest on a typical day. Chapter 8G reviews the studies that measure it and sets them side by side in their own units. They run from Barbon and Buraschi's intraday momentum and reversal in stocks to the 0DTE studies of Dim, Eraker and Vilkov and of Adams and co-authors.

In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's reading, the episodes that everyone remembers are February 2018, March 2020, the GME squeeze and the August 5, 2024 unwind. They are the right tail of this distribution, where feedback briefly approached the unstable threshold (Chapters 8H and 8I). On most days, GEX is a probabilistic prior you can use to bias your strategy selection, not a deterministic forecast.

What to do with a regime reading is Chapter 8J's subject, and the book's two sources weigh it differently. The Dealer Mechanics Lab pairs the sign of GEX with the shape of the VIX term structure as a decision boundary for choosing strategies. The Edge Book keeps the sign as a low-confidence filter, worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight, and ignores it when it disagrees with the term structure (Chapter 10H). Chapter 8J sets out both, and turns the regime into a multiplier on size rather than a trigger for trades.

In practice

In an account, the regime changes size and stops before it changes which trades you take. A stop set at two standard deviations of a day's move sits at about 1.9% when effective volatility is 15%, because a day's standard deviation is then 15%/252=0.94%15\%/\sqrt{252} = 0.94\%15%/252​=0.94% (Chapter 5E). At 29%, the top of the explorer's default stress run, the same rule puts the stop at about 3.7%, and a stop left at 1.9% is hit by ordinary noise. Size falls for the same reason: with the stop nearly twice as far away, the same dollar risk buys about half the position.

When it fails

The regime read fails in three ways. The sign itself can be wrong, since it rests on a convention about who holds what (Chapter 8B). κ\kappaκ is not a constant: when depth vanishes, the same destabilizing gamma that meant mild amplification can approach the singular point, where a linear intuition badly understates the move (Chapter 8H). And the formula's own assumptions, linear permanent impact and continuous hedging of a known book, fail most in the episodes where it predicts the most.

Decision rule

Read the regime as a statement about volatility, and convert before you compute:

  • Convert the sign first. GGG is minus published GEX, and published GEX carries a vendor's convention (Chapter 8B). Check both before you read either.
  • Use the formula for direction and shape, not for a number. No published estimate gives κ\kappaκ for SPX hedge flow, so do not turn a GEX number into a volatility forecast.
  • Watch depth as closely as positioning. The same GGG matters more on a thin day, when κ\kappaκ is high, and thin depth and short gamma tend to arrive together.
  • Size stops from effective volatility, as in the practice callout, and cut size when dealers are short gamma and the market is thin at the same time.
  • Leave the choice of strategy to Chapter 8J, where the sign is a size multiplier, not a trigger.
Common mistake: reading the regime as a forecast

Positive GEX does not mean the market cannot move: on most days the regime is only a probabilistic prior. The episodes that did damage were the right tail, when the feedback briefly approached its unstable threshold. A sizing rule has to survive those episodes, not only the typical day.

Common mistake: mixing up G and GEX

The feedback formula uses GGG, the negation of published GEX. Put a vendor's "+$2.3B" into it unconverted, and a stabilizing regime reads as an amplifying one. Convert the sign first, and then check the vendor's convention from Chapter 8B as well. A dollar GEX also needs an impact in points per dollar before it means anything in (8D.1), by (8D.5).

Each return carries +0.45 times the one before, so a move tends to be followed by another in the same direction, which is positive autocorrelation, or momentum. Over many steps each shock's effect on the price is multiplied by 1/(1−0.45)=1.821/(1 - 0.45) = 1.821/(1−0.45)=1.82.

Question 5

Portfolio insurance covered only 2% to 3% of equity assets in 1987. How could it matter so much?

Answer

What matters is the flow against what trades at the moment, not the stock against what exists. On October 19 insurers sold about $6 billion, and in the first half hour their futures sales were 28% of public volume. Gennotte and Leland add that when the market cannot see the hedging, it is far less liquid than traditional models assume, so relatively little hedging can cause a crash.

Question 6

In Frey's model, where does the formula break, and what does the break mean?

Answer

Where ρSVSS\rho S V_{SS}ρSVSS​, the book's κG\kappa GκG, reaches 1. There the nonlinear Black-Scholes equation is singular: the hedger's own trades would move the price as much as the move they respond to, and the smooth price process no longer exists. Models that go past that point, such as Gennotte and Leland's, produce gaps and crashes rather than a very high but finite volatility.
