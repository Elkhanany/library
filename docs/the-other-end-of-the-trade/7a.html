Part VII · Theory · Chapter 7A

Why hedging works

The Black-Scholes-Merton equation in five steps

Where we are

Part VI followed a dealer through the day: inheriting a customer's Greeks, neutralizing delta, chasing gamma and deciding how often to rebalance. This chapter asks why that recipe works at all and derives the Black-Scholes-Merton equation from the dealer's hedged book in five steps. It then reads the book's profit and loss as a bet on realized against implied volatility.

What you need first
  • Log returns, the square-root-of-time rule and the variance drag μ−12σ2\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2μ−21​σ2 (Chapter 1A).
  • The normal distribution and its variance (Chapter 1B), and the random walk (Chapter 3B).
  • Delta, gamma and theta as partial derivatives, the approximation ΔV≈Δ⋅ΔS+12Γ(ΔS)2\Delta V \approx \Delta \cdot \Delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta S)^2ΔV≈Δ⋅ΔS+21​Γ(ΔS)2, and the reference call (Chapter 5C).
  • Implied and realized volatility, and the variance risk premium (Chapter 5E).
  • The delta hedge, and why gamma keeps undoing it (Chapters 6C and 6D).

Up to this point the book has described what the dealer does. This chapter answers a deeper question: why the dealer's delta-hedging strategy is the unique correct strategy, and what mathematical structure its hedging P&L actually has. It is the most technical chapter in the book and also the one that justifies every claim that follows.

If mathematics is not your native language, read slowly and accept the steps one at a time. Each formula is followed by what it says in plain words, while the heaviest algebra sits in boxes you can open or skip. The payoff for that patience is large. By the end of Part VII, one idea organizes every options strategy: every delta-hedged option position is, fundamentally, a bet on realized volatility against implied volatility, and nothing else.

In plain terms

A dealer who holds an option and the right number of shares against it has a book that does not care which way the market moves over the next instant. A book with no risk must earn what cash earns, or else someone could borrow cash, buy the book and keep the difference without risk. That one requirement, no free money, pins down the option's price. What is left of the book's profit and loss is gamma's gain from the size of each move, set against theta's charge for the passing of time.

1 · Five steps from a hedged book to an equation

The derivation runs in five steps, each building on the last and each doing one specific piece of intellectual work:

  1. A model for prices: assume the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion (GBM).
  2. Itô's lemma: derive how the option's value changes when the price moves at random.
  3. The hedged book: build the delta-hedged portfolio and see that it has no random part.
  4. No arbitrage: a portfolio with no risk over the next instant must earn the risk-free rate.
  5. The Black-Scholes-Merton equation: write down what that demands of the option's value, a consistency condition.

After the five steps comes the gamma-theta P&L decomposition that Chapter 5C previewed, which drives everything in the dealer-mechanics framework. The chapter ends by connecting option replication to variance swaps and to the way the VIX is built, the bridge to Part IX.

2 · A model for prices

Black and Scholes (1973) and, independently, Merton (1973) assumed that the underlying's price follows a geometric Brownian motion. That phrase rests on two older ideas: the random walk, and the Brownian motion it becomes when its steps grow very small.

From coin flips to Brownian motion

A random walk (Chapter 3B) is a path built from independent steps. The plainest version flips a fair coin at each step, moving up by hhh on heads and down by hhh on tails. Each step has mean zero and variance h2h^2h2. Because the steps are independent, their variances add, so after nnn steps the position has mean zero and variance nh2n h^2nh2. Its standard deviation is hnh\sqrt{n}hn​, the square-root-of-time rule of Chapter 1A.

Now let each step take Δt\Delta tΔt years, so that nnn steps cover t=n Δtt = n\,\Delta tt=nΔt years. To give the walk a volatility σ\sigmaσ, its variance after one year must be σ2\sigma^2σ2 however finely the year is sliced, which fixes the size of each step:

h=σΔt h = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} h=σΔt​ (7A.1)

With this step, the variance after ttt years is nσ2Δt=σ2tn\sigma^2\Delta t = \sigma^2 tnσ2Δt=σ2t, whatever Δt\Delta tΔt is. The step must shrink like the square root of the time it takes, not like the time itself. That one scaling rule is the source of everything unusual in this chapter.

As Δt\Delta tΔt shrinks toward zero with the right scaling, the coin-flip walk converges to a Brownian motion, written WtW_tWt​. The sum of many small independent steps is close to normal, whatever each step looks like, so the coin flips disappear into a bell curve. A Brownian motion has four defining properties:

  • It starts at zero: W0=0W_0 = 0W0​=0.
  • Its change over any interval of length Δt\Delta tΔt is normally distributed, with mean zero and variance Δt\Delta tΔt.
  • Its changes over intervals that do not overlap are independent of each other.
  • Its path is continuous, with no jumps.

The path is continuous but so jagged that it has no slope at any point: zoom in on any stretch and it looks as rough as the whole. The notation dWdWdW means its change over a very short time dtdtdt. That change is normal, with mean zero and variance dtdtdt, so its typical size is dt\sqrt{dt}dt​. For short times dt\sqrt{dt}dt​ is far larger than dtdtdt: one trading day is dt=1/252=0.0040dt = 1/252 = 0.0040dt=1/252=0.0040 of a year, but dt=0.063\sqrt{dt} = 0.063dt​=0.063, almost 16 times larger.

Adding a steady trend gives Brownian motion with drift, Xt=a t+b WtX_t = a\,t + b\,W_tXt​=at+bWt​: a line of slope aaa with noise of volatility bbb wrapped around it. Chapter 1D used it for the logarithm of an account's wealth, which drifts at the growth rate GGG with volatility σ\sigmaσ.

Geometric Brownian motion

Geometric Brownian motion applies the noise to the price in proportion to the price:

dS=μS dt+σS dW dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW dS=μSdt+σSdW (7A.2)

In words: in a small instant of time dtdtdt, the price SSS changes by a deterministic amount, μS dt\mu S\,dtμSdt, called the drift, and a random amount, σS dW\sigma S\,dWσSdW, called the diffusion. Here μ\muμ is the expected return per year and σ\sigmaσ the volatility. By construction, dWdWdW is normally distributed with mean zero and variance dtdtdt.

Multiplying the noise by σS\sigma SσS scales it by the current price and the volatility. That makes volatility a percentage per unit of time rather than a number of dollars per unit of time. It matches what you see in markets, where volatility scales with the price level: a $1,000 stock has bigger dollar moves than a $10 stock at the same volatility. Dividing both sides by SSS gives the form Chapter 1A used, dS/S=μ dt+σ dWdS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dWdS/S=μdt+σdW. The simple return over the instant is a drift plus normal noise.

This is the central modeling assumption of the entire Black-Scholes framework. Prices wiggle in proportion to themselves, which makes log returns normally distributed, or Gaussian, in continuous time. The model is wrong: real returns have fat tails, as Chapter 1B established. But it is wrong in ways that can be corrected at the margins without rebuilding the whole framework. For establishing why dealers hedge the way they do, geometric Brownian motion is fine.

One subtlety needs care: the parameter μ\muμ is the instantaneous expected return in simple-return space, not in log-return space. The expected log return is μ−12σ2\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2μ−21​σ2, the variance drag of Chapter 1A, which Step 2 derives. The two parameters live in different return conventions, both valid as long as you know which one you are using.

3 · Itô's lemma

The option's value depends on the price and on time, V(S,t)V(S, t)V(S,t), so it moves whenever SSS moves. Ordinary calculus has a rule for this, the chain rule, which would give dV=(∂V/∂S) dSdV = (\partial V/\partial S)\,dSdV=(∂V/∂S)dS for the price's part. That is wrong here, because SSS is random. The correct rule is Itô's lemma, the chain rule for Brownian motion. It rests on one surprising fact: a squared move does not vanish.

Why the square of a move does not vanish

For a smooth path, the change over a short time dtdtdt is proportional to dtdtdt, so its square is proportional to dt2dt^2dt2. Over a year cut into nnn steps, those squares add up to n×(1/n)2=1/nn \times (1/n)^2 = 1/nn×(1/n)2=1/n, which vanishes as the steps shrink. That is why ordinary calculus drops every squared change.

A Brownian motion behaves differently, because each squared step (ΔW)2(\Delta W)^2(ΔW)2 has mean Δt\Delta tΔt, the variance of the step. The squared steps over a year add up to a total whose mean is 1, a full year, however finely you slice. Its spread shrinks as the slicing gets finer, because the squared steps are independent: for normal steps, the total's standard deviation is 2/n\sqrt{2/n}2/n​.

Familiar ground

The rule that (dW)2=dt(dW)^2 = dt(dW)2=dt is the law of large numbers you rely on in every trial. One patient's response to a drug is unpredictable, while the response rate across 1,000 patients is pinned down tightly. Each squared step of a Brownian path is likewise a noisy measurement of the time it took, with a standard deviation about 1.4 times its mean. Add up thousands of them and the noise averages away, leaving the elapsed time. The relative spread falls as 2/n\sqrt{2/n}2/n​, just as a sample mean's standard error falls as 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​.

The chain rule with one more term

Chapter 5C approximated a change in value by delta times the move plus half of gamma times the move squared. Written for a function of both price and time, that second-order Taylor expansion is

dV≈∂V∂tdt+∂V∂SdS+12∂2V∂S2(dS)2 \begin{aligned} dV \approx{}& \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}dS \\ &+ \tfrac12\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}(dS)^2 \end{aligned} dV≈​∂t∂V​dt+∂S∂V​dS+21​∂S2∂2V​(dS)2​ (7A.4)

The extra term is there because (dS)2(dS)^2(dS)2 is not zero. For ordinary smooth functions, (dx)2(dx)^2(dx)2 is of order dt2dt^2dt2, which you drop in the limit. The term 12(∂2V/∂S2) σ2S2 dt\tfrac12(\partial^2V/\partial S^2)\,\sigma^2S^2\,dt21​(∂2V/∂S2)σ2S2dt is gamma showing up explicitly. This is the central technical insight of stochastic calculus. The second derivative matters when the underlying is random, and not when it is deterministic. Every "interesting" property of options derives from this single mathematical fact.

In the notation of Chapter 5C, where ∂V/∂S=Δ\partial V/\partial S = \Delta∂V/∂S=Δ, ∂2V/∂S2=Γ\partial^2V/\partial S^2 = \Gamma∂2V/∂S2=Γ and ∂V/∂t=Θ\partial V/\partial t = \Theta∂V/∂t=Θ, Itô's lemma becomes

Read it this way: over a small interval, the option's value changes by a time-decay piece, Θ dt\Theta\,dtΘdt, a directional piece, Δ dS\Delta\,dSΔdS, which moves with the underlying, and a curvature piece, 12Γσ2S2 dt\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\,dt21​Γσ2S2dt. 4 · The hedged book

Now build the dealer's book. Suppose the dealer has bought an option from a customer and hedges it by selling Δ\DeltaΔ shares of the underlying, the delta hedging of Chapter 6C. The book is worth

Π=V−ΔS \Pi = V - \Delta S Π=V−ΔS (7A.8)

where Δ=∂V/∂S\Delta = \partial V/\partial SΔ=∂V/∂S is set at the start of the instant and held fixed through it. Over the instant the short shares lose Δ dS\Delta\,dSΔdS, so the book changes by dΠ=dV−Δ dSd\Pi = dV - \Delta\,dSdΠ=dV−ΔdS. When (7A.6) is substituted for dVdVdV, its Δ dS\Delta\,dSΔdS meets the −Δ dS-\Delta\,dS−ΔdS of the shares and cancels it:

dΠ=(Θ+12Γσ2S2) dt d\Pi = (\Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2)\,dt dΠ=(Θ+21​Γσ2S2)dt (7A.9)

Look at what just happened: the dSdSdS terms canceled. The book's change has no random component, so over the instant it is risk-free. That is what delta hedging accomplishes: it removes the dWdWdW term and leaves only the deterministic dtdtdt terms.

This is the mathematical statement of what Chapter 6C said informally: a delta-hedged book is, at the instant of the hedge, directionless. The dWdWdW noise from the underlying has been hedged away. The remaining motion of the book is entirely deterministic, driven by theta and gamma alone, at first order.

Two remarks keep this honest. The first is that "at the instant" matters: delta changes as soon as SSS moves, so the hedge must be reset continually, which is gamma's chase in Chapter 6D. The second is that a dealer who sold the option holds −Π-\Pi−Π, long Δ\DeltaΔ shares against a short option. Every sign flips, while the argument that follows is unchanged.

5 · No free money

A book that is risk-free over the next instant must earn the risk-free rate rrr, otherwise there is free money. This is the no-arbitrage condition, the most consequential single principle in derivatives pricing. If the hedged book earned more than rrr, a trader could borrow at rrr, buy the book and keep the difference with no risk, an arbitrage. If the book earned less, the trader would short it and lend the proceeds at rrr. Either trade pushes prices until the gap closes, so

dΠ=r Π dt d\Pi = r\,\Pi\,dt dΠ=rΠdt (7A.10)

The book earns the risk-free rate on its current value. Combining this with (7A.9), and writing Π=V−ΔS\Pi = V - \Delta SΠ=V−ΔS:

(Θ+12Γσ2S2) dt=r(V−ΔS) dt (\Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2)\,dt = r(V - \Delta S)\,dt (Θ+21​Γσ2S2)dt=r(V−ΔS)dt (7A.11)

Cancel dtdtdt from both sides and rearrange:

Θ+12Γσ2S2=rV−rΔS \Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2 = rV - r\Delta S Θ+21​Γσ2S2=rV−rΔS (7A.12)

6 · The Black-Scholes-Merton equation

Write the Greeks back out as derivatives and move rΔSr\Delta SrΔS to the left:

∂V∂t+rS∂V∂S+12σ2S2∂2V∂S2=rV \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \\ &\quad + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = rV \end{aligned} ​∂t∂V​+rS∂S∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​=rV​ (7A.13)

This is the celebrated Black-Scholes-Merton partial differential equation (PDE). A partial differential equation is a relation among a function's partial derivatives. Its solution is not a number but a whole function, here the option's value at every price and time, fixed once you say what it must equal at expiry. Robert Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this work, jointly with Myron Scholes. Fischer Black, who would have shared the prize, had died in 1995.

A consistency condition, not a formula

The Black-Scholes-Merton PDE is not a pricing formula but a consistency condition: any option price function V(S,t)V(S, t)V(S,t) that does not satisfy it creates an arbitrage. Solve it with the condition that at expiry, with no time left, a call is worth max⁡(S−K,0)\max(S - K, 0)max(S−K,0). The solution is the Black-Scholes call formula of Chapter 5C, with d1d_1d1​ and d2d_2d2​ as defined there:

C=Se−qTN(d1)−Ke−rTN(d2) C = S e^{-qT}N(d_1) - K e^{-rT}N(d_2) C=Se−qTN(d1​)−Ke−rTN(d2​) (7A.14)

More profoundly, the PDE encodes the recipe by which a dealer can replicate the option using only the underlying and cash. The dealer is not "pricing the option" in some metaphysical sense but manufacturing it through dynamic hedging. The shares and cash, adjusted as the price moves, form a replicating portfolio that ends with exactly the option's payoff.

This is the deepest insight in the entire framework. An option's payoff looks as if it needs unusual cleverness to construct, because it is asymmetric and convex. In fact it can be built from a simple time-varying position in the underlying plus cash. The cost of building it is exactly the Black-Scholes price, no more and no less, under the model's assumptions. Any deviation from that price creates an arbitrage.

The practical implication is that when you buy an option, you are buying the right to a payoff that the dealer constructs for you through their hedging. The premium you pay is the cost of the construction kit, plus the dealer's margin, plus compensation for residual risks the model does not capture: jump risk, transaction costs and fat tails. The variance risk premium of Chapter 5E is the cumulative spread between what dealers charge and what the model says is fair. It is the structural compensation dealers require to be in this business.

Where the expected return went

Look again at (7A.13): the expected return μ\muμ appears nowhere in it. It entered through the price model, (7A.2), and reached Itô's lemma through dSdSdS. It disappeared in Step 3, when the hedge canceled the dSdSdS terms, the only route by which the drift could reach the book. So the option's price does not depend on how fast anyone expects the underlying to grow.

That settles the question Chapter 4B left open: which probabilities turn an expected payoff into a price. Because μ\muμ does not matter, you may price as if the underlying grew at the risk-free rate, then discount the expected payoff at that rate. Probabilities chosen that way are the risk-neutral probabilities of Chapter 5C.

Worked example: One step of replication

Step 1: the setup. Take Chapter 4B's toy, with r=0r = 0r=0 to keep the arithmetic clean: an underlying at 100 ends at 110 or 90, where a call struck at 100 pays 10 or 0.

Step 2: build the replica. Hold aaa shares and bbb dollars of cash, and require the replica to pay what the call pays in both outcomes: 110a+b=10110a + b = 10110a+b=10 and 90a+b=090a + b = 090a+b=0. Subtracting gives 20a=1020a = 1020a=10, so a=0.5a = 0.5a=0.5 and b=−45b = -45b=−45, a loan of $45. The share count aaa is the call's delta over the step.

Step 3: its cost. The replica costs 0.5×100−45=50.5 \times 100 - 45 = 50.5×100−45=5 today, so the call must also cost 5, or buying one and selling the other is an arbitrage.

Step 4: the probabilities do not matter. With a 50% chance of the rise, the expected payoff is 5, the price. With a 70% chance, the expected payoff is 7. The replica still costs 5, so the price is still 5. The probability that makes the expected payoff equal the price is the one under which the underlying's expected value is today's price, which here is 50%: the risk-neutral probability.

7 · The hedging P&L: gamma against theta

Now the PDE gives the most important formula in this book. Suppose the dealer delta-hedges continuously while the realized volatility σR\sigma_RσR​ differs from the volatility used to price the option, the implied volatility at entry σI\sigma_IσI​. Over each instant, the book's gain above what the same money would earn as cash is approximately

where Γ\GammaΓ is computed at the implied volatility.

Where the hedging P&L comes from

Step 1: expand using the actual move. The dealer values and hedges the option at σI\sigma_IσI​, so Θ\ThetaΘ, Δ\DeltaΔ and Γ\GammaΓ are the Black-Scholes values at σI\sigma_IσI​. The only change from Step 3 of the derivation is that the squared move is now the realized one, not σI2S2 dt\sigma_I^2S^2\,dtσI2​S2dt.

Read (7A.15) this way: over each interval, the delta-hedged dealer earns gamma times the difference between the realized squared return and the implied variance. This is the entire economic content of a delta-hedged option position. If the squared returns average the implied variance, the bracket averages zero and the book earns nothing beyond cash. If they do not, the book earns or pays the difference, scaled by 12ΓS2\tfrac12\Gamma S^221​ΓS2, with these signs:

  • If you are long the option (long gamma) and realized volatility exceeds implied, you make money. Every wiggle in spot lets you scalp gamma, buying low and selling high in your hedge, at a rate that in aggregate exceeds the theta you are paying.
  • If you are long the option and realized falls short of implied, you lose, because you bought too much volatility.
  • If you are short the option (short gamma, the dealer's typical position), the signs flip. You earn the implied volatility, as the theta you collect, and pay the realized volatility, as the rebalancing losses.

A short-gamma dealer wants quiet markets, while a long-gamma dealer wants moves.

Key idea

Under the standard delta-hedged framework, an option position is, at first order, a bet on realized against implied volatility. It is the dominant P&L driver in the absence of large vega or skew dynamics.

This is the punchline of the gamma-theta decomposition. Three choices set up the bet: strike selection affects the size of gamma, expiration selection affects its speed, and the implied volatility at entry sets the implied side. To first order, the resulting P&L is a function of whether realized exceeded implied.

The honest caveats

The "and nothing else" framing skips several real-world second-order effects that matter in practice:

  • Vega risk on IV moves: a delta-hedged position is not vega-neutral. If implied volatility changes between entry and exit, that mark-to-market vega P&L is a separate component on top of the gamma-against-realized term. For long-dated options with large vega, it can dominate.
  • Discrete hedging: the decomposition assumes continuous rebalancing, while real hedging happens in steps. The path of spot between rebalances adds variance around the theoretical gamma-scalping P&L. Two traders running the same position with different hedge frequencies realize different P&L from the same gap between realized and implied.
  • Skew and surface dynamics: options that sit on a steep part of the volatility surface accrue P&L from vanna and volga as spot and volatility move together. Those terms are not captured by the simple Γ×(σR2−σI2)\Gamma \times (\sigma_R^2 - \sigma_I^2)Γ×(σR2​−σI2​) decomposition, and Chapter 7B works them through.
  • Transaction costs: hedging is not free, so the gamma-scalping P&L is gross. Net P&L can be meaningfully smaller, especially in less liquid underlyings or on short-DTE options, where rebalancing is frequent.

For most retail-scale, short-to-medium-DTE, large-cap delta-hedged positions, the first-order framing of realized against implied captures the bulk of P&L variability. For longer-dated positions, exotic surface trades or low-liquidity underlyings, the second-order terms matter and should be modeled explicitly.

Hedging in steps, and paying for each step

The second and fourth caveats pull against each other, as Chapter 6E showed. Hedging more often tames the path's noise but pays the spread more often. The next figure puts both into one book. No schedule wins on both counts: the spread falls as the interval shrinks while the cost rises. That is the trade-off of Chapter 6E, which finds where the net result is best for an SPX book.

8 · Why the dealer feedback loop is built into the math

This is also the punchline for why aggregate dealer positioning moves the market. Suppose dealers are collectively short gamma. By (7A.15) they are mechanically losing money to volatility. The more volatility there is, the more they lose. To minimize losses they hedge aggressively, which means buying as the market rises and selling as it falls. That hedging creates volatility, which makes them lose more, which makes them hedge more.

The feedback loop is mathematically built into the gamma-theta decomposition just derived. It is not a story or a metaphor: it falls out of the PDE. Chapter 8D formalizes it into the effective-volatility equation, which quantifies how dealer feedback amplifies or dampens the underlying's volatility depending on the sign of aggregate dealer gamma.

9 · The variance swap connection

Add up (7A.15) over the life of the option. Each instant's gain is weighted by 12ΓS2\tfrac12\Gamma S^221​ΓS2, which changes as spot and time move. Now suppose you held a position whose gamma falls like 1/S21/S^21/S2 as spot rises, so that ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2 stays at a constant, ggg. The weight then comes out of the sum. Write PPP for the book's total gain above cash over a life of TTT years:

P≈g2[∑(dSS)2−σI2T]=g2 T (σR2−σI2) \begin{aligned} P &\approx \tfrac{g}{2}\Big[\sum\Big(\frac{dS}{S}\Big)^2 - \sigma_I^2 T\Big] \\ &= \tfrac{g}{2}\,T\,(\sigma_R^2 - \sigma_I^2) \end{aligned} P​≈2g​[∑(SdS​)2−σI2​T]=2g​T(σR2​−σI2​)​ (7A.17)

Here σR2=1T∑(dS/S)2\sigma_R^2 = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum (dS/S)^2σR2​=T1​∑(dS/S)2 is the realized variance, annualized. A payoff proportional to realized variance less a fixed number is exactly a variance swap (Chapter 4B). So integrating the hedging P&L over the life of the option and weighting by 1/S21/S^21/S2 gives, almost exactly, the payoff of a variance swap. This is not coincidence but the foundation of model-free variance replication, and the reason the VIX (Chapter 9A) is constructed as a weighted strip of options with 1/K21/K^21/K2 weights.

Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou's 1999 Goldman Sachs paper, "More Than You Ever Wanted to Know About Volatility Swaps", showed how. A portfolio of options weighted by ΔK/K2\Delta K/K^2ΔK/K2 across all strikes, where ΔK\Delta KΔK is the spacing between strikes, replicates the payoff of a variance swap when it is dynamically delta-hedged. The intuition is that weighting by 1/K21/K^21/K2 creates a "gamma flat" portfolio. Its ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2, which turns a squared return into dollars and is 100 times the per-share dollar gamma of Chapter 6D, is the same wherever spot is. That is exactly the property required to replicate realized variance.

Worked example: A strip with flat dollar gamma

Take options 30 days from expiry at 20% implied volatility and a zero rate, one at every strike from 40 to 300, $1 apart.

Step 1: a single strike at 100. An option struck at 100 has ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2 of 695 with spot at 100, but only 122 at spot 90 and 0.32 at spot 80. Its sensitivity to squared returns lives near its strike.

Step 2: equal weights at every strike. One option at every strike gives ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2 of 6,421 at spot 80, 10,033 at 100 and 14,447 at 120. The sensitivity is spread out now, but it grows with spot.

Step 3: weights of 1/K². Holding 1/K21/K^21/K2 of each option gives ΓS2=1.000\Gamma S^2 = 1.000ΓS2=1.000 at spot 80, at 100 and at 120. Every squared return then counts the same wherever spot has wandered, which is what a variance swap pays for. The flatness is no accident of the numbers. Apart from a straight line, the strip pays minus the logarithm of the final price: a short position in the log contract of Chapter 9A. Minus the logarithm has a gamma of exactly 1/S21/S^21/S2, so ΓS2=1\Gamma S^2 = 1ΓS2=1 at every spot.

This is the mathematical thread connecting dealer hedging to the VIX. The VIX is, fundamentally, the cost of replicating variance through delta-hedged options. Every option you trade contributes some amount of replicated variance to the dealer's book. Aggregate dealer gamma is, essentially, the aggregate variance-replication exposure of the entire options ecosystem. Chapter 9A returns to this connection in detail.

In practice
  • An unhedged option is not this bet: a short put you hold without a hedge makes or loses money mostly on direction, through delta. The formula (7A.15) describes only the part of its P&L that a hedge would leave.
  • Know the breakeven move before you trade: it is σI/252\sigma_I/\sqrt{252}σI​/252​ a day, 1.26% at 20% implied volatility. A seller of hedged options profits when the squared daily moves average less than its square, and a buyer when they average more.
  • Count the rebalancing costs: a retail account that hedges a short straddle with MES futures (Chapter 2A) pays the spread at every adjustment. Hedging less often saves costs but widens the spread of outcomes, as the friction figure showed.
  • Know what a bought option costs you: its premium pays for the dealer's construction, their margin and the residual risks they carry. The variance risk premium (Chapter 10C) is what that costs a buyer on average.
When it fails

The derivation assumes geometric Brownian motion with one constant volatility, continuous hedging at no cost, and a price that never jumps. When the price gaps, a hedge set before the gap is wrong after it. No rebalancing can follow a move that happens all at once. The dealer then carries the jump risk that the premium was meant to cover. Implied volatility also moves, which is the vega caveat. The surface moves with spot too, which Chapter 7B takes up. Finally, the no-arbitrage step needs someone able to borrow, lend and trade at the model's prices. When capital is scarce, the limits to arbitrage of Chapter 1G can hold prices away from the model's for some time.

Decision rule
  • Treat any option you hedge as a volatility trade: take it only when your forecast of realized volatility over its life differs from the implied volatility by more than your hedging costs. Go long gamma if you expect more realized volatility, short if less.
  • Size a short-gamma book for the large days, not the average day: by (7A.15), a day at twice the breakeven move costs three days of the implied variance the book collects. A day at three times the breakeven costs eight.
  • Choose a hedging interval deliberately: hedging less often widens the spread of outcomes roughly with the square root of the interval. Hedging more often raises costs roughly with one over that square root (Chapter 6E).
Common mistake: "delta-neutral means no risk"

A delta-hedged book has no risk from direction only for an instant. Over a day its P&L is (7A.15), which can be large. A short-gamma book loses eight days' worth of collected implied variance on a single day that moves three times the breakeven. Delta-neutral means the bet has changed from direction to volatility, not that the bet has gone.

Common mistake: reading the PDE as a forecast

The Black-Scholes-Merton equation says nothing about where the price goes, because the expected return has dropped out of it. Risk-neutral probabilities are pricing weights, not forecasts. The N(d2)N(d_2)N(d2​) of Chapter 5C is the chance of a call finishing in the money in a world where the underlying grows at the risk-free rate, not in the real one.

What to carry forward
  • A Brownian motion's change over a time dtdtdt is normal with variance dtdtdt, so its squared changes add up to the elapsed time. That is the rule (dW)2=dt(dW)^2 = dt(dW)2=dt, while (dt)2(dt)^2(dt)2 vanishes.
  • Itô's lemma is the chain rule plus one term, 12Γσ2S2 dt\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\,dt21​Γσ2S2dt: gamma earning from the size of moves.
  • A hedge of Δ\DeltaΔ shares cancels the random part of the book. No arbitrage then makes the book earn the risk-free rate. Together they give the Black-Scholes-Merton equation, a consistency condition whose solution is the Black-Scholes formula and in which μ\muμ does not appear.
  • A continuously hedged book earns 12ΓS2[(dS/S)2−σI2 dt]\tfrac12\Gamma S^2[(dS/S)^2 - \sigma_I^2\,dt]21​ΓS2[(dS/S)2−σI2​dt] above cash: at first order a bet on realized against implied, with vega, discrete hedging, surface dynamics and costs as the caveats.
  • Weighted by 1/K21/K^21/K2, a strip of hedged options has a flat ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2 and replicates a variance swap, the construction behind the VIX.
