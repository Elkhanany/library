Part VIII · The whole market · Chapter 8A

Gamma exposure

From one book to the whole market

Where we are

Part VI followed one dealer through one trade: the delta hedge of Chapter 6C and the gamma of Chapter 6D that keeps undoing it. This chapter adds up every dealer's gamma, across every strike and expiry, into one number called gamma exposure. It also shows that the number's sign rests on an assumption about who holds what, which no public data settle.

What you need first
  • Gamma: the change in delta for a $1 move in the underlying, largest at the money and near expiry (Chapter 5C).
  • Delta hedging: the dealer trades the underlying to hold the book's delta at zero. A long-gamma book hedges against each move and a short-gamma book hedges with it (Chapters 6C and 6D).
  • Dollar gamma per 1% move, about $46,000 for one at-the-money SPX contract at spot 6,000 (Chapter 6D).
  • Open interest and the contract multiplier (Chapters 5A and 5B).

The real market has thousands of strikes, dozens of expirations, and hundreds of trillions of dollars of open interest. Adding up the dollar gamma of every dealer position, strike by strike and expiration by expiration, gives a single number. It is the gamma exposure index, or GEX, which tries to capture the total mechanical flow that dealers' hedging generates for each 1% move in the market.

This part of the book builds that number and then tests it. This chapter constructs GEX and its sign. Chapter 8B describes the two regimes the sign separates, positive and negative dealer gamma, and the effect sizes documented in the academic literature. Chapter 8C studies the cases where the framework breaks, Volmageddon in 2018 and the March 2020 Great Liquidity Crisis among them. Chapter 8D turns those cases into a checklist for gamma squeezes. The numbers this part cites come from academic research, peer-reviewed papers and working papers, or directly from exchange data, not from social-media commentary.

In plain terms

Each dealer's hedge is small and mechanical. When the market moves 1%, the dealer's delta changes by its dollar gamma, and the dealer trades that amount back to stay flat. GEX adds all those trades together, each with a sign for its direction. A positive total means dealers as a group sell into rallies and buy into falls, which damps moves. A negative total means they buy rallies and sell falls, which pushes moves further. The catch is the sign: nobody publishes which side of each option the dealers hold, so every GEX number assumes it.

1 · One line of the chain, in dollars

Chapter 6D measured a position's gamma in the unit dealers use: dollars of hedging per 1% move. Call one strike, one expiry and one type (call or put) a line of the option chain, and label the lines i=1,2,3,…i = 1, 2, 3, \dotsi=1,2,3,…. Each line contributes

Why the formula has S squared

Step 1: the move. A 1% move in the underlying is ΔS=0.01 S\Delta S = 0.01\,SΔS=0.01S.

Step 2: one option's delta. Gamma is the change in delta per $1 of spot, so each option's delta changes by Γi×0.01 S\Gamma_i \times 0.01\,SΓi​×0.01S units of the underlying.

Step 3: the whole line. Each contract covers MMM units and the line has NiN_iNi​ contracts, so the line's delta changes by 0.01 ΓiSMNi0.01\,\Gamma_i S M N_i0.01Γi​SMNi​ units.

Step 4: into dollars. Each unit is worth SSS dollars, so that change is worth 0.01 ΓiS2MNi0.01\,\Gamma_i S^2 M N_i0.01Γi​S2MNi​ dollars. One SSS comes from the size of the move and the other from the price of what is traded.

Step 5: the dealer's side. A dealer who is long the line gains that delta on a rise, and a dealer who is short it loses it. That difference is the sign sis_isi​. To stay flat, the dealer trades the opposite amount in the underlying.

2 · Adding up the market

Summing (8A.1) over every line of every expiry gives the market's total:

GEX=∑iΓ$,i \text{GEX} = \sum_i \Gamma_{\$,i} GEX=i∑​Γ$,i​ (8A.2)

The Dealer Mechanics Lab writes this total as G$G_\$G$​, while this book calls it GEX and keeps the letter GGG for the "destabilizing gamma" of Chapter 8B, which has the opposite sign.

When spot moves by a fraction ΔS/S\Delta S/SΔS/S, the dealers' combined hedge trade, in dollars, is approximately

ΔH≈− GEX×ΔS/S0.01 \Delta H \approx -\,\text{GEX} \times \frac{\Delta S / S}{0.01} ΔH≈−GEX×0.01ΔS/S​ (8A.3)

The fraction divided by 0.01 is the move in percent: a 1% move multiplies GEX by 1, and a 0.5% move by 0.5. A positive ΔH\Delta HΔH is buying and a negative one is selling.

  • GEX > 0: the hedge trades against the move (contra-trend), which is stabilizing.
  • GEX < 0: the hedge trades with the move (pro-trend), which is destabilizing.

3 · Who holds what: the sign

The sign of each line follows from a chain of four links. The customer's position fixes the dealer's, because the dealer holds the other side (Chapter 6B). The dealer's position fixes the sign of its gamma, because a long option has positive gamma and a short one negative. The gamma sign fixes the hedge: long gamma trades against the move, short gamma with it. The hedge fixes the effect on the market.

The first figure walks this chain one case at a time. Choose what the customer holds (long or short), the option (call or put), and which way spot moves (up or down). The first table follows the chain for your choice, and the second lists all four customer positions with yours marked. Start with a customer who is long a call and spot moving up, then switch the customer to short, then try a put.

A customer who buys a call leaves the dealer short a call and short gamma. On a rally the dealer buys stock, pushing the move further: destabilizing. A customer who sells a put leaves the dealer long a put and long gamma. On a fall the dealer buys stock, cushioning the fall: stabilizing. The option type turns out not to matter; only the side the dealer holds does. Customers who buy options, calls or puts, make hedging destabilizing, and customers who sell options make it stabilizing.

Two conventions

Public data show open interest, the number of contracts outstanding on each line, without showing who holds them. Every GEX model therefore fills in sis_isi​ with a rule, a sign convention, and two are in wide use:

Conventionsis_isi​ for callssis_isi​ for putsAssumed positioning
JPM-era (pre-2022)+1−1Dealers long calls, bought from asset managers who sell them; short puts, sold to hedgers who buy them
0DTE-era (post-2022)−1+1Dealers short calls, sold to customers who buy them; long puts, bought from retail premium sellers who write them

Both rows are assumptions about who holds what, not observations. The 0DTE-era row needs customers to be net sellers of puts, as in cash-secured puts and put credit spreads. At the same time it needs them to be net buyers of calls. A customer who sells a call, in a call credit spread or an iron condor, leaves the dealer long that call, which pushes the call sign back toward +1. Which of those flows dominates is exactly what the convention assumes.

Familiar ground

A sign convention is an imputation. In a trial with missing outcomes, the result depends on what you assume about the patients you cannot see. A best-case and a worst-case imputation can point in opposite directions. Open interest is the observed data, and who holds each side is the missing variable. The honest report is a sensitivity analysis: the answer under each assumption, and a statement of which assumption you believe and why.

4 · A stylized sum

Chapter 6D's figure of about $46,000 per contract describes one contract. The claim that aggregate dealer GEX lives in the range of $20 billion to $100 billion per 1% move describes the sum. Walking one stylized sum end to end shows that the two orders of magnitude agree.

Worked example: Six lines of an SPX chain

Step 1: the setup. With SPX at 6,000, gamma follows a stylized profile that peaks at the money and decays symmetrically: about 0.0013 at the money, 0.0010 at 100 points away and 0.0006 at 200 points away. Open interest follows a typical put-heavy SPX profile. More put than call open interest is the empirical norm, because SPX skew is steep (Chapters 5E and 5G). The at-the-money strike is split 70/30, puts over calls, to reflect that skew.

Against that order of magnitude, the real listed SPX book has hundreds of strikes across several expirations. Its dollar gamma is also concentrated more sharply at short-dated at-the-money strikes than this five-strike snapshot shows. Scaling the toy's total by a factor of 3 to 15, which the ratio of a real strip to the toy supports, lands in the range of $20 billion to $100 billion. The per-contract claim and the aggregate claim are the same claim, summed at different grains.

The choice of sign convention is model risk, the risk of being wrong because a model's assumption is wrong. The same open interest produces opposite GEX signs depending on the assumption. No convention is universally correct; the right one depends on which side of each option type dealers actually hold in aggregate.

For SPX in the 0DTE era, the empirical evidence of Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024) and of Adams and co-authors (2025), both described below, suggests dealers are net long gamma on most days. That is consistent with the 0DTE-era convention, which the toy shows giving a positive total on a put-heavy book. For SPY and individual stocks, the appropriate convention may be regime-dependent. When you read a vendor's GEX number, check which convention it uses before you draw conclusions.

5 · The vendor formula and where its sign came from

The standard vendor formula, published by SqueezeMetrics in 2017, was adopted in some form by every later vendor, including SpotGamma, Tier1Alpha and Cboe's own derivative dashboards:

GEX=∑iΓi OIi×100×S2×0.01×signi \begin{aligned} \text{GEX} &= \textstyle\sum_i \Gamma_i\,\text{OI}_i \times 100 \\ &\quad \times S^2 \times 0.01 \times \text{sign}_i \end{aligned} GEX​=∑i​Γi​OIi​×100×S2×0.01×signi​​ (8A.4)

Here signi=+1\text{sign}_i = +1signi​=+1 for calls and −1 for puts, which makes it (8A.2) written out, with OIi=Ni\text{OI}_i = N_iOIi​=Ni​, M=100M = 100M=100, and the JPM-era convention built in. Each term is the dollar gamma per 1% move of the open interest on one line. The sign is the whole game, because it encodes the assumption about which side of each option type the dealer typically holds.

The JPM era

SqueezeMetrics published its method in 2017 on the assumption that dealers are net long calls and net short puts. The reasoning came from the dominant flow at the time. Institutional asset managers were the main sellers of calls, through covered calls and the famous "JPM collar" rolled by JPMorgan's Hedged Equity Fund. A collar holds stock with a bought put below the price and a sold call above it, so the fund sold calls and bought puts. Institutional hedgers such as pension funds and endowments were the main buyers of puts. Dealers stood on the other side of both: long the calls they bought from yield-hunting asset managers, and short the puts they sold to hedgers.

Under that assumption, a rise in SPX makes the dealers' long calls gain delta, so dealers sell stock to stay flat, which damps the rally. A fall makes the puts' deltas more negative, which leaves the dealers who are short them longer in delta, so dealers sell stock again, which amplifies the fall. That produced the familiar pattern of slow grinds up and fast crashes down. The Dealer Mechanics Lab attributes the asymmetric character of equity volatility cycles directly to this asymmetric dealer positioning.

The 0DTE era

That was 2017, and the 0DTE era flipped the dominant flow. In 0DTE options, retail and small institutional accounts overwhelmingly sell premium, through credit spreads, iron condors and cash-secured puts, with the dealers buying from them. The new net positioning is plausibly the opposite of the JPM-era assumption. Dealers are net long puts, bought from customers who write them, and net short calls, sold to customers who buy them. Premium selling explains the puts. The calls rest on customers buying more calls than they sell, which is part of what the convention assumes.

The reversal is measured, not speculated. Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024), in "0DTEs: Trading, Gamma Risk and Volatility Propagation" (SSRN 4692190), a working paper from George Washington University, found that net market-maker gamma in 0DTE options is positive on average. The dealer is mostly long gamma in 0DTE, the opposite sign of the original SqueezeMetrics convention.

Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025) extended the work in "Do S&P 500 Options Increase Market Volatility? Evidence from 0DTEs" (SSRN 5641974, posted December 3, 2025). They found that 0DTE options dampen market volatility through dealer hedging. Their answer to the question in their title begins "On the contrary", and continues:

... the presence of 0DTEs dampens market volatility through a shift in market makers' hedging needs. Importantly, this shift occurs not due to trading 0DTEs on their expiration day but due to positions in longer-dated options accumulated earlier and becoming 0DTEs. Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025)

This is not a minor calibration issue but the dominant source of model risk in every public GEX product. Receive a claim that "GEX flipped negative today" with skepticism, unless you know which convention the publisher uses and whether it matches the actual flow in the product you trade.

6 · The gamma flip, and the walls

The level of spot at which net dealer gamma crosses zero is the gamma flip, also called the volatility trigger, which is SpotGamma's proprietary name for it. On one side of it dealer hedging amplifies moves, because dealers are short gamma in aggregate. On the other side it damps them, because they are long gamma. With the JPM-era convention and a book like the figure's, puts below spot and calls above, the amplifying side is below the flip and the damping side above it. The 0DTE-era convention swaps the two sides.

The flip moves as spot moves, because each strike's gamma depends on how far spot is from it. The flip is not a hard mechanical barrier; it is a regime boundary. Crossing it slowly during a calm session is uneventful. Crossing it during stress, when dealers themselves are under-positioned to absorb flow, is where the destabilization shows up, as the case studies of Chapter 8C show.

Vendors also mark the strikes where dealer gamma is most concentrated. The call wall is the strike with the largest call gamma, and the put wall the strike with the largest put gamma. The Dealer Mechanics Lab reads a call wall above spot as potential resistance and a put wall below spot as potential support. The market dashboard of Chapter 11A uses them that way.

The Edge Book disagrees about trading these levels: it rejects scalping around them, selling at the call wall and buying at the put wall, for retail accounts (Chapters 1G and 10I). It gives three reasons. Vendor models disagree, because each assumes different positioning. The rise of 0DTE options since 2022 changed who holds what. And a Cboe study from 2025 estimated 0DTE market-maker gamma hedging at only about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity. The Edge Book keeps only the sign of GEX, as low-confidence regime context for sizing other trades (Chapter 10H). Both sources agree that the sign is regime context, not a trade by itself.

7 · Five limits of the framework

The model has serious limits, and you should know them before you read any GEX chart with conviction.

  1. The counterparty assumption: as the toggle made plain, the sign is fragile. The standard SqueezeMetrics convention assumes dealers are long calls and short puts, while the 0DTE evidence above suggests the opposite. Different vendors make different assumptions, and their published numbers diverge accordingly. The right convention depends on which counterparty actually holds each option in aggregate, which is hard to observe.
  2. Open interest publishes with a lag: the OCC, the Options Clearing Corporation, publishes open interest T+1, that is, on the next business day. Intraday GEX is therefore imputed by inference from the trade tape rather than directly observed. Vendors do this with varying sophistication, and the imputation errors can be substantial intraday, especially in high-volume sessions.
  3. FLEX and OTC books are invisible: as Chapters 2B and 4A discuss, FLEX options reach public data feeds only in aggregate. Bank-issued structured products are completely invisible to public GEX models: autocallables, principal-protected notes and defined-outcome ETF inventories, measured globally in hundreds of billions of notional. The hedging flows from these books can dwarf the listed gamma during stress.
  4. Black-Scholes gamma is wrong under skew: GEX uses Black-Scholes gamma, which assumes a flat volatility surface, with no skew. Real put-wing gammas under steep skew can be several times the Black-Scholes number. The error does not reverse: it consistently understates put-side gamma in equity markets.
  5. GEX is positioning, not outcome: it tells you what dealers would do given a move; it does not tell you whether a move happens. The empirical effect sizes in the academic literature are real but modest. Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (2005) find pinning effects of about 16.5 basis points on expiry days. Pinning is the pull of a heavily traded strike on the closing price at expiry (Chapter 5F). Barbon and Buraschi (2021) find gamma imbalance effects of around 16 bps per standard deviation.

For scale, 16.5 basis points of SPX at 6,000 is 6,000×0.00165≈106{,}000 \times 0.00165 \approx 106,000×0.00165≈10 index points. That is a real effect, and a small one beside a typical day's range.

The invisible book

Of all the limits, the one that bites hardest in practice is the invisible book. Every public GEX model sees only listed open interest: contracts that trade on Cboe, cleared through the OCC, with strikes on a fixed grid. That dataset is comprehensive, but it captures only one side of the market.

FLEX options sit alongside the listed market, trading on Cboe but with a customizable strike, expiration, exercise style and settlement. Cboe reports FLEX volume averaging 1.3 to 1.4 million contracts a day in 2025, more than ten times the 2019 level. Total FLEX open interest is 35 million contracts, up from 2 million in 2019, a 17.5× increase.

The bigger invisible layer is bank-issued structured products: autocallables, principal-protected notes and range accruals, which retail and private-wealth channels sell to clients around the world. An autocallable, for example, pays a coupon and can be redeemed early when the index is above a set level. It exposes its holder to losses if the index falls through a lower barrier. The issuing bank's hedge replicates the structure's option exposure dynamically. Globally, the autocallable book alone is measured in hundreds of billions of notional, dominated by retail flow in Europe and Asia.

The invisible book matters most precisely when GEX matters most. On a quiet day, FLEX and OTC hedging is small and self-cancelling. On a stress day it can dominate everything the listed GEX number shows. That happens when a barrier is approached, when an autocallable book nears a knock-in level, or when an institutional buyer suddenly rolls a multi-billion-dollar collar.

Volmageddon's VIX call wing was in FLEX. The March 2020 vanna cascade ran through structured-product hedges as much as through listed puts (Chapter 8C). The next stress event is almost certain to feature a flow that surprises every published GEX model.

Key idea

Published GEX is a useful prior on typical days. On atypical days, treat it as one of several inputs, not a forecast.

8 · Demand-based option pricing

A foundational paper sits under all of this: Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009), "Demand-Based Option Pricing," Review of Financial Studies, Vol. 22, No. 10, pages 4259 to 4299. They showed that net end-user demand for index options is the primary driver of the deviation of option prices from no-arbitrage benchmarks. The deeper the dealers' positioning imbalance, the larger the deviation, and the larger the hedging activity needed to manage it.

The Garleanu-Pedersen-Poteshman framework establishes that option prices are not pure forecasts of future variance plus a risk-free rate. They also depend on who is buying and who is selling, beyond the model fundamentals. The Dealer Mechanics Lab takes this as the structural justification for the variance risk premium (Chapter 10C) and as the academic foundation for the GEX framework as a whole.

The Edge Book reads the same paper more narrowly (Chapter 10I). In its reading, demand pressure affects option prices at the level of option pricing, not the price of the underlying, and its effect per unit of demand is small. Nor does the paper validate specific level-trading rules.

In practice

Public GEX numbers come from vendors, each with its own convention and its own intraday imputation. Before you use one, find three things in its method: the sign convention, whether intraday values are imputed from the tape, and which expiries are included. A number built from SPY open interest is not the same as one built from SPX, because wholesalers internalize much of the SPY flow (Chapter 2B). Use the sign as context for sizing other trades, and do not size from the magnitude, which carries every limit above.

When it fails

GEX fails when it is most wanted. On a stress day the invisible FLEX and OTC books can dominate the listed number, the intraday figure is an imputation, and Black-Scholes understates the put-wing gamma. A convention that fitted one period's flows can also go stale when the dominant flow changes, as it did between 2017 and the 0DTE era.

Decision rule

Before you act on any GEX reading:

  • Name the convention. If you cannot find the vendor's sign convention, do not use the sign.
  • Use the sign, not the level. Treat positive or negative GEX as regime context for sizing other trades (Chapters 8B and 10H). Do not scalp the flip or the walls: the Edge Book rejects that for retail accounts (Chapters 1G and 10I).
  • Discount the atypical day. On a stress day, treat published GEX as one input among several, never as a forecast.
Common mistake: "GEX flipped negative today"

The claim is read as news about dealers, but it may be news about a vendor's convention. The same open interest gives opposite signs under the two conventions, and the flip stays where it is while the side that amplifies changes. Ask which convention before you ask what the number means.

Common mistake: treating the flip as a wall

The gamma flip is a regime boundary, not a barrier that prices bounce off. Crossing it on a calm day usually changes little. Its effect shows under stress, when dealers cannot absorb the flow.

9 · Check yourself

Question 1

A dealer is short 2,000 SPX calls with gamma 0.0010 per point, with SPX at 6,000. What is the line's dollar gamma per 1% move, and what does the dealer do if SPX falls 0.5%?

Answer

By (8A.1), −1×0.0010×6,0002×100×2,000×0.01=−$72-1 \times 0.0010 \times 6{,}000^2 \times 100 \times 2{,}000 \times 0.01 = -\$72−1×0.0010×6,0002×100×2,000×0.01=−$72 million per 1% move. By (8A.3), a fall of 0.5% gives ΔH≈−(−72)×(−0.5)=−36\Delta H \approx -(-72) \times (-0.5) = -36ΔH≈−(−72)×(−0.5)=−36: the dealer sells about $36 million of SPX exposure into the fall. Short gamma trades with the move.

Question 2

When a customer sells a put to a dealer, what does the chain predict for the market when spot falls?

Answer

The dealer is long the put, so long gamma. On a fall the put's delta grows more negative, so the dealer becomes shorter in delta and buys the underlying to get back to flat. Buying into a fall is stabilizing.

Question 3

The same open interest gives GEX of +$7.5 billion under one convention and −$7.5 billion under the other. What information would settle which is right?

Answer

Who holds each side: whether dealers are long or short each line in aggregate. Open interest counts contracts but not their holders. Without position data the sign is an assumption, which is why direct measurements of market-maker gamma, such as Dim, Eraker and Vilkov's, carry weight.

Question 4

Why does the invisible book make published GEX less reliable on stress days than on quiet ones?

Answer

Public models see FLEX only in aggregate and OTC structured-product books not at all. On quiet days those hedges are small and self-cancelling. On stress days, barrier levels, knock-in levels and large collar rolls can make them dominate the listed number.

Question 5

A vendor shows a call wall 1% above spot: what do the Dealer Mechanics Lab and the Edge Book each do with it?

Answer

The Dealer Mechanics Lab reads it as potential resistance, a level where dealer hedging may slow a rally (Chapter 11A). The Edge Book does not trade it. Level rules are model-dependent and not validated since the rise of 0DTE, so it uses only the sign of GEX, as low-confidence context (Chapters 1G, 10H and 10I).
