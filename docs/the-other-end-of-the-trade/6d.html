Gamma

Why the hedge keeps coming undone

Where we are

Chapter 6C set the dealer's hedge and watched it go stale after a 1% move. This chapter measures how fast it goes stale, which is gamma. It then shows which way the forced trades go for a dealer that is short gamma and for one that is long it. It ends with the unit that sizes those trades across a whole market: dollar gamma per 1% move.

What you need first
  • Gamma, its Black-Scholes formula, and why it peaks at the money and grows near expiry (Chapter 5C).
  • The P&L of a delta-hedged option, 12ΓS2[(dS/S)2−σ2dt]\tfrac12\Gamma S^2[(dS/S)^2 - \sigma^2 dt]21​ΓS2[(dS/S)2−σ2dt], and the gamma-theta trade-off (Chapter 5C).
  • Delta hedging, and the re-hedge after a move (Chapter 6C).
  • Open interest (Chapter 5B) and the contract multiplier (Chapter 5A).

1 · Gamma, briefly

Gamma is the partial derivative of delta with respect to spot, which makes it the second derivative of the option's price with respect to spot. Saying those words and understanding what gamma does are different things, so this chapter shows it rather than tells it, starting from the Black-Scholes formula:

Γ=e−qT φ(d1)S σT \Gamma = \frac{e^{-qT}\,\varphi(d_1)}{S\,\sigma\sqrt{T}} Γ=SσT​e−qTφ(d1​)​ (6D.1)

Here SSS is the price of the underlying, σ\sigmaσ the implied volatility, TTT the time to expiry in years, qqq the dividend yield and d1d_1d1​ the z-score of Chapter 6B. The function φ\varphiφ is the standard normal density: the height of the bell curve, not the area under it, which is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) NNN. Chapter 5C draws three facts from this formula:

  1. Gamma is the same for a call and a put with the same strike. By put-call parity (Chapter 5F), their deltas differ by a constant, so they change with spot at the same rate.
  2. Gamma is largest at the money (ATM). The density φ(d1)\varphi(d_1)φ(d1​) peaks when d1=0d_1 = 0d1​=0, which happens approximately when spot equals the strike.
  3. Gamma grows as 1/T1/\sqrt{T}1/T​. As time to expiry shrinks toward zero, at-the-money gamma diverges to infinity. This is not a mathematical curiosity but the dominant feature of modern intraday microstructure, because 0DTE options live exactly in this regime (Chapter 9B).
Worked example: Gamma of an at-the-money SPX call

Step 1: the inputs. Take SPX at 6,000, a strike of 6,000, 30 days to expiry (DTE), 18% implied volatility (IV), a 4% rate and no dividend. Then σT=0.18×0.2867=0.0516\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.18 \times 0.2867 = 0.0516σT​=0.18×0.2867=0.0516, and d1=(0.04+0.0162)×0.0822/0.0516=0.090d_1 = (0.04 + 0.0162) \times 0.0822/0.0516 = 0.090d1​=(0.04+0.0162)×0.0822/0.0516=0.090.

Step 2: gamma. The density at 0.090 is φ=0.397\varphi = 0.397φ=0.397, so (6D.1) gives Γ=0.397/(6,000×0.0516)=0.00128\Gamma = 0.397/(6{,}000 \times 0.0516) = 0.00128Γ=0.397/(6,000×0.0516)=0.00128, about 0.0013 per point of the index.

Step 3: one day before expiry. With 1 day left instead of 30, T\sqrt{T}T​ is 30=5.5\sqrt{30} = 5.530​=5.5 times smaller, so gamma is about 5.5 times larger: 0.0070.

2 · The short-gamma dealer chases the move

Take a dealer that has sold 1,000 at-the-money calls, strike $100, with 30 days to expiry. At first it hedges by buying shares equal to its net short delta, as in Chapter 6C. As spot moves, delta changes, and the existing share position becomes wrong. The dealer must rebalance, and gamma decides which way.

The dealer is short gamma, so its forced trades go in the same direction as the price move. A rise in spot lifts the calls' delta and leaves the dealer short more share-equivalents, so it buys, while a fall makes it sell. It buys high and sells low, and over a stretch of time the losses from doing so are set against the theta it collects.

Worked example: A $1 move up and back

Step 1: the gamma. For these calls, at spot $100 with 30 days left at 20% IV, gamma is 0.0693 per dollar. Across 1,000 contracts of 100 shares, a $1 rise lifts the calls' delta by about 0.0693×100,000=6,9300.0693 \times 100{,}000 = 6{,}9300.0693×100,000=6,930 share-equivalents.

Step 2: the round trip. The dealer buys about 6,930 shares near $101 and, if spot falls back to $100, sells them near $100 as delta falls back. Buying high and selling low has cost it about $6,930.

Step 3: the rule behind it. Each move dSdSdS costs a short-gamma hedger about 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 per share of options: here 12×0.0693×12×100,000=$3,465\tfrac12 \times 0.0693 \times 1^2 \times 100{,}000 = \$3{,}46521​×0.0693×12×100,000=$3,465 a move, and two moves make $6,930.

Step 4: what pays for it. The dealer's theta income over one trading day, dt=1/252dt = 1/252dt=1/252, is 12ΓS2σ2dt\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\sigma^2 dt21​ΓS2σ2dt per share: 12×0.0693×10,000×0.04/252×100,000=$5,500\tfrac12 \times 0.0693 \times 10{,}000 \times 0.04/252 \times 100{,}000 = \$5{,}50021​×0.0693×10,000×0.04/252×100,000=$5,500. The day's moves cost more than that only if they exceed σdt=0.20/252=1.26%\sigma\sqrt{dt} = 0.20/\sqrt{252} = 1.26\%σdt​=0.20/252​=1.26%.

Put together, the short dealer's P&L over each short interval dtdtdt, in which spot moves by dSdSdS, is the negative of the hedged long option's P&L in Chapter 5C:

dΠ≈12 ΓS2[σ2dt−(dSS)2] d\Pi \approx \tfrac12\,\Gamma S^2 \left[\sigma^2 dt - \left(\frac{dS}{S}\right)^2\right] dΠ≈21​ΓS2[σ2dt−(SdS​)2] (6D.2)

The first term is the theta income, 12ΓS2σ2dt\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\sigma^2 dt21​ΓS2σ2dt. The second is the cost of rebalancing, 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2: the buy-high, sell-low loss of short gamma. The dealer wins when the market moves less than the implied volatility it sold, and loses when it moves more.

Watch the flow bars line up with the price. On bar 1 spot slips to $99.21, delta falls from 0.534 to 0.478, and the dealer sells 5,645 shares. On bar 13 a 1.4% drop to $98.84 makes it sell 12,754. At bar 50 the theta income is $189,826, the rebalancing cost $60,766 and the net P&L +$129,060, and the readout says realized stayed below implied. Both are below the 20% at which the calls were sold, so on this path the dealer won.

This is the destabilizing pattern that dominates regimes of negative gamma exposure (GEX) at the market level. Every dollar of move generates more flow in the direction of the move, which generates more move. That feedback loop is what makes Volmageddon (February 2018), March 2020 and single-stock gamma squeezes like GME (GameStop) possible (Chapters 8H and 8I). It also explains why such episodes feel so violent: not because of news, but because of mechanical positive feedback.

3 · The long-gamma mirror

The opposite case is a dealer that is long gamma. It buys dips and sells rallies, the pattern that stabilizes most days in modern markets and gives positive-GEX regimes their mean-reverting feel.

Studies of dealers' SPX positions find them long gamma on average in the 0DTE era. The Dealer Mechanics Lab explains this by retail selling those options and dealers buying them. It reads the result as partly why intraday SPX volatility has compressed even as headline option volume has exploded. The flow data point elsewhere: customers' 0DTE buying and selling are about balanced. The long gamma comes mainly from longer-dated positions that become 0DTEs as they near expiry (Chapter 9B).

Mirror the short-gamma example, with the signs reversed, at SPX scale, so that it lines up with the dollar-gamma figures later in this chapter.

Worked example: A long-gamma dealer at SPX scale

Step 1: the position. A dealer is long 1,000 SPX 6,000 calls with 30 days to expiry, at 18% IV. Each call's delta is about 0.52, so the calls carry 1,000×100×0.52=52,0001{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.52 = 52{,}0001,000×100×0.52=52,000 share-equivalents. To neutralize them the dealer goes short 52,000 share-equivalents of SPX, through futures, a basket of stocks or an exchange-traded fund (ETF).

Step 2: a 1% rise. With gamma about 0.0013 per point, a 1% rise to 6,060 lifts each call's delta by 0.0013×60=0.0780.0013 \times 60 = 0.0780.0013×60=0.078, from 0.52 to about 0.60. The calls now carry about 60,000 share-equivalents, so the hedge must grow from 52,000 short to 60,000 short: the dealer sells about 8,000 more.

Step 3: a 1% fall. At 5,940 the delta falls to about 0.44, the required short shrinks from 52,000 to 44,000, and the dealer buys about 8,000 to close part of the short.

Spot rising makes the long-gamma dealer sell, which presses the rally back toward where it started. Spot falling makes it buy, which absorbs the fall. The pattern reverses the short-gamma case at every step:

Dealer, with 1,000 at-the-money SPX callsSpot falls 1%Spot rises 1%
Short the calls (short gamma)Sells about 8,000Buys about 8,000
Long the calls (long gamma)Buys about 8,000Sells about 8,000

The short-gamma dealer's forced trades reinforce the move, selling into drops and buying into rallies, while the long-gamma dealer's forced trades fade it. It is the same 8,000-share rebalance per 1% move with the opposite sign. This is the structural source of the stabilizing dynamic in positive-GEX regimes. Nobody is providing liquidity out of judgment: the arithmetic of delta hedging requires the opposite trade when the dealer's gamma has the other sign.

The long-gamma dealer's trades also make money. Selling after rises and buying after falls earns 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 per move, the mirror of the short dealer's cost, and harvesting it is called gamma scalping. The long dealer pays for the privilege in theta, so it profits only if the market moves more than the implied volatility it paid for.

Familiar ground

The two signs of gamma are the two kinds of physiological feedback. A long-gamma book behaves like the baroreceptor reflex: a rise in pressure triggers a response that brings it down, and a fall triggers one that brings it up. A short-gamma book behaves like the clotting cascade, where each step amplifies the next. The first kind of system settles and the second runs away, and a market's total dealer gamma decides which kind it resembles that day.

The bridge to Chapter 8A is that long gamma damps and short gamma amplifies. The aggregate dealer book in any product is a sum of both signs across all dealers, strikes and expiries. When that sum is positive, with dealers net long gamma, the market behaves like the long-gamma case writ large, and when it is negative, like the short-gamma case. Chapter 8A performs exactly this aggregation, collapsing thousands of long- and short-gamma positions into a single signed number that classifies the prevailing regime.

4 · Dollar gamma: the unit that matters

Raw gamma is measured in delta per dollar of spot move, which is hard to compare across underlyings at different prices. The standardized unit dealers actually care about is dollar gamma per 1% move:

$Γ1%=Γ×S2×0.01 \$\Gamma_{1\%} = \Gamma \times S^2 \times 0.01 $Γ1%​=Γ×S2×0.01 (6D.3)

To see why this form, start from a 1% move in price, ΔS=0.01 S\Delta S = 0.01\,SΔS=0.01S. The dealer's delta changes by Γ ΔS=0.01 ΓS\Gamma\,\Delta S = 0.01\,\Gamma SΓΔS=0.01ΓS for each share the options are written on, and to hedge it transacts that many shares. The dollar value of that trade is (0.01 ΓS)×S=0.01 ΓS2(0.01\,\Gamma S) \times S = 0.01\,\Gamma S^2(0.01ΓS)×S=0.01ΓS2 per share. (6D.3) is that per-share figure. For one contract, multiply by the multiplier of 100, and for a whole strike, by the open interest (OI) as well: 0.01 ΓS2×100×OI0.01\,\Gamma S^2 \times 100 \times \text{OI}0.01ΓS2×100×OI. Written per share, without the constant 100, it is the canonical dollar gamma per 1% used in every GEX model from SqueezeMetrics onward.

Worked example: Dollar gamma for SPX and for SPY

Step 1: one SPX contract. For the ATM SPX call at 6,000 with 30 days to expiry and 18% IV, Γ≈0.0013\Gamma \approx 0.0013Γ≈0.0013. Per unit of the index, (6D.3) gives about 0.00128×6,0002×0.01=$4610.00128 \times 6{,}000^2 \times 0.01 = \$4610.00128×6,0002×0.01=$461. Times the multiplier of 100 that is $46,100, about $46,000 a contract. With gamma carried to more places, 0.001283, the units lab below gives $46.20k.

Step 2: the same option on SPY. An at-the-money SPY call at $600, with the same expiry and IV, has 10 times the gamma per dollar, 0.0128, because (6D.1) divides by SSS. Its dollar gamma per 1% is 0.0128×6002×0.01=$460.0128 \times 600^2 \times 0.01 = \$460.0128×6002×0.01=$46 a share, or about $4,600 a contract.

Step 3: why a tenth. Gamma scales as 1/S1/S1/S and dollar gamma as ΓS2\Gamma S^2ΓS2, so dollar gamma scales with SSS. SPY is about a tenth of the index, so its contract carries about a tenth of the SPX contract's dollar gamma.

Dollar gamma also prices the rebalancing itself. The trade a 1% move forces is worth $Γ1%\$\Gamma_{1\%}$Γ1%​, and the hedger's gain or loss on it is half the trade times the move, the area of a triangle. So for a move of ppp percent the gain or loss is

ΠΓ≈12×$Γ1%×p2100 \Pi_\Gamma \approx \tfrac12 \times \$\Gamma_{1\%} \times \frac{p^2}{100} ΠΓ​≈21​×$Γ1%​×100p2​ (6D.4)

This is 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 written in dollar gamma. For one SPX contract and a 1% move, that is 12×46,200×0.01=$231\tfrac12 \times 46{,}200 \times 0.01 = \$23121​×46,200×0.01=$231, earned by a long-gamma hedger and paid by a short one. Aggregate dealer dollar gamma across the listed SPX book typically ranges from $20B to $100B per 1% move on a normal day. That is the scale of mechanical hedging flow potentially sitting in dealer inventory. A 1% move in SPX could generate tens of billions of dollars of forced rebalancing, depending on the sign of dealer net gamma. Chapter 8A formalizes this into the GEX metric, and Chapter 8D shows how it can characterize the market's regime. The magnitude of dollar gamma tells you the potential size of mechanical flow, and its sign tells you the direction. Chapter 6A gives the more skeptical reading of the same force from Part X.

5 · What the mechanism predicts

If the framework so far is correct, several predictions follow, and each has support:

  1. Intraday momentum should appear in markets dominated by short-gamma dealer positioning. Baltussen, Da, Lammers and Martens (2021) document this across more than 60 futures markets (Chapter 6C).
  2. Intraday mean reversion should appear in markets dominated by long-gamma dealer positioning. Modern SPX intraday character has compressed measurably since the growth of 0DTE, consistent with the Cboe and Dim-Eraker-Vilkov finding that 0DTE dealer positioning is net long gamma on average (Chapter 8B).
  3. Volatility should be self-reinforcing in short-gamma regimes. The Volmageddon and March 2020 case studies of Chapter 8H document exactly this.
  4. The transition between regimes, the "gamma flip", should be marked by a visible change of character. The gamma flip is the index level at which dealers' net gamma changes sign. Practitioner experience and the academic literature both confirm the change, though the precise identification of the flip level is contested across vendor methodologies.

The framework is empirically supported, but not in the sense that gamma squeezes account for everything. It is supported in the sense that gamma hedging accounts for measurable effects of tens of basis points, with occasional larger episodes during stress. Part VIII quantifies this, Part IX applies it and Part XIII sizes positions for it.

In practice
  • If you sell options, you are the short-gamma side. Two short at-the-money SPX calls at 30 days carry 2×$46,200=$92,4002 \times \$46{,}200 = \$92{,}4002×$46,200=$92,400 of dollar gamma per 1%. By (6D.4), a 2% day costs about 12×92,400×4/100=$1,848\tfrac12 \times 92{,}400 \times 4/100 = \$1{,}84821​×92,400×4/100=$1,848, close to four days of their theta of about $240 a contract a day.
  • Compare positions by dollar gamma, never by raw gamma, since an SPY contract's raw gamma is 10 times an SPX contract's while its dollar gamma is a tenth.
  • Read the sign of dealer gamma as a tendency. Positive dealer gamma tends to damp intraday moves and negative gamma to extend them, by tens of basis points on most days.
When it fails

Gamma is a local number, so for a large move you should reprice rather than multiply (Chapter 5C). Near expiry gamma itself changes fast, as speed and color measure (Chapter 5D), so a 30-day dollar gamma says little about the last day. The sign of a market's dealer gamma rests on an assumption about who holds which options, and the units lab's convention button flips it (Chapter 8B). And on most days the whole mechanism moves the index by tens of basis points, not by percentage points.

Common mistake: comparing raw gamma across underlyings

An at-the-money SPY call shows a gamma of 0.0128 and the matching SPX call 0.0013, so the SPY option looks 10 times more explosive. In dollars it is the other way round: one SPY contract carries about $4,600 of dollar gamma per 1% against the SPX contract's $46,000. Convert with (6D.3) before you compare.

Decision rule
  • Before selling an option, compute its dollar gamma per 1% and the daily move at which gamma losses outrun theta, the implied volatility divided by 252\sqrt{252}252​ (Chapter 5C). Size the position so that the loss from (6D.4) on the largest daily move you expect in the regime fits inside your cushion (Chapter 1E).
  • Treat the market's dealer-gamma sign as context, not as a trade. Use it to adjust size, not to pick levels, as Chapters 10H and 10I advise.
What to carry forward
  • Gamma is the change in delta per dollar of spot. It is largest at the money and grows as 1/T1/\sqrt{T}1/T​, which puts same-day options in the extreme.
  • A short-gamma dealer buys rallies and sells drops, which amplifies moves, while a long-gamma dealer does the opposite and damps them.
  • The short dealer earns theta and pays 12Γ(dS)2\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^221​Γ(dS)2 on every move, so it wins when realized volatility stays below the implied volatility it sold.
  • Dollar gamma per 1%, ΓS2×0.01\Gamma S^2 \times 0.01ΓS2×0.01, sizes the rebalancing trade in dollars and compares positions across underlyings. A 1% move is worth half of 1% of it to the hedger.
  • The evidence supports effects of tens of basis points, with larger episodes in stress.

6 · Check yourself

Question 1

A dealer is short 500 at-the-money calls with a gamma of 0.05. What does its hedge do when spot rises $2?

Answer

Each call's delta rises by about 0.05×2=0.100.05 \times 2 = 0.100.05×2=0.10, so the calls' exposure grows by 500×100×0.10=5,000500 \times 100 \times 0.10 = 5{,}000500×100×0.10=5,000 share-equivalents. The dealer buys 5,000 shares, after the rise.

Question 2

What does the dealer trade if it holds the same calls long instead and spot falls $2?

Answer

It buys 5,000 shares, because its long calls lose 5,000 share-equivalents of delta and leave its short hedge 5,000 too large. It buys the excess back after the fall.

Question 3

What is the dollar gamma per 1% of 10 SPY contracts at $600 with a gamma of 0.0128?

Answer

It is 0.0128×6002×0.01×100×10=$46,0800.0128 \times 600^2 \times 0.01 \times 100 \times 10 = \$46{,}0800.0128×6002×0.01×100×10=$46,080, about the same as one SPX contract, since 10 SPY contracts are about 10 units of the index.

Question 4

A long-gamma book has $10 million of dollar gamma per 1%. What does a 2% move earn it, before theta?

Answer

By (6D.4), 12×10,000,000×4/100=$200,000\tfrac12 \times 10{,}000{,}000 \times 4/100 = \$200{,}00021​×10,000,000×4/100=$200,000.

Question 5

Why does an at-the-money option's gamma rise about 5.5 times between 30 days and 1 day to expiry?

Answer

Gamma scales as 1/T1/\sqrt{T}1/T​ at the money, and 30=5.5\sqrt{30} = 5.530​=5.5. The units lab shows it: $46.20k per 1% at 30 days against $254.03k at 1 day.
