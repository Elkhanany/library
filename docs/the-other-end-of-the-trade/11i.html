Part XI · Swing trading · Chapter 11I

Pre-earnings momentum

Riding the run-up, and leaving before the report

Where we are

Chapter 11H's four setups read prices. This one reads a calendar: the week or two before a company reports its earnings, when its implied volatility climbs and its shares often drift. The trade rides that run-up and leaves before the report, which turns a gamble on one number into a swing trade with a capped downside.

What you need first
  • Implied volatility, the rule of 16, and why a straddle is worth about 0.8 of a one-standard-deviation move (Chapters 5E and 11A).
  • Forward volatility between two expiries (Chapter 5G).
  • The Trend Pullback and the Compression Breakout (Chapter 11H), Stage 2 (Chapter 11E) and the composite signal (Chapter 11G).
  • Why the quoted implied volatility climbs into a scheduled event (Chapter 11K).
  • Gap risk (Chapter 5F), and call debit and diagonal spreads (Chapter 12C).

1 · The evidence, first

The Dealer Mechanics Lab grades pre-earnings momentum Tier B/C on Chapter 11B's scale. Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD), the subject of Chapter 11J, is Tier A, on the evidence of Bernard and Thomas (1989) and Chordia and Shivakumar (2006). The pre-earnings drift, conditional on momentum and options flow, has mixed evidence.

So the framework's primary edge is the exit before earnings, not the entry. The disciplined exit converts a binary catalyst gamble into a swing trade with a capped downside. Without it, the trade is a single-name event play with a strongly negative expected value. The Dealer Mechanics Lab cites Adams and Neururer (2020) as documenting this for retail short volatility held through earnings.

Chapter 10I gives that paper's finding in the Edge Book's words: naive single-name short volatility through earnings has a negative mean despite a positive median. Most trades win a little, and a few lose a lot. The paper is about selling options through the report. A long stock position held through it is a different exposure, which the finding speaks to only indirectly.

2 · The central claim

The central claim is that stocks with a constructive pre-earnings setup and momentum-confirming flow can be swung profitably into the IV ramp, with the trade closed before the earnings event itself. This is fundamentally different from plays that trade through earnings, which are catalyst gambles. The pre-earnings swing captures the structural bid for implied volatility plus the pre-event drift, then exits before the binary outcome.

The trade gets its own chapter because its catalyst dynamics, its effects on the volatility surface and its execution friction differ in kind from the four setups of Chapter 11H. It is also the standalone application behind the screener described at the end of this chapter.

3 · Two effects before a report

The IV ramp

Implied volatility rises in the days before an earnings announcement, because options look forward and the report is a known source of a large jump. Option implied volatility before earnings blends two parts: ambient volatility, the normal daily movement, and event volatility, the anticipated jump on the report. As each trading day passes, the one-day event becomes a larger share of the option's remaining life, which pulls up the headline IV. The mechanism is well documented.

The arithmetic is short. Write σa\sigma_aσa​ for the ambient volatility, JJJ for the standard deviation of the report's one-day move as a fraction of the price, and TTT for the option's time to expiry in years. Variances add (Chapter 5E), so the option's total variance and its quoted volatility σI\sigma_IσI​ are

σI2 T=σa2 T+J2 \sigma_I^2\,T = \sigma_a^2\,T + J^2 σI2​T=σa2​T+J2 (11I.1)
σI=σa2+J2/T \sigma_I = \sqrt{\sigma_a^2 + J^2/T} σI​=σa2​+J2/T​ (11I.2)

As TTT shrinks toward the report, J2/TJ^2/TJ2/T grows, and it grows faster the closer the report comes. The Dealer Mechanics Lab gives the typical magnitude: at-the-money IV rises 20% to 50% in the 5 to 10 days before earnings, with most of the rise concentrated in the final 3 days. IV typically peaks the day before earnings.

Worked example: A quote that rises while the premium falls

Step 1: the inputs. Suppose a $100 stock has an ambient volatility of 30% and a priced report move of J=6%J = 6\%J=6%, and take an option expiring 7 calendar days after the report.

Step 2: the quote. Ten days before the report, T=17/365T = 17/365T=17/365, and (11I.2) gives σI=0.09+0.0036×365/17=40.9%\sigma_I = \sqrt{0.09 + 0.0036 \times 365/17} = 40.9\%σI​=0.09+0.0036×365/17​=40.9%. Just before the report, with 7 days left, it gives 52.7%. The quote has risen 29%, and the last 3 days carry almost half of that rise.

Step 3: the premium. Priced by Black-Scholes with a 4% interest rate and no dividend, the at-the-money call is worth $3.61 ten days out and $2.95 just before the report. Its value fell 18% while its quote rose 29%, because the total variance of (11I.1), σa2T+J2\sigma_a^2 T + J^2σa2​T+J2, loses a day of ambient variance every day.

The example carries the point Chapter 11K makes about any scheduled event. A rising quote on a fixed expiry is arithmetic, and it pays a long option nothing. A long option gains from the ramp only if the market raises its price for the event itself, a larger JJJ, as the report nears. Shares collect nothing from the ramp at all. The Edge Book's advice points the same way: use the earnings calendar to avoid being caught long premium that decays into a print (Chapter 10I).

The pre-earnings drift

The second effect is a prelude to PEAD. Some stocks tend to drift modestly higher in the 1 to 3 weeks before earnings. They share three marks: constructive momentum, positive options flow, with call buying outweighing put buying, and a tightening IV term structure, with forward volatility cheap relative to event volatility. The literature on this drift is less robust than that on post-earnings drift, which is one of the most replicated findings in finance. The Dealer Mechanics Lab judges the pre-earnings effect real nonetheless, and sharpest in large-cap momentum names with strong analyst-revision flow.

The swing combines both effects. It rides the underlying drift while the IV ramp inflates the option premium, then exits before the report resolves the IV crush against it. By the arithmetic above, the option leg of that plan works only when the event is being priced up.

4 · The implied move

For any earnings event, the market prices an implied move through the at-the-money straddle of the first expiry after the report. With CCC and PPP the prices of the at-the-money call and put and SSS the price of the stock,

implied move=C+PS \text{implied move} = \frac{C + P}{S} implied move=SC+P​ (11I.3)

A more accurate version weights the at-the-money straddle with the first two out-of-the-money strangles. ORATS uses such a composite expected-move calculation as a standard reference for earnings flow.

Worked example: Reading a straddle

A stock trades at $200, and the at-the-money call and put of the first expiry after its report cost $6.20 and $5.80. The straddle costs $12.00, so by (11I.3) the implied move is 12/200=6.0%12/200 = 6.0\%12/200=6.0%. A one-standard-deviation move is about 6.0%/0.8=7.5%6.0\%/0.8 = 7.5\%6.0%/0.8=7.5%.

The implied move is the expected move of Chapter 11A. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls it the market's median expectation. Strictly, a straddle prices the average absolute move, which for a normal curve is 0.8 of a standard deviation (Chapter 5E), while the median absolute move is smaller, at 0.67 of one. The operational uses do not depend on the distinction:

  1. Position sizing. If the implied move is ±9%, a position sized to absorb a 5% adverse move is obliterated by a typical earnings reaction. Size for the implied move, or exit before the event.
  2. Historical comparison. ORATS, Market Chameleon and similar tools give the median actual move over the trailing 8 quarters. If that median is 12% and the implied move 6%, the market is under-pricing volatility, which favors long-volatility structures, a long straddle or strangle, if held through. If the implied move is 9% against a historical 6%, the market is over-pricing, which favors short-volatility structures after the event. Selling premium after the event, into the IV crush, is a real strategy, and a separate one.
  3. Strike selection. The implied-move boundary is a natural strike for the short legs of diagonal and calendar spreads: sell calls or puts at the boundary on the front-month side.

The Edge Book rejects the neighboring trade, naive short volatility held through the report, for retail accounts (Chapter 10I). Its least-bad retail variant is a calendar spread on a liquid stock that reports, a small edge better used as an occasional small bet than as a sleeve.

5 · The setup

Six conditions must all hold:

  1. Stage 2 confirmed: the 30-day SMA rising, the price above it and ADX above 20, stricter than the 18 of Chapter 11E.
  2. Earnings 3 to 10 trading days away. The sweet spot is 5 to 7 days, enough for the IV ramp to develop without too much risk of the catalyst spilling over.
  3. Constructive options flow: net call buying on at least 5 of the prior 10 trading days, visible through Cboe options-flow data, ORATS, Market Chameleon or similar.
  4. An event premium in the term structure: front-month at-the-money IV above 90-day at-the-money IV by at least 15 vol points, a steep backwardation that reflects the report.
  5. Forward IV not cheap. Compute forward IV as in Chapter 5G. If it is below 70% of the ambient long-dated IV, the market expects a significant crush in volatility after the event, and the exit before earnings becomes mandatory.
  6. A composite signal of +20 or more, a supportive macro regime.

Once all six hold, enter on the next constructive technical signal:

  • A pullback to the 21-EMA with RSI(2) below 15, a Trend Pullback overlay.
  • A breakout from a VCP pivot, a Compression Breakout overlay.
  • A higher low confirmed against the prior pullback.

The pre-earnings setup is essentially one of the four base archetypes of Chapter 11H with a pre-earnings overlay, which justifies sizing up the conviction and a different exit rule. The Trend Pullback overlay here allows RSI(2) up to 15, looser than Chapter 11H's 10, and names only the 21-EMA.

6 · Vehicles

The trade has three primary vehicles, each with its own trade-offs:

  1. Shares, the preferred and cleanest expression. Long shares give pure directional exposure with no options friction. Exit before earnings, typically at the close on T−1 or on the morning of T-day, where T-day is the day of the report and T−1 the trading day before it.
  2. A long call spread, for defined risk. Buy an in-the-money or at-the-money call, and sell a call at or just above the implied-move boundary. The call debit spread captures most of the directional upside with a bounded loss. Its IV exposure is partly hedged, since the long call has positive vega and the short call negative, leaving a net vega that is positive but small. Exit before earnings.
  3. A diagonal call spread, a hybrid of direction and volatility that needs careful reading. Buy a back-month at-the-money or in-the-money call, and sell a front-month call at the implied-move boundary. This is not a pure directional expression. It joins a directional thesis, the long back-month call, to a short-volatility and short-event leg, the front-month call, which profits from the front month's IV crush after earnings.

Carrying the diagonal's short front leg through earnings takes on event risk on the short side. A large favorable gap can drive the short strike deep in the money and create assignment risk. Diagonals belong in the pre-earnings playbook only under two conditions. The surface analysis must favor them, with forward IV cheap relative to front IV, and you must have a defined plan for closing or rolling the short leg before earnings.

For most retail applications, shares are the default, and the long call spread is the option overlay for defined risk in single names. A diagonal is a trade on the volatility surface that happens to overlap a directional thesis. Treat it as such, not as a fancier call spread.

7 · The exit before earnings

The defining discipline of this trade is the exit before the report. The position is closed:

  • Shares: at the close on T−1 at the latest, or on the morning of T-day if the morning gap is favorable.
  • Long call spreads: at the close on T−1, or earlier if the profit target is hit.
  • Diagonals: depending on the structure, typically on T−1. Otherwise they are held through with an explicit acknowledgment that you are now in a trade-through position, with different risk.

Post-earnings outcomes are binary at retail scale. The realized move can be 2 to 3 times the implied move in either direction. The largest gaps come on surprises in guidance, not just in earnings per share (EPS). An implied move of 6% can therefore arrive as 12% to 18%. A stop does not cap a gap: a position with its stop 3.5% below entry that opens 15% lower loses 15/3.5=4.315/3.5 = 4.315/3.5=4.3 times its planned risk. The Dealer Mechanics Lab estimates that a single bad earnings outcome can wipe out 10 to 20 successful pre-earnings trades.

In its reading, the expected value of trading single names through earnings at retail is consistently negative or barely positive, again citing Adams and Neururer (2020). The expected value of exiting before earnings on the same setup remains positive, because the trade captures the IV ramp and the drift while declining the binary risk. This is the discipline that turns the pre-earnings thesis from a gambling strategy into a swing strategy.

Familiar ground

The exit resembles holding bevacizumab before elective surgery. The benefit already gained is kept, and the patient steps away from a scheduled, known risk, poor wound healing, at the one moment it would bite. The trade keeps the drift it has earned and steps away from the report, whose outcome it cannot predict and whose downside it cannot survive cheaply.

8 · A worked trade in NVDA

Worked example: A constructive pre-earnings swing

Step 1: the screen. NVDA trades at $720 with earnings in 6 days. Its 30-day SMA is at $680 and rising, with an ATR-normalized slope of +0.15, a strong Stage 2. RSI(14) is 62, healthy momentum that is not extreme, and the composite signal is +45. Net call buying shows on 7 of the last 10 sessions. Front-month at-the-money IV is 48% and 90-day at-the-money IV is 32%, a 16-point spread that confirms the event premium.

Step 2: the forward IV. The front month has 14 days to run, so by Chapter 5G the 76-day window after it carries σf2×76=0.322×90−0.482×14=9.216−3.226=5.990\sigma_f^2 \times 76 = 0.32^2 \times 90 - 0.48^2 \times 14 = 9.216 - 3.226 = 5.990σf2​×76=0.322×90−0.482×14=9.216−3.226=5.990, which gives σf2=0.0788\sigma_f^2 = 0.0788σf2​=0.0788 and σf=28.1%\sigma_f = 28.1\%σf​=28.1%. Forward IV is about 4 points below the 90-day IV, so a modest crush after the event is priced in. At 88% of 32%, it is well above the 70% floor of condition 5.

Step 3: the surface trade it suggests. The 16-point front premium carries the event component. A diagonal call spread, long the back month and short the front near the implied move, would harvest that asymmetry in the event premium.

Step 4: the trigger. NVDA pulls back 2.8% over 3 sessions to $700, with RSI(2) at 12. It touches the 21-EMA at $698 and holds with an intraday reclaim.

Step 5: the conviction. Chapter 13B scores the trade on five factors:

  • Evidence, E=0.65E = 0.65E=0.65: pre-earnings momentum is Tier B with mixed evidence, and the exit before the event lifts it above trading through.
  • Mechanism, M=0.80M = 0.80M=0.80: the IV ramp and PEAD are both well documented.
  • Regime, R=0.85R = 0.85R=0.85: a composite of +45, Stage 2 and all six conditions met.
  • Execution, X=0.85X = 0.85X=0.85: NVDA's options are highly liquid, but their spreads are wider than SPX's.
  • Sizing, S=0.80S = 0.80S=0.80: the exit before the event insulates the position from gap risk.

The product is C=0.65×0.80×0.85×0.85×0.80=0.300C = 0.65 \times 0.80 \times 0.85 \times 0.85 \times 0.80 = 0.300C=0.65×0.80×0.85×0.85×0.80=0.300. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls this medium-high and takes the trade. On Chapter 13B's bands it sits exactly at the floor of medium conviction, from 0.3 to 0.5, where a trade is taken at reduced size.

Step 6: the trade. Buy 200 NVDA shares at $702, intraday, on confirmation of the reclaim. The risk is $5,000, so the stop goes at $677, about 3.5% below entry, wider than a normal Trend Pullback's stop to absorb pre-earnings volatility. No profit target is needed, because the primary exit is time: the close on T−1.

Step 7: the outcome. On day 1 after entry NVDA is at $710 (+1.1%), and on day 2 at $718 (+2.3%), with IV ramping aggressively. On day 3 it is at $725, and the stop is trailed to $710. Day 4 is T−1: NVDA is at $733, and the position is closed at $732 at the close. The profit is $30×200=$6,000\$30 \times 200 = \$6{,}000$30×200=$6,000, about 1.2R.

Worked example: What the exit declined

Step 1: the report. Earnings come out in extended hours with the stock at $735. It first pops to $745 on the print, then guidance disappoints on the call, and the next day it closes at $698, a one-day fall of about 5%.

Step 2: the holder's result. A trader who held would stand at $698: a loss of $4×200=$800\$4 \times 200 = \$800$4×200=$800 on the trade, and $35×200=$7,000\$35 \times 200 = \$7{,}000$35×200=$7,000 below the $733 close on T−1. The trailed stop at $710 would not have saved the difference, because after an overnight report a stop fills at the first price available (Chapter 5F).

Step 3: the value of the exit. Exiting beat holding by ($732−$698)×200=$6,800(\$732 - \$698) \times 200 = \$6{,}800($732−$698)×200=$6,800, about 1.4R on the $5,000 risk. It is tempting to call it a $13,000 swing, 2.6R, by adding the $6,000 banked to the $7,000 given back. That counts the run-up twice, since the $7,000 fall from the T−1 close includes the $6,200 of open profit the holder gave back.

The example's dates do not quite add up, since three sessions of pullback and four days to T−1 take more than 6 trading days, but nothing above depends on them. Its conviction score also shows how little room the framework leaves: one factor a notch lower, and the trade falls below 0.3.

9 · The figure: exit or hold

The figure builds a synthetic run-up to earnings. A $100 stock moves by the daily drift each day up to T-day, while its implied volatility climbs in a straight line from the ambient level to the event peak on T-day. The earnings move lands on T+1, when IV is crushed to 85% of ambient.

The call is the $100 strike, priced by Black-Scholes with 30 days to run after T-day. A table sets selling at the close on T−1 against holding through the event, for 100 shares and for one contract. Sliders set the days before earnings (3 to 14, starting at 7), the ambient IV (10% to 60%, starting at 22%) and the event IV peak (20% to 150%, starting at 80%). Two more set the daily drift (−1% to +2% a day, starting at 0.5%) and the earnings move (−20% to +20%, starting at +3%).

The verdict beneath the table compares the stock rows only, and each clause of it appears only when it is true. A gap under $0.50 reads as similar outcomes. Start at the defaults, then set the earnings move to −5%, then to −0.5%.

At the defaults IV climbs from 22% to 80% and falls to 18.7% on T+1, while the stock rises to $103.04 by T−1 and $106.66 after the report. Exiting makes +$304 on the stock and +$723 on the call. Holding makes +$666 on the stock and +$424 on the call. The verdict reads "Hold-through outperformed by $362 on stock this time", with a warning that one realization is not an expectation.

The call column says the opposite of the verdict. The stock rose 3% on the report, yet the call lost value overnight, from $11.35 on T-day to $7.23, because its IV fell from 80% to 18.7%. At these settings the call needs an earnings move above about 6% before holding it beats selling on T−1.

At a move of −5%, exiting wins by $466 on the stock, and the verdict adds that declining the binary outcome preserved the drift's profit. At −0.5%, the stock's two plans differ by less than $0.50, and the verdict calls them similar. Holding shares adds exactly two more days, T-day's drift and the report, so it beats exiting whenever those two days together are positive.

Two limits keep the figure honest. It draws the ramp as a straight line imposed on the quote, where the arithmetic of (11I.2) bends upward near the report. Its call gains from the ramp only because the ramp raises the call's total variance: the market in the figure pays ever more for the event. The verdict's closing claim, that the negative skew of single-name earnings moves makes the exit dominant in expectation, is also more than one path can show. The case for the exit rests on the argument above.

10 · The screener and when to skip

For systematic use, a screener filters the universe daily on seven conditions:

  1. Earnings calendar: earnings 3 to 10 trading days out.
  2. Stage: Weinstein Stage 2 confirmed, with a rising 30-SMA, the price above it and ADX above 20.
  3. Momentum: RSI(14) between 50 and 70, healthy rather than extreme.
  4. Options flow: a cumulative call-buying signal over the prior 5 to 10 sessions.
  5. Term structure: front-month IV above 90-day IV by 15 or more vol points.
  6. Liquidity: average option volume above 5,000 contracts on the near-dated at-the-money strikes.
  7. Composite: a macro regime of +20 or more.

Stocks that pass all seven become candidates. A manual review of each checks for a clean technical pattern, no recent fundamental deterioration and positive analyst flow. This is the structural skeleton of the screener. The Dealer Mechanics Lab's signal-aggregation logic and 100-point composite scoring sit on top of it, though it does not describe them.

The most important discipline is recognizing when not to take a candidate:

  • An implied move above 10%. A single-name position cannot be sized cleanly with stops at this magnitude, so use defined-risk options or skip.
  • Stage 3 emerging, with the 30-SMA flattening. The setup requires Stage 2, and a flattening average means it is weakening structurally.
  • A composite below +20. The macro regime does not support the trade, so defer it.
  • A guidance cut or analyst downgrades within the last 2 weeks. Fundamental flow is already negative, so skip.
  • A move of more than 8% in the 5 days before the setup. The drift has likely already happened, and you would be chasing.
  • A flat or normal term structure. The event is not being priced into options, either because the market does not care or because the catalyst is not material.

The skip discipline is half the system. The most profitable thing about a screener is the candidates it rejects. Chapter 13D works through one candidate that the conviction score rejects.

In practice
  • Default to shares. Chapter 1F cites Govindaraj, Li and Zhao (2020): bid-ask spreads on single-stock options before earnings averaged 31.7% of the mid-quote. A round trip in the option overlay can cost more than the drift pays.
  • Make the options outlast the report. The ramp lives only in expiries that contain the report, so an option overlay should expire after earnings, typically 30 to 45 days out, although the trade closes before them (Chapter 11M).
  • Automate the exit. A market-on-close order placed on T−1 executes the rule without leaving room for a change of mind. Confirm the report's date and time first, since an exit timed to the wrong day protects nothing.
  • Mind the tax. Gains on shares held for days are short-term, taxed at ordinary rates (Chapter 2E).
When it fails
  • News arrives before the exit. Anything that moves the stock before T−1 hits a position sized for a 3.5% stop, and a stop does not cap a gap.
  • The ramp never comes. With a flat term structure the event is not being priced, and an option overlay decays with nothing to offset it.
  • The drift is already spent. A stock that has already run hard gives the chaser the risk without the drift, which is why a move of more than 8% in 5 days is a skip.
  • The exit is skipped. A trade that has gone well tempts you to hold through, the failure Chapter 11N names directly. One bad report can erase the gains of many good trades.
Decision rule
  • Enter only when all six conditions hold and a Trend Pullback, Compression Breakout or higher-low trigger fires, with none of the six skip conditions present.
  • Choose shares by default and a long call spread for defined risk. Use a diagonal only when the surface favors it and a plan to close its short leg before earnings is written down.
  • Size from a stop wide enough for pre-earnings volatility, and never for a move smaller than the implied move if there is any chance of holding through.
  • Exit at the close on T−1, or on the morning of T-day on a favorable gap. Do not hold through the report.
Common mistake: holding through "just this once"

The position is up, momentum looks strong and the report seems a formality. That is exactly the moment the rule exists for. Holding through turns a swing trade back into the catalyst gamble the exit was designed to avoid.

Common mistake: treating a diagonal as a fancier call spread

A diagonal is short the front-month event premium. Held through the report, it is a short-volatility trade on one stock, with assignment risk if the stock gaps through the short strike. Choose it for the surface, with a plan for the short leg, or not at all.

What to carry forward
  • Pre-earnings momentum is Tier B/C evidence; the exit before earnings is the framework's real edge.
  • The quoted IV rises into a report as the event becomes a larger share of the option's life. A long option profits from the ramp only if the market prices the event up; shares ride only the drift.
  • The implied move is the at-the-money straddle over the price. Size for it, compare it with past moves, and use its boundary for short strikes.
  • Six conditions, a technical trigger from Chapter 11H, shares by default, and an exit at the close on T−1.
  • Skip generously: a large implied move, a weakening stage, a weak regime, fresh bad news, a stock that has already run, or a flat term structure.

11 · Check yourself

Question 1

A stock trades at $150, and the at-the-money call and put of the first expiry after its report cost $4.10 and $3.90. What is the implied move, and what does it say about a position sized for a 4% adverse move?

Answer

The straddle costs $8.00, so the implied move is 8/150=5.3%8/150 = 5.3\%8/150=5.3%. A 4% cushion is smaller than the move the market prices as typical, so either size for the implied move or exit before the report.

Question 2

A candidate's front-month IV is 50% with 14 days to run and its 90-day IV is 35%. Do conditions 4 and 5 hold?

Answer

The spread is exactly 15 points, so condition 4 holds. Forward IV satisfies σf2×76=0.352×90−0.502×14=7.525\sigma_f^2 \times 76 = 0.35^2 \times 90 - 0.50^2 \times 14 = 7.525σf2​×76=0.352×90−0.502×14=7.525, so σf2=0.0990\sigma_f^2 = 0.0990σf2​=0.0990 and σf=31.5%\sigma_f = 31.5\%σf​=31.5%. That is 90% of 35%, above the 70% floor, so condition 5 holds too.

Question 3

In the NVDA trade, front-month IV is 48% over 14 days and forward IV 28.1%. Taking the forward IV as the ambient level, what report move do the options price?

Question 4

At the figure's defaults the stock rises 3% on the report. Why does holding the call through it do worse than selling on T−1?

Answer

On T+1 the call's IV collapses from 80% to 18.7%, and the loss of volatility outweighs the gain from a 3% move. The call falls from $11.35 to $7.23, so holding makes +$424 against +$723 for the exit. At these settings the move must exceed about 6% before holding the call wins.

Question 5

A candidate meets all six conditions but has risen 9% in the last 5 days. Do you take it?

Answer

No. A move of more than 8% in the 5 days before the setup means the drift has likely already happened, and entering now is chasing.

Question 6

Why is the exit's value in the NVDA example $6,800 and not $13,000?

Answer

The holder's final position is $698, $4 below the $702 entry, a loss of $800, against the $6,000 banked by exiting: a difference of $6,800. The $13,000 adds the $7,000 fall from the T−1 close to the $6,000 gain, but that fall already includes the $6,200 of open profit, so the run-up is counted twice.
