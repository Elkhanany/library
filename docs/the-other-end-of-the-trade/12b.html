Part XII · Strategy atlas · Chapter 12B

Novice strategies

Single options and simple spreads

Where we are

Chapter 12A chose a structure by the view it should express. This chapter opens the atlas with its first tier: five novice strategies, each a single option or an option paired with shares. Every later tier is built from these five, which are also where most accounts first meet options.

What you need first
  • The four primitive positions, breakevens and the contract multiplier (Chapter 5A).
  • Put-call parity, which makes a covered call and a cash-secured put the same position (Chapter 5F).
  • The Greeks (Chapter 5C) and implied volatility (Chapter 5E).
  • Reading a structure by adding its legs (Chapter 12A).

1 · How to read an entry

Each entry opens with a card that gives the legs, the view, the maximum profit and loss, the breakeven and the net Greeks at entry. The prose after it covers the regime the strategy fits, how it fails and a worked example. It adds the dealer's side where that says something new.

The cards use one notation. SSS is the underlying's price now and STS_TST​ its price at expiry, KKK is the strike and PPP the premium, each per share or per index point. For positions that hold shares, BBB is the cost basis, the price you paid per share. The Greeks are delta, gamma, theta and vega (Chapter 5C). A plus sign means the position gains when that quantity rises. A contract's dollars are the quote times $100: 100 shares for SPY, or $100 per index point for SPX (Chapter 5A).

The dealer who takes the other side of your trade holds your position with every sign flipped, then hedges it (Chapter 6B). Where you are long gamma, the dealer is short gamma, and so on through each Greek.

The premiums in the worked examples are round, illustrative numbers, chosen so the arithmetic is easy to follow, and each example stands alone. Rather than quotes, they are numbers that sit well below what a pricing model gives at the volatilities they state. At 18% implied volatility, with interest ignored, the Black-Scholes model of Chapter 5C prices the first example's 30-day 6,050 SPX call near $100, not $35. A price of $35 corresponds to an implied volatility of about 8%. The formulas hold at any premium, though a real breakeven lies further from the strike than the examples suggest.

2 · Long call

Long callAt a glance
LegsBuy 1 call at strike KKK for a premium PPP
ViewA strong rise; implied volatility cheap relative to your forecast

Maximum profitUnbounded
Maximum lossPPP, the premium paid
BreakevenK+PK + PK+P
Greeks at entryDelta +, gamma +, theta −, vega +

The dealer who sold this call is short the call, which leaves the dealer short delta, short gamma and long theta. The dealer buys shares at once to hedge the delta and keeps re-hedging as the price moves (Chapter 6C). Structurally, your long call pays the dealer to manufacture upside convexity for you out of the underlying.

The long call fits best in a low-IV regime, where your directional thesis exceeds the implied move. It fits worst when IV is high, because you pay an inflated premium that decays quickly. It fails in three ways, two of which can strike even when your view of direction is right:

  • An IV crush after the expected catalyst arrives: you are right on direction but lose on vega.
  • Time decay that accelerates in the final two weeks before expiry.
  • A move the wrong way, which leaves the call to expire worthless.
Worked example: A long SPX call

Step 1: the trade. With SPX at 6,000, buy one 6,050 call with 30 days to expiry (DTE) at 18% IV for $35.00. It costs 35×$10035 \times \$10035×$100, which is $3,500, and it breaks even at 6,050+356{,}050 + 356,050+35, which is 6,085.

Step 2: a rally. If SPX reaches 6,150 by expiry, the call pays (6,150−6,050)×$100(6{,}150 - 6{,}050) \times \$100(6,150−6,050)×$100, which is $10,000. Less the $3,500 it cost, that is a profit of $6,500.

Step 3: no move. If SPX is still at 6,000 at expiry, the call expires worthless, and the whole $3,500 is lost.

3 · Long put

Long putAt a glance
LegsBuy 1 put at strike KKK for a premium PPP
ViewA strong fall; or a hedge for long stock

The long put fits best as a hedge in mid-IV regimes. As a standalone directional bet it needs significant downside conviction, enough to overcome the negative drift built into option premiums (Chapter 10C). It fails in three ways, one from how puts are priced and two from how the index behaves:

  • Volatility skew makes out-of-the-money (OTM) puts typically expensive relative to OTM calls (Chapter 5G).
  • Structural mean reversion in calm regimes pulls the index back from its dips.
  • Equity drift over multi-week horizons works against you, since the index tends to rise.
Worked example: A long SPX put

Step 1: the trade. With SPX at 6,000, buy one 5,950 put with 30 DTE at 22% IV, higher than the at-the-money (ATM) call's IV because of skew, for $35.00. It costs $3,500, and it breaks even at 5,950−355{,}950 - 355,950−35, which is 5,915.

Step 2: a fall. If SPX falls to 5,800, the put pays (5,950−5,800)×$100(5{,}950 - 5{,}800) \times \$100(5,950−5,800)×$100, which is $15,000. Less the $3,500 it cost, that is a profit of $11,500.

4 · Covered call

Covered callAt a glance
LegsOwn 100 shares at cost basis BBB; sell 1 call at strike KKK above the price, for a premium PPP
ViewYou own the stock anyway, and would sell it at KKK for premium income
P&L at expiry(ST−B)+P−max⁡(ST−K,0)(S_T - B) + P - \max(S_T - K, 0)(ST​−B)+P−max(ST​−K,0) per share
Maximum profit(K−B)+P(K - B) + P(K−B)+P per share
Maximum lossB−PB - PB−P per share, if the stock goes to zero
BreakevenB−PB - PB−P
Greeks at entryDelta + but below +1.0 per share, gamma −, theta +, vega −

The P&L has a slope of +1 below the strike, where you hold the shares, and 0 above it, where the sold call cancels them.

The maximum loss is bounded but potentially enormous in dollars. A 100-share SPY position at $580 has about $58,000 of downside before the premium offsets any of it. Calling a covered call "limited risk" beside a naked short call is fair. Treating it as a small-risk position is not.

The dealer is long the call you sold, which leaves the dealer long delta, long gamma and short theta. To hedge the growing delta, the dealer sells shares as the underlying rises. In aggregate, that hedging contributes to the mean-reverting character of positive gamma exposure (GEX, Chapter 8B).

The covered call fits sideways or slowly rising markets. It fits worst in strong bull markets, where you cap your upside far below where the stock actually goes.

It needs shares to cover, so it runs only on single stocks and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) you can own: SPY, QQQ, IWM, AAPL, NVDA and so on. You cannot run a covered call on SPX, XSP, NDX or RUT, which are cash-settled indexes with no shares to own. The cash-settled equivalent of "I would sell at a higher level" is a cash-secured short call, a naked call against cash collateral. Its loss is unbounded, a fundamentally different risk profile from a covered call. Do not conflate the two.

Worked example: A covered call on SPY

Step 1: the trade. Own 100 SPY shares bought at $580, and sell one 595 call with 30 DTE for $4.50, a credit of $450 that lowers the breakeven to 580−4.50=$575.50580 - 4.50 = \$575.50580−4.50=$575.50.

Step 2: a quiet month. If SPY closes at $590, the call expires worthless, leaving you the shares and the $450 premium.

Step 3: a rally. If SPY closes at $605, the shares are called away at $595. You make (595−580)×100(595 - 580) \times 100(595−580)×100, which is $1,500, on the stock and $450 of premium, $1,950 in all. Had you held the shares without selling the call, you would have made $2,500.

5 · Cash-secured put

Cash-secured putAt a glance
LegsSell 1 put at strike KKK for a premium PPP, with cash of K×100K \times 100K×100 set aside
ViewYou are a willing buyer at KKK, paid to wait
P&L at expiryP−max⁡(K−ST,0)P - \max(K - S_T, 0)P−max(K−ST​,0) per share
Maximum profitPPP
Maximum lossK−PK - PK−P per share, if the underlying goes to zero
BreakevenK−PK - PK−P
Greeks at entryThose of a short put: delta +, gamma −, theta +, vega −

A cash-secured put has the payoff of a short put, with the obligation to buy at the strike fully funded in cash. Its maximum loss is bounded but very large in dollars: a 570-strike SPY cash-secured put has a notional maximum loss near $57,000 per contract. The loss is capped, not small. "Defined risk" is a claim relative to a naked call's exposure, not a license to size the position as if it were tiny (Chapter 13C covers sizing).

It fits sideways or slowly rising markets, where the put expires worthless. Run systematically on XSP, it is the canonical vehicle for harvesting the variance risk premium (VRP, Chapter 10C). It fits worst in a strong fall, when the put is exercised and you are left long stock that is underwater.

The failure modes are those of the covered call, because the two have identical payoffs under put-call parity (Chapter 5F). One sizing trap belongs to this entry. Traders see "bounded loss" and size by the credit collected, reasoning "I collected $400, so my risk is $400", rather than by the actual distance to their stop. The right anchor for size is the notional, or the loss at your stop level, not the option premium.

Worked example: A cash-secured put on SPY

Step 1: the trade. You would like to own SPY at $570, and it trades at $580. Sell one 570 put with 30 DTE for $4.00, a credit of $400, and set aside 570×100=$57,000570 \times 100 = \$57{,}000570×100=$57,000 of cash as collateral.

Step 2: above the strike. If SPY closes above $570 at expiry, the put expires worthless and you simply keep the $400.

Step 3: below the strike. If SPY closes at $565, you are assigned 100 shares at $570, which costs $57,000. Net of the $400 premium, the effective purchase price is 570−4=$566570 - 4 = \$566570−4=$566 a share. The shares are worth $565, so the position shows a loss of (566−565)×100=$100(566 - 565) \times 100 = \$100(566−565)×100=$100.

For American retail traders, run this on XSP rather than SPY, to get the Section 1256 60/40 tax treatment (Chapter 2E). The pre-tax arithmetic is identical, while the after-tax result is dramatically better on XSP. XSP puts settle in cash, so an assignment below the strike costs cash instead of delivering shares (Chapter 12H).

6 · Protective put

Protective putAt a glance
LegsOwn 100 shares at cost basis BBB; buy 1 put at strike KKK below the price, for a premium PPP
ViewYou hold stock you cannot or do not want to sell, and want insurance against a defined loss
P&L at expiry(ST−B)+max⁡(K−ST,0)−P(S_T - B) + \max(K - S_T, 0) - P(ST​−B)+max(K−ST​,0)−P per share
Maximum profitUnbounded, less the premium
Maximum loss(B−K)+P(B - K) + P(B−K)+P per share
BreakevenB+PB + PB+P
Greeks at entryDelta + but below +1.0 per share, gamma +, theta −, vega +

A protective put insures shares you hold: below the strike the put pays back every further dollar the shares lose, so the loss stops growing at (B−K)+P(B - K) + P(B−K)+P. By put-call parity, shares plus a put have the payoff of a long call at the same strike plus cash (Chapter 5F).

The protective put fits best when you have an asymmetric concern about a specific event, such as earnings, a Federal Reserve (FOMC) meeting or a geopolitical risk, and want capped downside through it. It fits worst as a continuous overlay, because the cost of rolling puts over long horizons is significant.

Worked example: A protective put on SPY

Step 1: the trade. Hold 100 SPY shares bought at $580, and buy one 560 put with 60 DTE for $6.00, insurance that costs $600 and raises the breakeven to 580+6=$586580 + 6 = \$586580+6=$586.

Step 2: a fall. If SPY falls to $540, the shares lose (580−540)×100(580 - 540) \times 100(580−540)×100, or $4,000. At expiry the put is worth (560−540)×100(560 - 540) \times 100(560−540)×100, or $2,000. The net loss is 4,000−2,000+6004{,}000 - 2{,}000 + 6004,000−2,000+600 dollars, which is $2,600, against $4,000 unhedged.

Step 3: check the cap. The maximum loss is (580−560)+6=$26(580 - 560) + 6 = \$26(580−560)+6=$26 a share, or $2,600, which any close below $560 costs exactly.

Familiar ground

A protective put is prophylaxis, and it is dosed like prophylaxis. Growth-factor support is given with regimens whose risk of febrile neutropenia is high, not with every regimen, because its costs accumulate across the many patients who would not have needed it. A put bought for a known event is the high-risk regimen. A put rolled every month for years pays its premium through all the months that needed nothing.

In practice

All five strategies run in a cash account, with no margin (Chapter 2E). The covered call and the cash-secured put tie up capital dollar for dollar: 100 shares, or the strike times 100 in cash. Size both from that notional, or from the loss at your stop, never from the premium. For an index view in a taxable account, sell the puts on XSP rather than SPY. SPY and single-stock options are American-style, so a short call or put there can be assigned before expiry and deliver shares (Chapter 12H).

When it fails

The two bought strategies fail slowly, as time decay and a fall in implied volatility erode a correct view. The two income strategies fail suddenly: a covered call or a cash-secured put carries nearly the whole downside of the stock, cushioned only by the premium, under 1% of the price in the examples above. The protective put fails by cost, when it is rolled through years that never needed it.

Decision rule
  • Buy calls or puts when implied volatility is cheap and the move you expect exceeds the implied move. When IV is rich, look to Chapter 12C's spreads instead.
  • Sell covered calls or cash-secured puts only on stock you would be content to hold through a large fall, sized from the notional or the loss at your stop.
  • Do not sell a covered call into a view of a strong rally, since it caps the gain you expect.
  • Buy protective puts for a named event, not as a permanent overlay.
Common mistake: sizing a cash-secured put by its credit

A trader who collects $400 of premium and calls that the risk has the wrong number. The risk is the loss at your stop, and at worst the strike less the premium: about $56,600 for the 570 SPY put above. Size from that.

Common mistake: "limited risk" read as "small risk"

A covered call's loss is limited only by the stock's price reaching zero: about $58,000 for 100 SPY shares at $580. It is limited beside a naked short call, whose loss has no bound, but it is not small. The same holds for a cash-secured put, which is the same position under another name (Chapter 5F).

What to carry forward
  • A long call or put risks the premium for a large gain, and breaks even at K+PK + PK+P or K−PK - PK−P. It loses to time, to an IV crush and to a wrong-way move.
  • A covered call and a cash-secured put are one payoff under two names. Each earns a small premium for nearly the whole downside of the stock.
  • A protective put caps the loss on shares at (B−K)+P(B - K) + P(B−K)+P, and is cheapest when bought for a specific event.
  • Size the income strategies from the notional or the loss at the stop, never from the credit.
  • Prefer XSP to SPY for index put-writing in a taxable account.

7 · Check yourself

Question 1

You buy one SPX 6,100 call for 40 points. What are your breakeven, your maximum loss in dollars and your P&L if SPX settles at 6,200?

Answer

The breakeven is 6,100+40=6,1406{,}100 + 40 = 6{,}1406,100+40=6,140, and the most you can lose is 40×$100=$4,00040 \times \$100 = \$4{,}00040×$100=$4,000. At 6,200 the call pays 100×$100=$10,000100 \times \$100 = \$10{,}000100×$100=$10,000, a profit of $6,000.

Question 2

You own 100 shares at $50 and sell a 55 call for $1.20. What are your maximum profit, your breakeven and your maximum loss?

Answer

The maximum profit is (55−50)+1.20=$6.20(55 - 50) + 1.20 = \$6.20(55−50)+1.20=$6.20 a share, or $620. The breakeven is 50−1.20=$48.8050 - 1.20 = \$48.8050−1.20=$48.80. The maximum loss is $48.80\$48.80$48.80 a share, or $4,880, if the stock goes to zero.

Question 3

You sell a 100-strike put for $2.50 on a stock at $104 and are assigned at expiry with the stock at $97. What is your effective purchase price, and your loss on the shares at $97?

Answer

The effective price is 100−2.50=$97.50100 - 2.50 = \$97.50100−2.50=$97.50 a share. At $97 the shares show a loss of (97.50−97)×100=$50(97.50 - 97) \times 100 = \$50(97.50−97)×100=$50.

Question 4

Why can you not run a covered call on SPX, and what is the nearest cash-settled equivalent?

Answer

SPX is a cash-settled index with no shares to own, so there is nothing to cover the call. The nearest equivalent is a cash-secured short call, a naked call against cash, whose loss is unbounded as the index rises.

Question 5

You hold 100 shares bought at $80 and buy a 75 put for $2. What is the most you can lose, and where do you break even?

Answer

The maximum loss is (80−75)+2=$7(80 - 75) + 2 = \$7(80−75)+2=$7 a share, or $700, reached at any price below $75. The breakeven is 80+2=$8280 + 2 = \$8280+2=$82.
