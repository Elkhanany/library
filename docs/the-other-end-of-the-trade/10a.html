Five kinds of edge

How the kind of edge sets its size and its kill rule

Where we are

Chapter 1G sorted every claimed edge by its source: why it pays at all. This chapter sorts the same edges by the job they do in a portfolio. The job decides how large an edge may be, how long to trust it and which rule ends it, and the rest of Part X then takes the edges one at a time.

What you need first
  • The four sources of edge, and how they map roughly onto five categories (Chapter 1G).
  • The Sharpe ratio, its haircut for negative skew, the deflated Sharpe ratio and the stress scenarios S1 to S3 (Chapter 1D).
  • Drawdown, and the gain needed to recover from it (Chapter 1E).
  • Correlation, tail correlation, and how correlation sets the gain from combining two strategies (Chapter 1F).

1 · One table cannot rank every edge

The Edge Book, the source of Part X, went through three versions. The first ranked every edge in a single table, which implied a false comparability. The equity risk premium (ERP) and an OPEX-week tilt, a change of positioning in the week of the monthly options expiration (OPEX), are different kinds of object. They should not compete against each other for the same capital.

The fix is a taxonomy of five categories. It separates what kind of edge something is from how strong the evidence for it is, and both from how much capital it should get. Once you know an edge's source (Chapter 1G) and have scored your conviction in it (Chapter 13B), the category tells you how it fits into a portfolio. Different categories need different sizing rules, different assumptions about how long the edge persists, and different kill criteria.

The key cognitive rule follows: before you can know how to size a strategy and how to kill it, you have to know what kind of edge it is.

In plain terms

An edge's kind tells you its job. A few edges are the engine of a portfolio, paying you for bearing a risk that you hold through bad years. Others lean the engine one way, or change its speed for a week or a quarter at a time. Some are gauges on the dashboard, which you read but never trade. The rest are traps, and learning to know them on sight is half the work.

The taxonomy has five categories, running from the most durable edges to the ones you should never trade:

  1. Foundation premia: the risk premia that anchor the portfolio.
  2. Equity factor tilts: systematic departures from weighting stocks by market capitalization that have produced excess returns.
  3. Structural and tactical overlays: calendar or flow effects that modulate your exposure to the other premia.
  4. Regime filters: variables that size other strategies and are never traded on their own.
  5. Rejected or non-retail edges: those that decayed, were never robust, or can be traded only by institutions.

Each category has its own role and its own operating rules. The table gathers them, and the sections below take each row in turn.

"Portfolio risk" in the table means a share of the portfolio's volatility, not of its dollars. A dollar in a volatile sleeve carries more risk than a dollar in a calm one, so the two shares differ. Chapter 13G shows how to compute each sleeve's share of the risk.

The category matters most when something goes wrong, because it decides what you do next. The example applies the kill rule of one category to an edge from another.

Worked example: A factor's kill rule on a foundation premium

Step 1: the rule fires. A factor tilt is cut by 50% when its 24-month rolling Sharpe ratio turns negative. A rolling Sharpe ratio is recomputed each month over the latest 24 months, so it falls below zero whenever those months lost money on balance. Suppose a $1 million portfolio holds $600,000 of an equity index as its ERP sleeve, and a bear market takes the index down 30%. The sleeve is worth $420,000 and its 24-month Sharpe ratio is negative, so the factor rule sells half of it, $210,000, at the low.

Step 2: the recovery. By Chapter 1E, a 30% loss needs a gain of 0.30/0.70=43%0.30/0.70 = 43\%0.30/0.70=43% to repair. When the index makes that gain, the full sleeve returns to 420,000/0.70=$600,000420{,}000/0.70 = \$600{,}000420,000/0.70=$600,000. The halved sleeve returns to 210,000/0.70=$300,000210{,}000/0.70 = \$300{,}000210,000/0.70=$300,000, which with the $210,000 in cash makes $510,000.

Step 3: the cost. The wrong rule cost $90,000, which is 9% of the portfolio. The rule for a foundation premium says the opposite: do not cut during a drawdown, because the drawdown is the cost of the premium.

The reverse mistake costs money too, because a foundation rule never switches an overlay off. It would keep an OPEX-week tilt running after its effect had diluted, paying trading costs every month for an edge that had gone.

2 · Category A: foundation premia

Foundation premia are compensated risk premia, payments for bearing risk that cannot be diversified away, and four of them anchor the portfolio:

  • The equity risk premium, earned by holding equities (Chapter 10B).
  • The index variance risk premium (VRP), earned by selling volatility on the index (Chapter 10C).
  • Trend following, or time-series momentum (TSMOM), run systematically across asset classes (Chapter 10D).
  • Cross-asset carry, earned by buying the assets that pay the most carry (Chapter 10E).

Almost all foundation premia are risk premia, Source 1 of Chapter 1G, which makes them the most durable category. Their premium exists for structural reasons, and those reasons do not disappear when capital exploits them. Their typical aggregate allocation is 60% to 70% of portfolio risk. Chapter 10E adds the four sleeves together and arrives at about 70% to 80%, a range from a different chapter of the Edge Book that meets this one only at 70%.

Four sizing principles follow from what a foundation premium is:

  • Size each foundation sleeve from its long-run expectation, not from its recent performance.
  • For a short-volatility foundation sleeve such as the VRP, assume the volatility of a stress period rather than a calm one.
  • Assume drawdowns of 35% to 50%, which by Chapter 1E need gains of 54% to 100% to repair, and make sure the portfolio survives them.
  • Do not cut the allocation during a drawdown, because drawdowns are the cost of the premium.
Worked example: Sizing a short-volatility sleeve for stress

Step 1: sized for calm. Suppose you want a put-writing sleeve to add at most 3 percentage points of volatility to the portfolio, counted on its own. A sleeve holding a fraction www of the portfolio at volatility σ\sigmaσ adds wσw\sigmawσ, so at a calm-year volatility of 10% the budget allows w=3%/10%=30%w = 3\%/10\% = 30\%w=3%/10%=30% of the portfolio.

Step 2: sized for stress. If the sleeve's volatility reaches a hypothetical 30% in a stress period, that same 30% of the portfolio adds 0.30×30%=90.30 \times 30\% = 90.30×30%=9 points, three times the budget. Sized for the stress, the budget allows only 3%/30%=10%3\%/30\% = 10\%3%/30%=10% of the portfolio.

The persistence of foundation premia is high. These edges are documented across centuries, and they survive under different regulatory regimes. The ERP has been positive in 35 of 35 markets in the Dimson-Marsh-Staunton database. The VRP has been positive in roughly 80% of months for more than 30 years, and trend has been positive in every decade since 1880.

Their decay risk is limited: a premium compresses when investors' demand for the risk behind it shifts, but it does not go to zero. Higher equity allocations, for example, have compressed the forward ERP, the premium that equities are expected to pay from here on. So foundation premia are never switched off. They are sized down in adverse regimes, under the specific kill criteria of Chapter 13G.

3 · Category B: equity factor tilts

Factor tilts are systematic departures from market-capitalization weighting that have produced positive long-run excess returns within equity markets. A cap-weighted index such as the S&P 500 holds each company in proportion to its market value. A tilt leans away from those weights toward stocks with a chosen trait, and Chapter 10F examines the five tilts of this category:

  • Quality or profitability, measured by the RMW ("robust minus weak") and QMJ ("quality minus junk") factors: long the high-quality stocks.
  • Momentum, cross-sectional (CSMOM): long the recent winners, short the recent losers.
  • Value, measured by the HML ("high minus low") factor: long the cheap stocks, short the expensive ones.
  • Low volatility, or betting against beta (BAB): long the low-beta stocks, short the high-beta ones.
  • Size conditional on quality: long the small companies of high quality.

By source the tilts are mixed: most are behavioral (Source 2), with risk-premium and institutional parts. Quality is the most risk-premium-flavored of them, and momentum the most behavioral. Tilts live within the equity sleeve, at a typical 15% to 30% of it, expressed through factor ETFs, exchange-traded funds that track a factor. On a $550,000 equity sleeve, a 20% tilt puts $110,000 into factor ETFs and leaves $440,000 in the broad index, so the total equity exposure does not change. Chapter 10F, building the tilt factor by factor, arrives at 25% to 40%, and the two ranges share only 25% to 30%.

Four sizing principles guard against the way factors fail:

  • Tilt rather than concentrate: do not replace SPX with pure factor ETFs.
  • Diversify across factors, since quality, momentum and low volatility together do better than any single factor.
  • Plan for drawdowns that last several years.
  • Do not try to time the rotation from one factor to another.

The persistence of tilts is medium, because behavioral edges decay after publication. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023) documented a decline of about 47% after publication. Quality has held up best, while size and naive value have struggled. The decay risk is therefore real: some factors have already decayed substantially, among them size on its own and naive value from 2010 to 2020. Others, such as quality and momentum, have decayed less but compressed.

Three kill criteria govern a tilt, and the first is the one the earlier example misapplied:

  • If the 24-month rolling Sharpe ratio falls below 0, cut the allocation by 50%.
  • If the vehicle's tracking error to the academic factor exceeds 4% annualized, switch vehicles. By the square-root-of-time rule of Chapter 1A, that is about 4%/12=1.15%4\%/\sqrt{12} = 1.15\%4%/12​=1.15% a month.
  • Multi-decade drawdowns are tolerable if you are committed to the long run. If you would panic and exit during one, do not allocate at all.

4 · Category C: structural and tactical overlays

Overlays are calendar or flow effects that modulate exposure to other premia rather than serving as strategies of their own. Chapter 10G examines the five overlays of this category:

  • The Cieslak FOMC even-week cycle: the equity premium is concentrated in the even weeks of the cycle between meetings of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).
  • End-of-quarter (EOQ) rebalancing flow: pension funds rebalancing to their targets create predictable flow, as in the worked example of Chapter 1G.
  • The Halloween effect, or "sell in May": a seasonal pattern in equity returns.
  • The OPEX-week tilt: effects in the week of the monthly options expiration.
  • The turn-of-the-month effect: the timing of cash flows around the end of each month.

By source, overlays are mostly institutional frictions (Source 3), with some behavioral effects (Source 2). Halloween is a mystery: the pattern persists, but no clean mechanism explains it.

How this book files OPEX and Halloween

The Edge Book files two of these effects differently in different chapters. Its chapter on sources lists seasonality among the behavioral edges and OPEX dynamics under microstructure. Its taxonomy calls the overlays mostly institutional with some behavioral. Its chapter on overlays files OPEX as microstructure and institutional, and finds no clean mechanism for Halloween.

This book uses one classification throughout, in which both effects are overlays by category. By source, the OPEX-week tilt is microstructure with an institutional part: dealers hedge into expiry, and option-related rebalancing concentrates around it. The Halloween effect is filed with the behavioral seasonality effects, as Chapter 1G files it. That filing stays provisional until someone finds a mechanism, and Chapter 10G examines both effects in full.

Overlays sit on top of existing positions. Their typical aggregate allocation is 10% to 15% of portfolio risk as modulation, not as separate sleeves, because they scale equity exposure up or down rather than run strategies of their own. Three sizing principles follow from that role:

  • Express an overlay through instruments you already hold, by rotating ETFs or changing the size of a futures position, rather than through a new strategy.
  • Do not add transaction costs that eat the small premium.
  • Most overlays add 10 to 30 bps of Sharpe, in the Edge Book's phrase, so check that implementing one costs well below that.

The last principle compares a gain in Sharpe ratio with a cost in money, so the gain has to be turned into return first. The Sharpe ratio is SR=μ/σp\text{SR} = \mu/\sigma_pSR=μ/σp​, where μ\muμ is the portfolio's expected return above the risk-free rate and σp\sigma_pσp​ is its volatility. At a fixed volatility, a rise of ΔSR\Delta\text{SR}ΔSR in the Sharpe ratio is therefore a rise in expected excess return of

Δμ=ΔSR×σp \Delta\mu = \Delta\text{SR} \times \sigma_p Δμ=ΔSR×σp​ (10A.1)
Worked example: An overlay against its costs

Step 1: the gain. Suppose an overlay lifts the Sharpe ratio of a portfolio running at 12% volatility by a hypothetical 0.02. By (10A.1), that is 0.02×12%=0.24%0.02 \times 12\% = 0.24\%0.02×12%=0.24% a year, or 24 bps.

Step 2: the cost. Suppose it moves $200,000 of a $1 million portfolio out and back 6 times a year, at 5 bps each way. Each round trip costs 10 bps of $200,000, which is $200, so six of them cost $1,200 or 12 bps of the portfolio.

Step 3: the verdict. Costs take half of the gain. At 12 round trips a year they would reach $2,400, the whole 24 bps, and the overlay would earn nothing.

Read literally, a basis point of Sharpe ratio is 0.0001, so 10 to 30 bps would be 0.001 to 0.003. Chapter 10G adds the five overlays together and puts their combined gain at 0.10 to 0.30 of Sharpe ratio if all are implemented well. Whichever scale is meant, (10A.1) turns the gain into return, the only number a cost can be compared with.

The persistence of overlays varies with how well their mechanism is understood. The FOMC cycle and EOQ rebalancing have clear mechanisms and seem persistent. OPEX and Halloween show empirical patterns without clear mechanisms, so their persistence is uncertain. Their decay risk is real: the S&P 500 inclusion effect has already decayed from 7% to under 1%, and the OPEX effect may be diluting as weekly and 0DTE options spread. Track every overlay for evidence of decay, under three kill criteria:

  • If the effect underperforms its baseline by more than 50% over 4 cycles, suspend it. The baseline is what the evidence leads you to expect: an overlay expected to earn 0.3% a quarter has a baseline of 1.2% over 4 quarters, so earning less than 0.6% triggers the rule.
  • If the implementation costs exceed the expected edge, simplify the overlay or stop it.
  • If the mechanism changes, through regulation or through the structure of a product, reassess the overlay.

5 · Category D: regime filters

A regime filter is a variable used to size exposure to other strategies up or down. It is not a standalone edge, and Chapter 10H examines five of them:

  • The VIX term structure, whether it is in contango or in backwardation (Chapter 9A).
  • Realized against implied volatility, the gap behind the VRP (Chapter 7A).
  • The liquidity regime, read from the TED spread, the MOVE index and corporate credit spreads, which gauge strain in funding and bond markets (Chapter 10E).
  • The sign of dealer gamma, read at low confidence (Chapter 8D).
  • Breadth and internals, which measure how many stocks take part in a move.

Filters are informational signals about the regime, not edges to trade. The "edge" they create takes the form of avoided losses on other strategies rather than direct returns. So a filter gets zero direct allocation, and its whole use is to modulate other allocations under three principles:

  • Define the rule explicitly, for example: "if the VIX divided by the VIX3M exceeds 1.05 for 3 days, halt new put sales".
  • Phase changes in and out over several days to avoid whipsaw, being pushed in and out repeatedly by a signal that flickers across its threshold.
  • Do not trade the filter itself: for example, do not buy VXX, an exchange-traded product (ETP) that holds VIX futures, because the VIX is in contango. VXX has lost 99.84% of its value to roll decay.
Worked example: Why a long VIX-futures fund bleeds in contango

Step 1: the roll. A fund that holds VIX futures must sell each contract before it expires and buy the next one. In contango each contract trades above the spot VIX, and if the VIX stays where it is, the contract's price drifts down toward spot as expiry approaches: a negative roll yield (Chapter 9A).

Step 2: a month and a year of it. Suppose the VIX does not move, and each contract the fund buys loses a hypothetical 1% of its price over the month as it drifts toward spot. Twelve such months leave 0.9912=0.8860.99^{12} = 0.8860.9912=0.886 of the fund's value, a loss of about 11% a year, and ten such years leave 0.88610=0.300.886^{10} = 0.300.88610=0.30.

Step 3: the long run. Contango is the usual shape of the VIX term structure (Chapter 5E), so the drag is the fund's normal state, and each spike fades as the curve returns to contango. VXX's loss of 99.84% leaves $16 of every $10,000 invested.

The persistence of filters is high at the level of information: the fact that the VIX term structure backwardates in stress is structural and persistent, even though specific threshold rules may need recalibrating. So the decay risk is low for the underlying signal but meaningful for particular rules, and a filter has two kill criteria:

  • If the rule produces persistent false positives, with more than 50% of its "stress" calls followed by no stress, recalibrate it.
  • If the underlying signal becomes unavailable or unreliable, switch to another source.
Worked example: A filter's false alarms

Step 1: the setting. Suppose 1 month in 10 is a stress month, in which a put-writing sleeve loses 8%, and every other month earns it 1%. A filter flags 80% of the stress months in advance, and it also flags 10% of the calm months by mistake.

Step 2: count 100 months. Of the 10 stress months the filter flags 8, and of the 90 calm months it flags 9. Only 8 of its 17 flags are followed by stress, which is 47%, below the 50% line of the kill rule.

Step 3: the verdict. Standing aside on the 17 flags avoids 8 losses of 8%, or 64 points of sleeve return, and gives up 9 calm months at 1%, or 9 points. Even with most of its calls wrong, the filter is worth 55 points over the 100 months. So the kill rule does not say drop it: it says recalibrate it, because a hit rate of 47% suggests a threshold set too loose.

Familiar ground

A regime filter's hit rate is a positive predictive value. The filter above is a screening test with 80% sensitivity and 90% specificity, applied to a condition with 10% prevalence, so its positive predictive value is 8/17=47%8/17 = 47\%8/17=47%. Stress is rare, and low prevalence drags the predictive value down however good the test is. The kill rule's 50% line is a floor on that value, and recalibrating the filter is moving the test's cut-off.

The figure counts the same hundred months, one square each. Lower the share of stress months and watch the hit rate fall, while the net value stays positive.

6 · Category E: rejected or non-retail

An edge is rejected because it has decayed, because its evidence was never robust, or because it is real but open only to institutions. Chapter 10I examines the seven examples of this category, each with the evidence that rejects it:

  • Pre-FOMC announcement drift has decayed. Kurov, Halova, Wolfe and Gilbert (2021) document that it disappeared after 2015, because of reforms to the Federal Reserve's forward guidance.
  • The S&P 500 inclusion premium has decayed. Greenwood and Sammon (2024) document an effect under 1% after 2010, against 7% or more in the 1990s.
  • Naive single-name earnings short volatility is institutional-only and carries high tail risk. Adams and Neururer (2020) show a negative mean despite a positive median. An institutional cross-sectional version exists (Cao, Han, Tong and Zhan 2022), but it needs infrastructure covering 200 names.
  • GEX-level scalping is driven by narrative and has not validated since 2022. Gamma exposure (GEX) estimates the dealers' net gamma, strike by strike (Chapter 8A), and a Cboe study of 2025 estimates that 0DTE hedging by market makers is about 0.2% of liquidity. That study measures same-day hedging only, so using it against levels built mostly from longer-dated open interest extends the measurement.
  • Systematic iron condors in the style of Cboe's CNDR index empirically underperform massively. The CNDR returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024, against about 5,500% for the SPX Total Return index (SPX TR), compound rates of about 5.5% and 11% a year (Chapter 1D).
  • A 1-year put spread combined with a LEAP, a listed option with a year or more to expiry, is leverage with extra steps rather than edge. A worked example in Chapter 13E shows it to be economically equivalent to about 1.4 times SPX exposure, with worse path properties (Chapter 1E).
  • VIX ETPs, long or short, have catastrophic tails. VXX has lost 99.84% to roll decay. A short position in VIX futures held for its roll yield, through products such as XIV and SVXY, carries the opposite danger: XIV was terminated after losing 96% in a day in 2018.

Most retail trading content focuses on Category E strategies. They are vivid, with specific narratives and dramatic claims, and easy to backtest, because their data is clean. They are also frequently traded, with high volume in trading communities. But they do not survive an evaluation by evidence tier.

Knowing what not to trade is half the battle, and a textbook should teach that negative space explicitly. With an allocation of zero, the category's members are documented for reference and to inoculate you against narrative-driven retail bait.

Worked example: A positive median with a negative mean

Step 1: ten trades. Suppose you sell options on ten companies before their earnings reports. Seven reports pass quietly, and each of those trades makes $100. Three bring large moves, and each of those trades loses $400.

Step 2: median and mean. Sorted, the results are three losses and then seven gains, so the median trade is a $100 gain while the mean is (7×100−3×400)/10=−$50(7 \times 100 - 3 \times 400)/10 = -\$50(7×100−3×400)/10=−$50 a trade. The rare large losses outweigh the common small gains, so the strategy loses money although its typical trade makes money.

Some Category E items still have legitimate informational uses, even though the trade is rejected:

  • The directional sign of GEX, used as low-confidence regime context and never as a set of specific levels.
  • Awareness of the earnings calendar, used for risk management, so that you are not caught holding bought options into a report.
  • The VIX term structure, used as a filter in Category D and never as a trade.

The trades themselves stay rejected: the information is useful, and the implementations are not.

Where this book's two traditions disagree

Two items on the rejected list are taught elsewhere in this book, in the chapters drawn from the Dealer Mechanics Lab:

  • Trading GEX levels: Chapter 8C builds the gamma exposure profile and its walls. The market dashboard of Chapter 11A reads the call wall above spot as potential resistance and the put wall below as potential support. The conviction score of Chapter 13B asks whether dealer positioning supports a trade's path. The Edge Book gives three reasons for caution: vendor models disagree, 0DTE changed who holds what, and the measured hedging flow is small. Both sides agree that scalping level by level is not supported (Chapter 13F).
  • Iron condors: Chapter 12C teaches the condor for when you expect realized volatility to come in below implied, and Chapter 5E counts condor writing among the short-premium strategies with positive long-run returns. The Edge Book rejects running condors systematically on the CNDR record (Chapters 1D and 10I). Chapter 12C reports that record too, so the open question is whether a condor chosen for the regime escapes it.

A third disagreement concerns the VRP, which both traditions keep. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls it the most important source of edge available to retail traders (Chapters 4C and 5E). The Edge Book is more guarded about what it is worth to a retail account (Chapters 1D and 10C).

8 · Three questions, three tables

The Edge Book's first version ranked every edge in one table. Its second separated three independent questions, and its third, the textbook behind Part X, keeps them apart, one table to a question:

TableThe questionHow it is answered
1. Evidence qualityWhat does the academic literature say?By tier: T1 is peer-reviewed, replicated and multi-decade; T2 is strongly empirical but sensitive to method; T3 is thin or decayed
2. Retail implementation viabilityCan a retail account implement it, and at what Sharpe ratio?By the realistic user-account Sharpe ratio after the four-layer haircut of Chapter 13A
3. Crash behaviorHow does it behave in 2008, March 2020 and 2022?By its tail beta, and whether it diversifies in stress or moves with everything else that sells premium

The three questions have different answers for different strategies. The index VRP has T1 evidence and an acceptable retail implementation, with a user-account Sharpe ratio of about 0.30. Its tail beta of about 1.0 in the left tail still makes it a problem for portfolio construction. Trend following has a middling Sharpe ratio but uniquely diversifying behavior, so its value to a portfolio sits in the third table, not the second (Chapter 10D). Keep all three answers in view: a single ranked number would let a strong answer to one question hide a failing answer to another.

9 · Using the taxonomy

When you evaluate a candidate move, take four steps in order. These four steps are the full pipeline:

  1. Source first: which of the four sources is it (Chapter 1G)?
  2. Category second: where in the taxonomy does it fit?
  3. Conviction third: how does the move score on the conviction score of Chapter 13B?
  4. Implementation fourth: which vehicle, sizing and kill rule does its category call for?

Each category has its own style of implementation. A foundation premium gets the foundation style: long-term, sized for stress and executed mechanically. An overlay gets the overlay style of ETF rotation or a simple change of size, with no derivatives unless your capital justifies them. A filter gets the filter style: never traded directly, and used only to size other things. The dossiers of Chapters 10B to 10I fill the taxonomy edge by edge, and the worked examples of Chapter 13E put the whole pipeline to work.

Common mistake: mixing categories

Treating an edge as a member of the wrong category is the most common operational error. Four examples show the pattern, each with its cost:

  • Treating GEX scalping, a rejected edge, as a foundation sleeve leads to systematic destruction of capital.
  • Treating end-of-quarter rebalancing, an overlay, as a primary source of income leads to overconfidence in a small effect.
  • Treating the VIX term structure, a filter, as a trade through VIX ETPs leads to catastrophic roll decay.
  • Treating value, a factor, as a market-timing signal rather than a structural tilt leads to exits during its inevitable drawdown.

The taxonomy exists to prevent these mismatches.

A second common mistake is to stack several edges in one sleeve and expect their Sharpe ratios to add. Chapter 1F showed how correlation governs the gain from combining two strategies, and the same algebra covers any number of them.

Worked example: Four overlays, stacked

Step 1: the hope. Suppose you run four overlays, each with a hypothetical Sharpe ratio of 0.2, in the belief that they are independent. With ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0, (10A.3) gives a stacked Sharpe ratio of 0.2×4=0.400.2 \times \sqrt{4} = 0.400.2×4​=0.40.

Common mistake: stacking edges in one sleeve

The belief is that with several edges, you can stack them in one sleeve for a better Sharpe ratio. Some edges combine; many do not. Foundation premia such as the ERP, the VRP and trend combine well, because the risk premia beneath them are partly independent. Factor tilts within equity, such as quality with momentum, combine modestly. Mixing categories usually produces correlated bets disguised as diversification, whether through a regime filter used as a trade or through many overlays stacked in the belief that they are independent. Evaluate sleeves as sleeves, with explicit attention to their correlation in stress, the third of the three tables.

In practice

Keep a written register of your edges. For each position, record its source, its category, its conviction score, its vehicle, its size and its kill rule, with a date for the next review. The register makes a category mistake visible before it costs money: an overlay with a sleeve of its own, a filter with an allocation, or a rejected strategy with any position at all. When trading content offers a new edge, fill in the register's line for it before you read the sales pitch a second time.

When it fails

The taxonomy is a classification, and a classification can be wrong or can go stale. An overlay's mechanism can vanish, as most of the S&P 500 inclusion effect did. A foundation premium can compress, as higher equity allocations have compressed the forward ERP. And a shared category is no promise of diversification: the ERP and the VRP are both foundation premia, yet they lose in the same crashes. Revisit an edge's category whenever the reason it pays has changed.

Decision rule

Before you allocate to any edge, name its category, then follow that category's rules:

  • Foundation premium: allocate for the long run, sized for stress and for drawdowns of 35% to 50%. Size it down in adverse regimes, but do not cut it during a drawdown.
  • Factor tilt: hold 15% to 30% of the equity sleeve, spread across factors, and cut it by 50% if its 24-month rolling Sharpe ratio turns negative.
  • Overlay: adjust existing exposure only if the cost is well below the gain, and suspend it after 4 cycles more than 50% below its baseline.
  • Regime filter: allocate nothing to it, and write it instead as an explicit rule for sizing something else, recalibrated if more than half its stress calls fail.
  • Rejected edge: do not trade it, though you may use its information.
What to carry forward
  • Classify every edge by its category as well as its source, because the category sets its size, how long you trust it and the rule that ends it.
  • Foundation premia anchor 60% to 70% of portfolio risk and are sized down in bad regimes rather than switched off. Factor tilts sit inside the equity sleeve, overlays modulate existing exposure, and filters get no capital.
  • The rejected category is half the value of the taxonomy: vivid, easily backtested strategies that fail on evidence, on retail viability or on crash behavior.
  • Keep evidence, retail viability and crash behavior as three separate answers, because one ranked number hides a failure in any of them.
  • Stacked edges combine only as far as their correlation allows, so correlated overlays add far less than their number suggests.

10 · Check yourself

Question 1

Name the five categories. Which is the most durable, and which carries the most decay risk?

Answer

They are foundation premia, equity factor tilts, structural and tactical overlays, regime filters, and rejected or non-retail edges. Foundation premia are the most durable, because they are risk premia and exist as long as the risk is real. Of the categories still traded, factor tilts carry the most decay risk. They are mostly behavioral, and behavioral edges decay after publication, by about 47% in Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023). Overlays carry a real decay risk of a different kind, since a change in the rules can end them, as it ended most of the S&P 500 inclusion effect.

Question 2

Why do risk premia usually become foundation sleeves?

Answer

A risk premium is a payment for risk that cannot be diversified away. More capital can compress it, but cannot remove it while the risk remains. That durability, and the large capacity that comes with it, suits a sleeve that is held for decades and sized for its crashes. Behavioral edges decay, institutional edges depend on a constraint that can change, and microstructure edges break at size.

Question 3

Why are regime filters not standalone allocations? A filter flags 20 months as stress, and 8 of them turn out to be stress months. What does its kill rule say?

Answer

A filter carries information about the regime rather than a return of its own. Its value is the losses it avoids on other strategies, and trading the filter itself can be ruinous: VXX, a trade on the VIX, has lost 99.84% to roll decay. This filter's hit rate is 8/20=40%8/20 = 40\%8/20=40%, so more than 50% of its stress calls had no follow-through. The rule says recalibrate it, not abandon it.

Question 4

Classify three edges by source and by category: buying SPX at the put wall and selling it at the call wall; holding low-beta stocks against high-beta ones; and selling at-the-money XSP puts every month. Is each pairing consistent?

Answer

The first is GEX-level scalping, microstructure by source and rejected by category. The second is betting against beta, institutional by source and a factor tilt by category, one of the known exceptions to the rough mapping. The third harvests the variance risk premium, a risk premium by source and a foundation sleeve by category. All three pairings are consistent with the matrix. A red flag would be a microstructure edge offered as a foundation sleeve.

Question 5

Why does the taxonomy use three separate ranking tables instead of one?

Answer

Evidence quality, retail implementation viability and crash behavior are separate questions with different answers. The index VRP has T1 evidence and a workable retail Sharpe ratio of about 0.30, yet a tail beta of about 1.0 in the left tail. Trend following has a middling Sharpe ratio but diversifies in stress. One ranked number would let a strong answer hide a failing one.

Question 6

Pick a rejected strategy, and state the evidence that rejects it.

Answer

Systematic iron condors are rejected on the record of Cboe's CNDR index, which returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024. The SPX Total Return index returned about 5,500% over the same years, and the two are compound rates of about 5.5% and 11% a year. The structure caps each loss but keeps the negative skew, and a few breaks of its wings erase the premium. Chapter 12C, which teaches the condor, reports the same underperformance.
