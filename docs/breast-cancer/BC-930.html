Why an intracranial response rate belongs to its cohort before it belongs to its drug.

1 · Scope, and why the central nervous system is treated as a compartment

This chapter covers parenchymal brain metastases and leptomeningeal disease, together with the local therapy, the systemic therapy, the delivery argument that sits under both, and the endpoints these trials report.

The biology of organ tropism is in Metastatic dissemination and organ tropism, the brain microenvironment in Spatial organization and what it adds and radiation technique in Radiation oncology. The HER2-directed agents themselves are in Metastatic therapy from first line onwards, where their pharmacology is set out rather than repeated here.

The compartment is a population defined by anatomy rather than by tumour biology, with two consequences that shape everything below.

The first is that most of the randomised evidence was generated by radiation oncology in brain metastases from solid tumours generally. A breast-specific estimate from those trials is a subgroup at best.

The second is that the registration trials of the modern systemic agents excluded untreated or progressing brain metastases by protocol. The claim that a large molecule cannot reach the compartment was therefore assumed rather than tested. Nobody enrolled the patients who would have tested it.

The breast-specific randomised record is about half a dozen trials wide. Three of them held brain radiotherapy constant and varied what was given alongside it: A-PLUS, WBRT with or without temozolomide and NRG Oncology–KROG / RTOG 1119. Of the rest, ATTAIN varied the systemic drug, DEPOSEIN varied the route into the compartment, and Proton CSI versus photon IFRT varied the irradiated field. Not one randomised a modern brain-active systemic agent against local therapy. Everything else in this chapter is single-arm. Outside HER2-positive disease the record is thinner still. U-BOMB and ABC study are the first prospective cohorts of any size.

One warning belongs at the front rather than at the back. Intracranial response rates from these studies are not comparable with one another, because the ranking tracks who was let in at least as closely as it tracks what they were given. Reading an intracranial response rate, and what the cohort contributes to it is where that is argued, and every section between here and there depends on it.

2 · Local therapy and the cognitive cost of controlling the compartment

The randomised base for this decision is larger than for anything else in the chapter, though it is almost entirely borrowed. These trials enrolled brain metastases from solid tumours and were powered on that mixed population.

The central nervous system as a compartment

When a limited number of brain metastases has already been treated focally, does adding whole-brain radiotherapy earn what it costs?

Rationale
Deposits exist beyond what MRI resolves. Irradiating the whole brain should therefore postpone the appearance of new lesions and the neurological death that follows them. The hippocampus and the rest of the normal brain take the same dose as the deposits, so whatever is bought in intracranial control is paid for in cognition. That trade has to be measured rather than assumed.
Experiment
Randomised comparisons that held the focal treatment constant and varied the whole-brain component. Whole-brain radiotherapy against observation after complete resection or radiosurgery. Radiosurgery alone against radiosurgery plus whole-brain treatment, with a cognitive primary endpoint. Cavity radiosurgery against whole-brain treatment after resection. Cavity radiosurgery against observation after resection. Whole-brain treatment with or without a radiosurgical boost, with survival as the primary endpoint. Alongside these, a prospective cohort asked whether the number of lesions treated with radiosurgery alone changes survival.
Finding
Whole-brain radiotherapy lowers relapse at the treated site and at new sites. It lengthens neither survival nor the time to loss of performance status. It worsens cognition against focal treatment alone, whether it follows resection or radiosurgery. Adding a radiosurgical boost to whole-brain treatment did not lengthen survival in the trial population as a whole. Survival after radiosurgery alone did not fall away as the number of metastases treated rose, across the range the cohort covered. Leaving the resection cavity untreated is a different question. It costs local control. The case is therefore against irradiating the whole brain rather than against irradiating anything.
Limitation
Every one of these studies enrolled brain metastases from solid tumours generally and was powered on that mixed population, so a breast-specific estimate is a subgroup at best. Cognitive deterioration is a scoring convention rather than a clinical event. One trial called it a decline beyond a set margin on any single test in a battery. Another used a reliable change index. Both scored only the patients still well enough to be tested, which is an increasingly selected group as the disease progresses. The survival advantage for a radiosurgical boost lives in a single-lesion subgroup on univariate analysis of a trial whose survival endpoint was not met in the population as a whole. The lesion-count comparison used to justify radiosurgery for several metastases comes from a prospective observational cohort compared against itself rather than from a randomisation.

If whole-brain radiotherapy has to be given, can its cognitive cost be engineered down?

Rationale
The memory decline that follows whole-brain treatment is attributed to the dose delivered to the hippocampal stem cell niche, and to a vascular and glutamatergic injury that a receptor antagonist might blunt. Neither mechanism requires the dose to the metastases to change, so both are modifiable without giving up intracranial control.
Experiment
One randomised trial gave memantine or placebo through and after whole-brain radiotherapy, with delayed recall at a fixed timepoint as the primary measure. A later trial held memantine constant in both arms and varied the plan instead, conformally avoiding the hippocampi against conventional whole-brain treatment, with time to cognitive failure as the primary measure.
Finding
Avoiding the hippocampi lowers the risk of cognitive failure against conventional whole-brain treatment given on the same memantine backbone. Survival and intracranial progression were unchanged. The memantine trial favoured the drug on its own primary measure of delayed recall without crossing its threshold for significance. A secondary measure, the time to cognitive decline, did favour it.
Limitation
The memantine result is an attrition failure rather than a demonstration of no effect. Only a minority of those randomised were analysable at the primary timepoint, and the report states its own power as far below what the design called for. That matters for how the later trial reads. Its control arm already contained memantine on evidence that never met its own threshold, so the avoidance question was asked on a backbone the field had adopted rather than proved. Both trials scored cognition as a change on a test battery against a statistical rule, which is a measurement convention rather than a clinical event. Neither enrolled breast cancer specifically, and the hippocampal-avoidance trial further required that no metastasis sit in the region being spared.

Randomised trials of how much of the brain to irradiate, and of whether the cognitive cost of irradiating all of it can be engineered down. All of them enrolled brain metastases from solid tumours rather than from breast cancer alone.

Three of those trials randomised whether the whole brain was irradiated at all. They agree on two things. Whole-brain radiotherapy lowers relapse at treated sites and at new ones. It lengthens neither survival nor the time to loss of performance status. The decision therefore turns entirely on what the control costs.

Where they differ is in what each set out to measure, and the field quotes them as though they had all measured cognition. EORTC 22952-26001 made time to performance-status deterioration its primary endpoint and scored no cognitive battery. NCCTG N0574 made cognition primary at three months. NCCTG N107C / CEC.3 paired cognitive-deterioration-free survival with overall survival as co-primary endpoints. Mahajan postoperative SRS trial asked only about local control in the resection cavity. Four questions, four designs, one conclusion attributed to all of them.

Cognition here is a scoring convention rather than a clinical event. The trials that made it primary did not use one convention. NCCTG N0574 called deterioration a decline beyond a set margin on any single test in a battery. NRG CC001 used a reliable change index. Both scored only the patients still well enough to be tested, which attrition selects unevenly across arms as the disease progresses. None of that overturns the direction of the finding. It does mean the magnitude belongs to the instrument as much as to the brain.

The decision rests on the two trials that randomised whole-brain treatment against what a clinic would otherwise do, which is focal treatment and an MRI. NCCTG N107C / CEC.3 carries the same message from the other side of the operation. Mahajan postoperative SRS trial is the guard rail. Leaving the resection cavity untreated is a different question with a different answer, because it costs local control. The case here is against irradiating the whole brain, not against irradiating anything.

Two rows point the other way, and neither is what it is usually taken for. RTOG 9508 added a radiosurgical boost to whole-brain treatment and missed its survival endpoint in the population as a whole. The survival advantage quoted from it lives in a single-lesion subgroup on univariate analysis. JLGK0901 is not a randomised comparison at all. It is a prospective cohort given radiosurgery alone and compared against itself across lesion-count strata. It is the evidence usually cited for treating five to ten metastases focally. A cohort compared with itself cannot say what whole-brain treatment would have done to the same patients.

The cost can be lowered without giving up intracranial control. NRG CC001 conformally avoided the hippocampi and lowered cognitive failure against a conventional plan delivering the same dose to the metastases. Both arms received memantine. RTOG 0614, the trial that put memantine there, never crossed its own threshold on its primary measure of delayed recall. Only a minority of those randomised were analysable at that timepoint, and the report states its own power as far below what the design called for. Its failure was attrition rather than a demonstration of no effect. The avoidance question was therefore asked on a backbone the field had adopted rather than proved, which is worth knowing before quoting either result.

What the table cannot show is the patient this chapter is now about. Median follow-up in these trials runs to months, in populations where most deaths were extracranial. A woman with HER2-positive disease and years of systemic control ahead of her sits outside the range they sampled. She is the one who lives long enough to pay the cognitive bill in full. No trial here stratified by subtype. None was run in an era with brain-active systemic therapy to defer to.

In practice

For a limited number of metastases amenable to focal treatment, treat focally and follow with MRI. Do not add whole-brain radiotherapy by default.

Where whole-brain radiotherapy is unavoidable, ask for hippocampal avoidance unless a metastasis sits in the region to be spared.

Say the trade out loud. Whole-brain treatment buys intracranial control, costs memory, and does not buy time.

Set the expected survival before choosing, because survival after a breast cancer brain metastasis varies several-fold with performance status, subtype, lesion number and extracranial disease9. A patient with months and a patient with years want opposite sides of the same trade.

Do not abandon an effective systemic regimen for an isolated intracranial progression that focal treatment can handle Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease.

3 · Adding a systemic drug to brain radiotherapy

The rationale is one of the better ones in this chapter. Radiation and the drug act on the same lesion at the same time. The barrier around that lesion is already disrupted by the tumour, and by the radiation itself.

Does adding a systemic drug to brain radiotherapy improve intracranial control?

Rationale
Radiation and a drug act on the same lesion at the same time. A drug that reaches the brain at all should reach it best while the barrier is disrupted by the tumour and by the radiation itself. Radiosensitisation would also let the delivered dose do more without raising it.
Experiment
Randomised trials that held brain radiotherapy constant and added a drug. Concurrent lapatinib in HER2-positive disease. Concurrent temozolomide. Induction bevacizumab with etoposide and cisplatin given before whole-brain treatment rather than with it. Alongside those, a single-arm study paired radiosurgery with a checkpoint antibody to ask whether the combination was safe rather than whether it worked, and a protocol pairing a brain-penetrant HER2 inhibitor with radiosurgery sits on the record with no outcomes.
Finding
No randomised addition improved intracranial response. Concurrent lapatinib produced no complete responses at the radiological timepoint and no gain in objective response. Temozolomide did not raise response above whole-brain treatment alone. The induction arm separated from radiotherapy alone on brain-specific progression only against a significance level the trial had set unusually permissive. Its early brain response was no better than the control arm's.
Limitation
The induction trial declared its result against a prespecified alpha far looser than convention. Its interval for brain progression or death included no difference. It is therefore a signal-seeking result rather than a positive trial. The response endpoints in these trials were read on MRI at a fixed early timepoint, where radiation change and residual tumour are hard to separate, which is a measurement problem rather than a biological one. The radiosurgery and checkpoint study was small, single-arm and sized for safety, and the only report on its record is a long-term follow-up rather than a primary analysis, so its local control has no comparator. The tucatinib and radiosurgery study on the record is a protocol with no outcomes, and it asserts nothing about efficacy.

Does giving a HER2-directed drug concurrently with brain radiotherapy improve intracranial control over radiotherapy alone?

Rationale
The compartment is being irradiated in any case. A HER2 inhibitor that reaches it could act as a radiosensitiser. That would buy local control without adding a modality and without waiting for the drug to work on its own.
Experiment
Whole brain radiotherapy or radiosurgery was randomised with or without concurrent lapatinib in HER2-positive brain metastases. Entry required at least one measurable, previously unirradiated lesion. Intracranial complete response at an early fixed timepoint was the primary endpoint. A dose-finding study is now testing the same idea with a more brain-penetrant inhibitor alongside radiosurgery.
Finding
Concurrent lapatinib did not improve intracranial complete response over radiotherapy alone. Objective response at the same timepoint was not improved either. The complete responses that did occur were in the arm given radiotherapy alone. Higher-grade toxicity was commoner with lapatinib added than with radiotherapy alone.
Limitation
A complete response rate at an early fixed timepoint is a radiographic surrogate chosen because it reads out quickly. It is not a measure of how long the compartment stays controlled. It is also the endpoint most exposed to post-radiotherapy imaging change, where treatment effect and residual tumour look alike. Lapatinib is the weakest brain-penetrant member of its class. The same class given without radiotherapy did change intracranial outcomes, which makes this evidence against one drug on one schedule rather than against concurrency. Entry required an unirradiated measurable lesion. The trial therefore says nothing about adding a drug to re-irradiation. The study that would test the concept with a better-penetrating drug is a dose-finding protocol with no outcomes on the record. A maximum tolerated dose cannot settle efficacy whatever it eventually reports.

Can checkpoint blockade work inside the central nervous system?

Rationale
The brain was long described as immunologically privileged, which predicted that checkpoint blockade would fail there. Melanoma and lung cancer then produced durable intracranial responses. If the privilege argument is wrong, the compartment is open to an agent whose effect does not depend on the drug reaching every cell.
Experiment
Single-arm studies of pembrolizumab in brain metastases of mixed primary histologies, and in leptomeningeal dissemination from solid tumours where most patients had breast cancer. Separately, a checkpoint inhibitor combined with an antiangiogenic antibody and platinum in triple-negative disease with active brain metastases.
Finding
Both pembrolizumab studies met their primary endpoints, and the combination study reported a high intracranial response rate. None of the three had a comparator.
Limitation
Mixed-histology cohorts dilute what can be said about breast cancer. The leptomeningeal study is small enough that its confidence interval spans the range between useless and useful. The combination study cannot separate the contribution of the checkpoint inhibitor from the platinum and the antiangiogenic antibody, and the last of those alters the measurement.

Is exclusion by the blood-brain barrier the reason systemic drugs fail in the brain?

Rationale
The standard explanation for intracranial progression during effective systemic treatment is exposure, in that the drug holds disease everywhere it reaches and the brain is the place it does not. If exposure is the whole story, a molecule engineered to cross, or a drug chosen for its penetration, should convert into intracranial response.
Experiment
Three different ways of testing that claim. A window study gave an antibody-drug conjugate before craniotomy and measured the payload recovered from the resected metastasis, making delivery itself the endpoint. A peptide conjugate built to cross by receptor-mediated transcytosis was given to patients with recurrent brain metastases. Two further studies took drugs already chosen for brain penetration, a CDK4/6 inhibitor in hormone receptor-positive disease and an mTOR inhibitor added to HER2 blockade with chemotherapy, and asked for intracranial response.
Finding
Delivery and activity came apart. The window study met its concentration endpoint in the resected metastases. The CDK4/6 study measured unbound drug in resected brain metastases above the concentrations its two target kinases require, and returned almost no confirmed intracranial response in either of its cohorts. The transcytosis conjugate, the CDK4/6 inhibitor and the mTOR combination each missed the intracranial response rule they had set themselves in advance.
Limitation
A drug concentration in resected tissue is a pharmacological surrogate with no clinical outcome attached, and the tissue came only from patients selected for being operable. That holds for the window study and for the resection cohort of the CDK4/6 study alike. The transcytosis conjugate was read at two different response rates by investigators and by independent review, and those should be read separately rather than merged, with its preset rule assessed on the independent read. The CDK4/6 study ran its hormone receptor-positive HER2-negative and HER2-positive cohorts under separate two-stage designs, so it offers two small single-arm results rather than one. The mTOR study's response rests on the smaller group evaluable for efficacy rather than on everyone enrolled, and its correlative work found no pathway alteration that marked a responder, so no biomarker-defined subgroup survives in which the rationale still holds.

Reaching the central nervous system and reading what comes back

Does engineering a drug for brain penetration turn intracranial failure into intracranial response?

Rationale
The standing explanation for central nervous system relapse is that drug does not reach the compartment. That explanation makes a prediction. Raising exposure, building a carrier that crosses, or choosing a molecule that crosses by design should convert patients who do not respond into patients who do.
Experiment
Four treatment studies came at that prediction from different directions. One measurement study tested its premise directly. Trastuzumab was given at high dose with pertuzumab in brain metastases progressing after radiotherapy, on the argument that exposure was the limiting variable. The same high-dose pair was given again with a checkpoint inhibitor added. Paclitaxel was conjugated to a peptide that uses a receptor-mediated transport route across the barrier. Everolimus, which crosses, was added to trastuzumab and vinorelbine in progressive brain metastases, under a staged design built to separate a useless intracranial response rate from a useful one. Separately, a window study measured a conjugate and its payload in brain metastasis tissue resected the day after dosing.
Finding
Drug is measurable in resected brain metastasis tissue, at the concentration that study set out to demonstrate. None of the four treatment studies delivered the intracranial activity its rationale predicted. The peptide conjugate missed its preset response rule on independent review. The everolimus study returned a single intracranial partial response, against a staged design built to find many more. The checkpoint combination fell below its prespecified efficacy threshold and was closed early. The high-dose antibody pair given without a checkpoint inhibitor confirmed central nervous system responses in only a minority of patients, while reporting a median overall survival among the longest in this cell. Reaching the compartment looks necessary. On this evidence it is not sufficient.
Limitation
The window study measured concentration and not benefit. Its lesions were operable and therefore large and accessible. It enrolled a glioblastoma cohort alongside the breast cancer one. Its drug is aimed at a different target from everything else under this question. No design here connects tissue concentration to response. The peptide conjugate reported an investigator-read and an independently-read intracranial response rate that differ from one another. They have to be read separately rather than averaged. That study also took all comers rather than restricting entry to HER2-positive disease. The everolimus study reported response on the patients evaluable for efficacy rather than on everyone enrolled. Its correlative claim about pathway mutations rests on almost no responders. The checkpoint combination stopped at a preset threshold, which is a judgement about that dose and that schedule rather than about the hypothesis. The high-dose antibody pair given alone had no preset response rule to fail. Its response result and its survival result point in different directions, from a single arm with nothing to compare against.

A higher antibody dose, a carrier built to cross, a molecule chosen for crossing, a concentration read out of the lesion itself. All four converge on intracranial activity far below what the exposure argument predicts. That convergence is the finding of this table. It is not a finding about any of these drugs individually.

Delivery and activity came apart most visibly in the two studies that measured drug rather than patients. Sacituzumab govitecan phase 0 window study recovered payload from resected brain metastases at the concentration it set out to demonstrate. The divergence here is in what each study was allowed to claim, not in how well the drugs worked. ANG1005, LCCC 1025 and Atezolizumab plus pertuzumab and high-dose trastuzumab each set a response rule in advance and each missed it. PATRICIA set none. That matters, because PATRICIA is the study in this group most often quoted as encouraging, and its two results point in opposite directions. Its confirmed intracranial response rate reached only a small minority of those treated, while its median overall survival is among the longest in that table. ATTAIN is the row the argument rests on, because it is the only randomised comparison here. A formulation designed for prolonged exposure was tested against the chemotherapy a clinician would otherwise have chosen, with overall survival as the primary endpoint, and survival did not move. Two qualifications travel with it. Entry required stable, previously treated brain metastases, so the trial says nothing about activity in progressing intracranial disease. And its central nervous system progression-free survival ran in the drug's favour without reaching conventional significance, which is the kind of secondary result that gets quoted as though the trial had been positive.

Three candidates for what else is limiting are worth holding, none of which has been separated from the others in a trial. The metastasis is not the primary, because acquired amplifications and subtype switching are both described in resected brain lesions21. The brain microenvironment supplies survival signals the same tumour does not receive elsewhere Spatial organization and what it adds. And efflux at the residual barrier around microscopic disease is not addressed by a concentration measured in a lesion large enough to resect.

That last point is also the main limit on this table. A drug concentration in resected tissue is a pharmacological surrogate with no clinical outcome attached. The tissue came only from patients selected for being operable, which means large and accessible lesions. The window study also enrolled a glioblastoma cohort alongside the breast one, and its drug is aimed at a different target from everything else under this question. No design here connects concentration to response in the same patient.

Interplay

The compartment is usually filed as a pharmacokinetic problem and it behaves like a heterogeneity problem. A brain metastasis is a clonal selection event that happened somewhere the drug historically was not. What grows there has been selected by a different pressure from what grows in the liver Metastatic dissemination and organ tropism. Exposure is the variable this literature measures because it is the variable it can measure. The measurements that would separate a delivery failure from a biological one, paired concentration and response in the same lesion, exist for almost nobody. Until they do, every intracranial failure is allocated to the barrier by default, which is the same reasoning error that allocated conjugate failure to antigen loss.

Molecular weight governs transit across an intact barrier. It does not govern access to a lesion whose vasculature is already leaking. That distinction is the whole of the objection and the whole of the answer to it.

A conjugate also has a second route the argument never considered. It has only to deliver a payload, and a membrane-permeable payload acts on cells the antibody never bound Antibody-drug conjugates and targeted delivery.

Rationale
The size argument applies to an intact barrier. A metastasis large enough to measure on imaging has vasculature that is already disrupted. The question is therefore not whether the antibody crosses healthy brain but whether it reaches tumour. A conjugate also has only to deliver its payload. A membrane-permeable payload can act on cells the antibody never bound.
Experiment
Single-arm studies of conjugates in patients with brain metastases, entered by the state of the brain disease rather than by line of treatment. Intracranial responses with the deruxtecan conjugate are routine rather than anecdotal. They occur in active disease as well as in disease already treated and stable. In the largest cohort, the central nervous system progression-free rate at the fixed timepoint was close to the overall progression-free rate in that same cohort. The emtansine conjugate produced intracranial responses too, at a lower rate than the deruxtecan cohorts report, in populations that are not otherwise comparable.

None of these studies has a control arm. Each is measured against what its own cohort was expected to do. DEBBRAH splits the same way across its cohorts. TBCRC 022 does not reproduce the pattern. Its previously untreated cohort held six patients. The generalisation should be held loosely, which is an argument for reading the population column rather than for substituting a new ranking.

The two ends of the table make the point without any comparison of drugs being possible. PATRICIA required progression after radiotherapy and sits at the bottom. LANDSCAPE required that no brain radiotherapy had been given at all and sits near the top, on the combination now considered the weakest of its class. ABC study sits at the very top, in triple-negative disease, which is the subtype with the least intracranial evidence behind it. Read as a ranking of drugs, the column says a lapatinib combination outperforms the deruxtecan cohorts, and that a chemotherapy and antibody regimen in triple-negative disease outperforms everything. Nobody believes either claim, which is the tell.

What the table cannot show is a comparison of any kind. Not one of these studies randomised anything, least of all the order of radiotherapy and systemic treatment, which is the variable its rows differ by most. There is also no column for who read the scans. Most of these rates are investigator-assessed. The one study in this chapter that published an investigator read and an independent read of the same scans is tabulated under Whether exclusion by the barrier is the explanation it is taken to be. Its two rates were not the same.

Caution

Reading the result column of that table as a ranking of drugs is the commonest error in this literature. It is made in review articles, in slide decks and at tumour boards.

The studies that look best are in large part the studies that admitted radiotherapy-naive, asymptomatic, measurable disease. The studies that look worst are in large part the studies that required progression after radiotherapy. Neither the drugs nor the patients have been randomised against each other at any point.

When a colleague quotes an intracranial response rate, the useful question is not which drug it belonged to. It is what the patient had to have to get into the study.

8 · Leptomeningeal disease and whether delivery has to be intrathecal

Leptomeningeal disease is a different problem from parenchymal disease, in three ways that matter before any drug is chosen.

There is no bulky lesion with disrupted vasculature to exploit, so the mechanism that lets a conjugate work in the parenchyma is largely absent. Disease is read from cerebrospinal fluid cytology and from neurological examination rather than from target lesions. Cytology is highly specific and insensitive, so a negative result in a patient with characteristic symptoms and characteristic imaging does not exclude the diagnosis. Repeated large-volume sampling raises the yield. Cerebrospinal fluid circulating tumour DNA improves on cytology where it is available Cerebrospinal fluid analysis in leptomeningeal disease. Survival is short enough that the choice of endpoint constrains the design before the drug is chosen.

The central nervous system as a compartment

Can systemic therapy alone change the course of leptomeningeal disease?

Rationale
Leptomeningeal involvement has been treated as a terminal event of the compartment, and the trials that established the modern systemic agents excluded it by protocol. If a drug reaches the meningeal deposits at all, survival counted in weeks might become survival counted in months without any intrathecal or radiation treatment at all.
Experiment
Two single-arm studies enrolled leptomeningeal disease specifically and set overall survival as the primary endpoint, each against a historical median named in advance. One gave tucatinib with trastuzumab and capecitabine in HER2-positive disease. The other gave trastuzumab deruxtecan in a cohort that admitted HER2-positive and HER2-low disease. A leptomeningeal group within the brain-penetrant conjugate study was reported alongside its parenchymal population.
Finding
Both cohorts ran past the historical median each had set as its comparator, and the deruxtecan cohort states that its prespecified endpoint was met. The leptomeningeal group within the conjugate study was reported as surviving longer than its own historical expectation, while the parenchymal primary endpoint of that same study was not met.
Limitation
A historical median is the weakest comparator available here, and the medians on offer were assembled before the systemic drugs these patients had already received existed, so the comparison contains an era effect as well as a treatment effect. Both cohorts are very small, and entry required fitness for a trial. Diagnosis rested on cytology or on MRI depending on the protocol, which is a case definition rather than a biological boundary. The conjugate's leptomeningeal group is a subset of an enrolled population rather than a separately powered cohort, so its survival claim is a subgroup measured against history.

Does treating the leptomeningeal compartment directly add to treating the patient systemically?

Rationale
Cerebrospinal fluid is a space that can be dosed on its own, either by placing a drug in it or by irradiating the whole neuraxis. Both approaches take the exposure problem at face value. Both accept a toxicity confined to the compartment in exchange for an exposure that systemic dosing cannot reach.
Experiment
One randomised trial added intrathecal liposomal cytarabine to whatever systemic therapy the patient was already receiving, and left that systemic choice to the treating clinician. Three single-arm studies gave intrathecal trastuzumab in HER2-positive leptomeningeal disease, two of them dose-finding and one with a neurological progression endpoint. A randomised study compared proton craniospinal irradiation against photon radiotherapy to the involved field. A phase III successor to it sits on the record with nothing reported.
Finding
Adding intrathecal cytarabine lengthened leptomeningeal progression-free survival against systemic therapy alone. Intrathecal trastuzumab proved deliverable by both escalation routes. In the study with a neurological endpoint, most patients were free of neurological progression at the early timepoint. In the study that escalated to a twice-weekly schedule, response was confined to a minority. The randomised intrathecal trial measured progression in the compartment it was treating and allowed systemic therapy to vary from patient to patient, so the endpoint favours the intervention by construction and the background treatment is uncontrolled. It added an intrathecal drug that is not HER2-directed, in a population not selected by subtype. What no row offers is the comparison the clinic needs. Nobody has randomised compartment-directed against systemic treatment, or added the intrathecal route to a modern systemic backbone. The case definition also varies across these studies, some requiring cytology and some accepting imaging and clinical signs, which is a case definition rather than a biological boundary. And the outcome that matters most to a patient here, neurological function over time, appears as a primary endpoint in one row of ten.

In practice

Do not let a negative cytology close the question when the symptoms and the MRI say otherwise. Repeat the tap, take a large volume, and image the whole neuraxis before the puncture rather than after it.

In HER2-positive disease, start with systemic therapy that has leptomeningeal evidence behind it, however weak the comparator. That is where the survival signal is.

Reserve the intrathecal route for disease progressing in the compartment while systemic treatment holds elsewhere. Nothing tests it as an addition to modern systemic therapy, so it is a rationale rather than a result. Describe it to the patient in those terms.

Name the goal at the outset. For most patients here it is neurological function and symptom control rather than length of life. Saying so at the start makes every later decision easier Palliative and end-of-life care.

9 · Screening, surveillance, and a decision the trials have not tested

Routine brain MRI in asymptomatic patients is not supported by randomised evidence in any subtype. That should be stated to the patient rather than left for her to assume otherwise.

The argument for screening is that smaller lesions are more amenable to focal treatment and less likely to require whole-brain treatment. The argument against is lead time without benefit, together with the cascade that follows finding lesions which would never have become symptomatic. Neither has been tested against survival in this disease.

Two developments have shifted the balance without settling it. Focal treatment is better tolerated and reaches more lesions than it did when the screening question was last asked. And systemic agents with intracranial activity now exist, so finding a lesion earlier can change the systemic choice rather than only adding a local one. Both are arguments. Neither is evidence.

Prognosis is what should set surveillance intensity, and it is not uniform. Survival after a breast cancer brain metastasis varies several-fold across prognostic groups defined by subtype, performance status, age, lesion number and extracranial disease9. The same scan interval cannot be right at both ends of that range.

The decision the trials have not tested is the one made most often. A patient on effective systemic therapy develops isolated intracranial progression while everything else is controlled. No trial randomises whether to change the systemic regimen or to treat the brain and continue. The general oligoprogression argument is in Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease and applies here with one addition. The brain may be a sanctuary rather than a site of resistance, so a regimen still working everywhere else may be working in the only sense available to it.

In practice

Image the brain for symptoms. Where you screen an asymptomatic patient, say that you are doing it on a rationale rather than on evidence. Say what you would do with an abnormal result.

Set the interval from the prognostic group rather than from the calendar9.

Biopsy a solitary brain lesion when the diagnosis is uncertain. Request receptors whenever tissue is being removed anyway. Acquired ERBB2 amplification and intrinsic subtype switching are both described in brain metastases21, so the target in the brain may not be the target in the primary.

Treat isolated intracranial progression as a question about the compartment before treating it as systemic failure Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease.
