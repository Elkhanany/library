Risk assessment and prevention

The drugs work. Roughly one eligible woman in six takes them, and that is the problem worth solving.

1 · Individual and population risk models and their calibration

A risk model answers a question, and different models answer different questions. Most confusion in this area comes from asking a model something it was not built to estimate.

Two properties of a model have to be judged separately, and they can move in opposite directions.

Calibration asks whether the predicted number of cancers matches the observed number. It is what makes a threshold such as a 1.66% five-year risk mean anything, because eligibility rules are built on absolute numbers.

Discrimination asks whether the model ranks individuals correctly. It is measured by a concordance statistic, and for breast cancer risk models it is modest. In a prospective family cohort of 15,732 women followed for a median of 11.1 years, during which 619 developed breast cancer, concordance statistics ranged from 0.60 to 0.71 across four widely used models1.

Those numbers deserve a plain reading. A model that is well calibrated at the population level can still order individuals only a little better than chance. That is not a reason to abandon models. They succeed at allocating an intervention across a population. They fail at telling one woman what will happen to her.

Population risk and individual risk also diverge in a way that changes policy. Most breast cancers occur in women who were never in a high-risk category, because that category is small. A programme that acts only on the top few percent of predicted risk will therefore prevent a minority of the disease, however well it is targeted.

2 · Gail, Tyrer-Cuzick, BOADICEA and CanRisk compared

Each of these models was built for a specific population and a specific decision. Reading the design intent explains most of the disagreement between them.

The Gail model estimates absolute invasive breast cancer risk from age at menarche, age at first live birth, number of previous breast biopsies and number of affected first-degree relatives2. It was derived from a screening programme cohort, and the original paper says the projections are most reliable for women who are examined about annually. It contains almost no family structure, so it cannot represent a strong pedigree. Its clinical role is eligibility screening for prevention, and that is the role it was built for.

It was also derived in white women, and that limitation was addressed rather than ignored. A model rebuilt on data from 1,607 African American women with invasive breast cancer and 1,647 controls gave higher risk estimates than the original tool3. Applied to screening data from a prevention trial, 30.3% of African American women met a five-year risk of at least 1.66% by the new model against 14.5% by the original. An eligibility threshold is only as good as the model feeding it.

Tyrer-Cuzick was built to combine a detailed pedigree with personal risk factors. It uses Bayesian inference across the family history to estimate the probability of carrying a predisposing variant, then refines the estimate with hormonal and reproductive factors4. It is the appropriate choice when family history is substantial and genetic testing has not been done.

BOADICEA was built as a genetic model and has since absorbed everything else. The current version incorporates truncating variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, CHEK2 and ATM, a 313-variant polygenic score, a residual polygenic component, lifestyle and reproductive factors, and mammographic density5. Across the UK population it predicts lifetime risks from 2.8% at the first percentile to 30.6% at the ninety-ninth. It classifies 14.7% of women as moderate risk by the 17% to 30% lifetime band, and 1.1% as high risk at 30% or above.

CanRisk is the delivery mechanism for that model, not a separate model. It is the web interface carrying BOADICEA, with a pedigree builder, and it was the first freely accessible cancer risk prediction tool to carry a CE marking6. The distinction matters when reading a paper, because a study of CanRisk is a study of BOADICEA plus an interface.

Independent validation ranks them consistently. In the prospective family cohort, the ratio of expected to observed cancers was 1.05 for BOADICEA and 1.03 for Tyrer-Cuzick1. Both were well calibrated. The ratio was 0.79 for the Gail-derived tool and 0.59 for BRCAPRO, both of which underpredicted. Concordance statistics were 0.70 for BOADICEA and 0.71 for Tyrer-Cuzick, against 0.60 for the Gail-derived tool. Models that use multigenerational family history performed better, even in women at average risk.

In practice

Pick the model by the question. For eligibility for pharmacologic prevention in a woman without notable family history, a Gail-type five-year absolute risk is the established input and the one prevention trials used7.

For a woman with a meaningful pedigree, use Tyrer-Cuzick or CanRisk. A Gail estimate in that setting will understate risk because the model has nowhere to put the family history1.

Check whether the model you are using was validated in a population like your patient. The Gail-type tool required rebuilding for African American women, and the rebuilt version doubled the proportion meeting the prevention threshold3.

Record which model produced the number. A risk figure without its model is uninterpretable, and it will be reused by the next clinician who reads the note.

3 · Benign breast disease and risk lesions as risk modifiers

Benign breast disease is common, and its risk implications are graded rather than binary.

A cohort of 9,087 women biopsied for benign disease was followed for a median of 15 years8. Relative risk of subsequent breast cancer against a population registry rose across three histologic groups: 1.27 for nonproliferative lesions (95% CI 1.15 to 1.41), 1.88 for proliferative lesions without atypia (95% CI 1.66 to 2.12), and 4.24 for atypical hyperplasia (95% CI 3.26 to 5.41). Nonproliferative findings accounted for 67% of that cohort. The earlier Nashville series gave the same ordering, with a relative risk of 5.3 for atypia against nonproliferative findings (95% CI 3.1 to 8.8)9.

Atypia is where the risk becomes management-relevant. Among 331 women with atypia followed for a mean of 13.7 years, 66 developed breast cancer, and cumulative incidence approached 35% at 30 years10. Multifocal atypia with calcifications carried a relative risk of 10.35 (95% CI 6.13 to 16.4), and women under 45 at diagnosis carried a relative risk of 6.76 (95% CI 3.24 to 12.4). Risk was similar for atypical ductal and atypical lobular hyperplasia. The management options that follow from this risk level are set out in the accompanying review11.

Caution

Family history did not add significant further risk in women who already had atypia10. This is counterintuitive and it is clinically consequential, because most risk models will add the two. A woman with atypia is already in a high-risk group, and the pedigree does not move her much further within it.

The risk from atypia is bilateral, not confined to the biopsied breast. That is the observation that makes atypia a marker of a susceptible field rather than an obligate precursor, and Hyperplasia, atypia, and the limits of a linear continuum develops the argument. It also means that unilateral surgery is a poor response to it.

4 · Breast density and other risk modifiers

Density is discussed as a property of the breast and measured as a property of an image. Breast density, what it is and why it predicts sets out what the measurement is. This section is about what it does to a risk estimate.

The association is one of the strongest in the field. A meta-analysis of 42 studies aggregated more than 14,000 cases and 226,000 non-cases12. Relative risks of incident breast cancer in the general population rose across four percent density categories, each compared against density below 5%: 1.79 for 5% to 24%, 2.11 for 25% to 49%, 2.92 for 50% to 74%, and 4.64 for 75% or more. The association was much stronger for percent density than for categorical parenchymal pattern scores, which is a statement about measurement rather than about biology.

Masking and independent risk have to be separated, and nested case-control data separate them. Women with density in 75% or more of the mammogram had an odds ratio of 4.7 against women below 10%13. The odds ratio was 3.5 for screen-detected cancers and 17.8 for cancers appearing within 12 months of a negative screen. The excess risk persisted for at least 8 years, which masking alone cannot explain.

Supplemental imaging follows from the masking component rather than from the risk component, and that policy question belongs in Dense breasts, supplemental imaging, and notification policy with the DENSE data14.

Two other modifiers belong in a risk model rather than in a lifestyle discussion. Endogenous hormone levels predict postmenopausal risk, with a relative risk of 2.00 in the highest quintile of serum oestradiol against the lowest (95% CI 1.47 to 2.71) in a reanalysis of nine prospective studies15. And the genetic architecture of an individual tumour relates to how it was detected, so mode of detection is not independent of biology16.

The modern models absorb these rather than leaving them to judgement. BOADICEA takes mammographic density and a polygenic score as inputs alongside family history5, which is why a density result now changes a computed risk rather than a clinical impression.

5 · Lifestyle, metabolic, and environmental prevention

This section is short because the evidence is thinner than the volume of advice suggests.

Alcohol has the clearest dose relationship. A collaborative reanalysis pooled 53 studies including 58,515 women with breast cancer and 95,067 without17. Relative risk rose by 7.1% for each additional 10 g of alcohol per day (95% CI 5.5 to 8.7). Relative risk was 1.32 at an intake of 35 to 44 g per day and 1.46 at 45 g or more, each against no alcohol. The same analysis estimated that about 4% of breast cancers in developed countries are attributable to alcohol. Restricting the analysis to 22,255 women with breast cancer who reported no alcohol showed no independent effect of smoking.

Dietary intervention has been tested prospectively and the result is modest. Long-term follow-up of the Women's Health Initiative dietary modification trial found a reduction in breast cancer mortality with a low-fat dietary pattern18. That is an effect on mortality after diagnosis rather than a demonstrated large effect on incidence.

Metabolic intervention has been tested directly and failed. Metformin did not improve invasive disease-free survival against placebo in early breast cancer19. Obesity remains associated with worse outcomes, including differential benefit from endocrine agents by body mass index20, but an association of that kind does not establish that intervening on weight reverses it.

Environmental exposures are where claims most outrun data. Night shift work is a worked example of how the evidence moves. Analysis of three prospective cohorts, combined with a meta-analysis of prior publications, found no association between night shift work and breast cancer incidence21. Earlier positive findings had come largely from retrospective designs.

Caution

The honest summary is that lifestyle modification has a real but small effect on breast cancer incidence, and the interventions with large effects are pharmacologic and surgical. Presenting lifestyle advice as the main preventive option is comfortable and it misallocates the conversation. The options with the largest demonstrated effect are in Pharmacologic risk reduction and its persistent underuse and Risk-reducing surgery and the decision calculus.

6 · Pharmacologic risk reduction and its persistent underuse

The evidence here is unusually strong for a preventive intervention, and it is almost unused. The 2009 ASCO update found that tamoxifen and, after the menopause, raloxifene reduce risk, and did not recommend aromatase inhibitors or fenretinide outside a trial22.

The randomised record is wider than the trials usually quoted. Much of it was not built to answer this question. Some trials selected women for breast cancer risk. Others selected them for osteoporosis, coronary risk, a hysterectomy or a germline variant, and counted breast cancers alongside the outcome they were designed for. A few enrolled women after surgery for an in situ or small invasive lesion.

Where these trials agree, they agree on mechanism. Every endocrine agent that reduced breast cancer did so in oestrogen receptor-positive disease wherever receptor status was analysed. None reduced receptor-negative cancer7,27,28. None had overall mortality as its primary endpoint, and none has shown a reduction in deaths. The unit of benefit is an incidence rate, which is why the harms of treatment weigh so heavily.

NSABP P-1 established the principle and set the eligibility threshold that NSABP P-2 (STAR) and NCIC CTG MAP.3 later reused7. Endometrial cancer rose, with a risk ratio of 2.53 (95% CI 1.35 to 4.97), and thromboembolic events rose, both predominantly in women aged 50 or older.

Two smaller tamoxifen trials found no effect at their first analyses, published in the same year as P-1. Each set of investigators pointed to who had been enrolled. Royal Marsden tamoxifen prevention trial selected women mainly on a strong family history. Its investigators proposed that the importance of oestrogen promotion may differ between such women and the women P-1 selected mostly on non-genetic risk factors38. Italian Tamoxifen Prevention Study enrolled women who had had a hysterectomy and were at usual or slightly reduced risk. Part of that cohort had also had both ovaries removed39,29.

Long follow-up moved both trials toward P-1 without turning either into a confirmation. In the Royal Marsden trial the effect on all invasive cancer stayed short of significance. The reduction in receptor-positive cancer appeared mainly after treatment stopped33. The Italian trial stayed null overall. Its benefit was confined to women at high risk of hormone receptor-positive disease, a group defined on reproductive and hormonal factors in an analysis its authors call exploratory40,29.

Neither design can say whether a strong family history, or the hormonal profile of women who have had a hysterectomy, blunts tamoxifen. Each trial tested one population and was a fraction of the size of P-1. Read with P-1, they say that the benefit depends on how much of the risk being treated is oestrogen-driven, which is a question of selection rather than of the drug.

The durability is what makes the benefit-to-harm calculation work. In IBIS-I, protection in the second decade of follow-up was as large as in the first28. Five years of treatment buys at least sixteen years of protection, while the excess of serious adverse events is concentrated during treatment. The late receptor-positive reduction in the Royal Marsden trial looks like the same carry-over, in a trial that had not shown it during treatment33.

NSABP P-2 (STAR) is the only trial in the table built to compare two preventive drugs directly. It compared raloxifene with tamoxifen in postmenopausal women41,23. Parity at the first report gave way to a tamoxifen advantage for invasive cancer with longer follow-up. Raloxifene kept its safety advantage, with fewer endometrial cancers and fewer thromboembolic events23. The trial has no placebo arm. Its estimate that raloxifene keeps 76% of tamoxifen's effect assumes the halving of risk seen in P-1, so it is an extrapolation rather than a measurement. Women still taking tamoxifen were offered raloxifene after the first report, so later follow-up also carries a partial crossover.

In the table, the placebo-controlled evidence for raloxifene comes from trials that did not select women for breast cancer risk at all. MORE/CORE and RUTH tested raloxifene, PEARL (lasofoxifene) tested lasofoxifene and Generations (arzoxifene) tested arzoxifene, in women recruited for osteoporosis or for coronary risk27,24,26,25. Each drug reduced breast cancer against placebo. Where the trials separated receptor-negative cancer, as MORE, CORE and RUTH did, it was not reduced42,27,43. The harm was as consistent as the benefit. Venous thromboembolism rose with each of the three drugs. Raloxifene also raised fatal stroke in RUTH24.

These trials add two things. Breast cancer was a primary endpoint in RUTH, PEARL and Generations, so their results are not incidental findings. And in RUTH the relative reduction was similar whatever a woman's estimated baseline risk43. What they cannot supply is the absolute benefit in women selected for breast cancer risk, because their own absolute reductions are small, as the RUTH row shows. CORE did not randomise again. It continued the MORE arms in the women who agreed to go on, so its longer estimate rests on a self-selected part of the original randomisation27. PEARL reports its breast cancer reduction for the higher of its two doses26.

Aromatase inhibitors reduce breast cancer in their placebo-controlled trials at least as much as tamoxifen did in its own, without the endometrial and thrombotic signal. In IBIS-II prevention, anastrozole reduced breast cancer against placebo, and the benefit continued after treatment stopped, with no excess of fractures or cardiovascular disease44,31. Exemestane gave a consistent result in NCIC CTG MAP.330. The comparison with tamoxifen crosses trials. No trial in the table randomised an aromatase inhibitor against tamoxifen. The randomised comparisons of the two after ductal carcinoma in situ are argued in Endocrine risk reduction.

Carriers of a BRCA1 or BRCA2 variant are at the highest risk. The randomised endocrine evidence in them is the thinnest. LIBER randomised postmenopausal carriers to letrozole or placebo. Its confidence interval is wide enough to include a large benefit, no effect and harm37. Its investigators call it underpowered. Women with a breast cancer in remission were eligible, so part of its endpoint is a second cancer. Carriers who had already had a prophylactic oophorectomy or a breast cancer were more likely to enter. Those who declined cited possible side effects, the chance of placebo and a lack of support from their physicians45. The question is left open. The answer may differ between the genes, because BRCA1-associated disease is predominantly triple-negative, as Inherited susceptibility and familial breast cancer sets out. LIBER is the only trial in the table restricted to carriers. The surgical evidence in Risk-reducing surgery and the decision calculus is observational.

Dose has been addressed, because toxicity is the stated barrier. In TAM-01, low-dose tamoxifen reduced breast events against placebo in women treated for intraepithelial neoplasia. The effect held at the ten-year report46,35. Patient-reported outcomes did not differ between arms except for slightly more frequent daily hot flushes46.

The same dose has not shown a significant effect in the two other trials in the table that tested it. HOT gave it to recently postmenopausal women taking hormone replacement therapy. Women on such therapy were the group in which the Italian trial had seen its early benefit47. HOT did not significantly reduce breast cancer, and its favourable trends were in subgroups34. Its confidence interval is wide enough to include an effect of the size TAM-01 found, so HOT is a null result rather than a negative one. In Low-dose tamoxifen and fenretinide 2x2 trial, low-dose tamoxifen lowered circulating IGF-I and mammographic density36. Neither biomarker at baseline, nor the change in density, predicted breast events. The combination with fenretinide did no better than placebo, with an adverse interaction between the two drugs.

Fenretinide, a vitamin A analogue, is the one drug in the table that is not endocrine. Its trial with a clinical primary endpoint was null32. In Fenretinide second-breast-cancer trial, fenretinide after surgery for stage I cancer or ductal carcinoma in situ did not reduce contralateral or ipsilateral breast cancer. A benefit in premenopausal women, with an opposite trend after the menopause, came from analyses its investigators called post hoc and exploratory. The single-centre long-term follow-up kept that pattern but covers only part of the cohort48. Fenretinide alone also had the lowest rate of breast events in the 2x2 trial, a secondary endpoint with few events36. Two signals in premenopausal women make a hypothesis rather than an indication, and the 2009 ASCO update did not recommend fenretinide outside a trial22.

Now the failure. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies covering 21,423 women at increased risk found a pooled uptake of preventive therapy of 16.3% (95% CI 13.6 to 19.0)49. Uptake was 25.2% within trials and 8.7% outside them. Uptake was higher after an abnormal biopsy, at higher objective risk, and where a physician had recommended it. Only one study reported persistence of at least 80% at 5 years.

In practice

Physician recommendation is among the strongest modifiable determinants of uptake49. The decision is often framed as a patient preference problem. It is at least as much an offer problem.

Offer the aromatase inhibitor to postmenopausal women. Its effect against placebo is at least as large as tamoxifen's, without the endometrial or thrombotic excess31. That comparison crosses trials, because none in the table randomised the two.

Where the choice after the menopause lies between tamoxifen and raloxifene, it is a trade. Tamoxifen prevents more invasive cancer. Raloxifene causes fewer endometrial cancers and thromboembolic events23.

Quote the benefit as absolute numbers over a defined horizon, and quote the harms the same way. The NSABP P-1 row of the table gives one finding twice, as a relative reduction and as cancers per 1,000 women. The two are heard differently7.

For a woman who declines because of toxicity, low-dose tamoxifen is an evidence-based alternative rather than a compromise, at least in intraepithelial neoplasia46. Outside that population its randomised tests in the table have not shown a significant effect34,36.

Say that receptor-negative disease is not prevented by any of these agents7. A woman whose family history suggests triple-negative disease should hear that before she starts. A carrier should also hear that LIBER, which tested an aromatase inhibitor in carriers, was too small to settle whether it works37.

7 · Risk-reducing surgery and the decision calculus

Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy produces the largest risk reduction available, and the size of the effect is not in doubt.

One series followed 639 women with a family history who underwent bilateral prophylactic mastectomy at a median age of 42, for a median of 14 years50. In the moderate-risk group, 4 breast cancers occurred against 37.4 predicted by a risk model, a reduction of 89.5%. Among high-risk probands, breast cancer occurred in 3 of 214, which is 1.4%. It occurred in 156 of their 403 sisters who had not had the surgery.

In carriers specifically, breast cancer occurred in 2 of 105 women after bilateral prophylactic mastectomy, which is 1.9%51. It occurred in 184 of 378 matched carriers who did not have the operation, which is 48.7%, over a mean follow-up of 6.4 years. Risk reduction was approximately 90% in women with intact ovaries and approximately 95% in women with prior or concurrent oophorectomy. A larger prospective cohort of 2,482 carriers found no breast cancers among 247 women after risk-reducing mastectomy52.

Salpingo-oophorectomy is the decision with a mortality signal behind it. In the same cohort, risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy was associated with lower ovarian cancer risk52. It was associated with lower risk of a first breast cancer in BRCA1 carriers (hazard ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.96) and in BRCA2 carriers (hazard ratio 0.36, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.82). All-cause mortality was lower (hazard ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.61), as were breast cancer specific mortality and ovarian cancer specific mortality.

The asymmetry between the two operations is the part to carry into the consultation. Mastectomy has the larger effect on breast cancer incidence and no demonstrated effect on overall mortality in these cohorts. Oophorectomy has a smaller effect on breast cancer incidence and an association with lower all-cause mortality. They are not interchangeable, and a patient who understands only the incidence figures will weigh them wrongly.

Three cautions belong with any of these numbers. All of these data are observational, because no randomised trial of risk-reducing mastectomy exists or will. Women who choose surgery differ from women who do not. And the follow-up in the carrier series is short relative to a lifetime of risk, which matters because the comparator group's risk keeps accruing.

Caution

Contralateral risk-reducing mastectomy after a unilateral cancer is a different decision from bilateral surgery in an unaffected carrier, and the evidence above does not transfer to it. In a woman whose prognosis is governed by the cancer she already has, removing the other breast reduces a second cancer without changing the risk that is actually driving her outcome. The exception is the carrier with early-stage disease and a long expected survival, where the contralateral risk is high and durable53.

8 · Prevention implementation and unequal access

Everything above works. The gap between efficacy and delivered benefit is where the preventable burden actually sits, and it is not distributed evenly.

Start with the size of the gap. Preventive endocrine therapy reduces breast cancer incidence by roughly half in the trials, and about one in six eligible women takes it49. No biologically plausible improvement in the drugs would produce the benefit that closing that gap would.

The gap widens along familiar lines, and each mechanism has been measured separately.

Risk assessment itself is unequal. The standard risk model required rebuilding for African American women, and the rebuilt version identified roughly twice the proportion meeting the prevention eligibility threshold3. Women assessed with a miscalibrated model are not offered what they qualify for.

Genomic risk stratification is unequal. A polygenic score derived in European-ancestry populations discriminated substantially worse in women of African ancestry, with an area under the curve of 0.571 against 0.63054,55. Expanding gene panels widens the disparity in uninterpretable results rather than narrowing it56.

Access to the pathway is unequal at every subsequent step. Time from an abnormal screening mammogram to biopsy differs by multiple factors operating at patient, facility and area level57. Time from diagnosis to surgery differs by race even within an equal-access health system, and the difference tracks survival58. Residence in historically redlined districts is associated with worse breast cancer outcomes59, and cumulative physiological stress burden is associated with all-cause mortality after a breast cancer diagnosis60. Structural racism is a measured exposure in this literature rather than a framing device61.

Trial participation is unequal, which feeds back into the evidence base. Structural, clinical and physician-level barriers account for most of the shortfall in enrolment among under-represented groups, rather than patient refusal62.

In practice

Audit the offer, not the uptake. A clinic that records how many eligible women were offered preventive endocrine therapy, rather than how many accepted it, will find the modifiable step49.

Check which risk model your service uses and in whom it was validated. A single default model applied to every patient is a silent source of inequity3.

Treat the polygenic component of a risk estimate as ancestry-dependent, and say so when the estimate is given54. Disparities across the breast cancer continuum develops why this matters beyond prevention.

Measure the intervals your service controls, from abnormal screen to biopsy and from diagnosis to treatment. Those intervals differ by group within single institutions, and they are visible to audit57,58.

The concluding claim of this chapter is narrow and should be read strictly. Breast cancer prevention does not currently need a better drug. It needs the drugs that exist to be offered, and the models that select for them to work in everyone.
