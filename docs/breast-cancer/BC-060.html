Part II · Normal biology, carcinogenesis, and inherited susceptibility · Chapter 6

The somatic genomic landscape

A mutational signature is not a label on a tumour. It is a process that is still running.

1 · Driver mutations and copy number architecture by subtype

Breast cancer has few recurrently mutated genes and a great deal of copy number change. That asymmetry is the first thing to hold, because it determines which assays are informative.

The comprehensive molecular portrait of the disease found only three genes mutated at high frequency across all cases, namely PIK3CA, TP53 and GATA3, with a long tail of low-frequency events1. The same work showed that the four main expression subtypes differ more in copy number architecture than in point mutation burden. Luminal A disease is comparatively quiet and is dominated by PIK3CA mutation. Basal-like disease carries TP53 loss in the large majority of cases together with widespread segmental aneuploidy. HER2-enriched disease is defined by an amplification.

Copy number is also where the alternative classification lives. Clustering 2,000 tumours on joint copy number and expression produced ten integrative groups with distinct outcomes, several of which are defined by specific amplifications rather than by receptor status2. Those groups differ in when they relapse as well as in whether they relapse3.

Whole-genome sequencing of 560 breast cancers extended the driver catalogue and, more usefully, placed the drivers alongside the rearrangement and signature landscape in the same tumours4.

The practical consequence is about panels. A targeted panel built around recurrently mutated genes captures the minority of this disease that is driven by point mutation. It reports poorly on the amplifications and deletions that define most of the rest. A negative panel result is a statement about the panel.

2 · Mutational signatures and the processes that generate them

A mutational signature is a characteristic distribution of base changes in their sequence context. It is extracted computationally from a catalogue of mutations, and it is a record of the processes that produced them.

The scale of the analyses is worth stating because it explains why the framework is stable. The first large extraction analysed 4,938,362 mutations from 7,042 cancers and recovered more than 20 distinct signatures5. The pan-cancer whole-genome analysis that followed used 84,729,690 somatic mutations from 4,645 whole genomes and 19,184 exomes, and defined 49 single-base substitution signatures, 11 doublet-base signatures, 4 clustered-base signatures and 17 small insertion and deletion signatures6.

Here is the part that is usually lost. A signature is not a descriptive category applied to a finished genome. It is the fingerprint of a mechanism, and mechanisms have tenses. Some signatures record an exposure that has ended. Some record a repair defect that is still present. Some record an enzyme that is still active in the tumour being treated.

That distinction is the whole argument of this chapter. A signature attributable to a defect in homologous recombination tells you about a current vulnerability, which is why it can be used to select therapy7. A signature attributable to an enzymatic mutator tells you that the tumour is still generating new variants, which is a different and more uncomfortable statement.

Two measurement caveats apply to every signature result. Extraction needs enough mutations, so signature calling is unreliable on small panels and on low tumour content. And signatures are correlated with one another, so attributing a given tumour's catalogue to a specific set of processes is an inference with uncertainty attached, not a readout.

3 · APOBEC and repair-associated signatures as worked examples

Two signature families dominate breast cancer, and they illustrate the two tenses.

APOBEC3 is the mutator. The APOBEC mutational pattern was found across 954,247 mutations in 2,680 exomes from 14 cancer types, and it reached 68% of all mutations in some individual samples8. Within breast cancer the HER2-enriched subtype was clearly enriched for it. The pattern also produces kataegis, meaning localised hypermutation clustered around rearrangement breakpoints5.

The causal chain has been established directly. Deleting APOBEC3A from human cancer cell lines that naturally accumulate these mutations diminished the APOBEC-associated signatures, and deleting APOBEC3B as well reduced the burden further without eliminating it9. APOBEC3A is the main driver of these mutations. The uracil glycosylase UNG was required for the transversion component, and loss of the translesion polymerase REV1 lowered the overall mutation burden. The signature therefore has a defined enzymology, and it is ongoing rather than historical.

That last word is why this section exists. APOBEC3 mutagenesis generates the specific hotspot alterations that terminate endocrine therapy with CDK4/6 inhibition, including PIK3CA E545K and E542K, ESR1 E380Q and RB1 loss, and APOBEC3-dominant tumours show shorter progression-free survival on that combination10. A mutational process is here acting as a therapeutic problem in its own right. Temporal heterogeneity and clonal evolution returns to it as a generator of acquired resistance.

The repair-associated signatures are the other tense. Loss of homologous recombination leaves a compound mark across substitutions, indels and rearrangements, and that mark can be read from a genome and used to predict BRCA1 and BRCA2 deficiency without inspecting the gene7. Crucially it detects the phenotype produced by promoter hypermethylation as well as by mutation11. A germline test answers a question about inheritance. A signature answers a question about current repair capacity, and Inherited susceptibility and familial breast cancer and DNA repair, replication stress, and genomic instability depend on the difference.

Caution

Signature terminology invites a category error. Saying a tumour "has APOBEC" sounds like a classification, in the way that saying it "is HER2 positive" is a classification. It is not. It is a claim that an enzyme has been mutating this genome and may still be doing so. The clinical implication is not a label but an expectation about what will appear next.

4 · Structural variation, chromothripsis, and extrachromosomal DNA

Structural variation in breast cancer is not a uniform background of rearrangement. Several distinct mechanisms operate, and they leave separable traces.

Chromothripsis is a single catastrophic event. Tens to hundreds of rearrangements occur in one cellular crisis, producing a characteristic crisscrossing pattern and oscillation between two copy number states12. Analysis of 2,658 tumours across 38 cancer types found the phenomenon pervasive, with frequencies above 50% in several cancer types, and showed that it contributes directly to oncogene amplification13. A large amplification is therefore not necessarily the endpoint of gradual selection. It may be the residue of one bad mitosis.

Extrachromosomal DNA is the mechanism with the sharpest consequences. Amplified oncogenes can be carried on circular elements that lack centromeres, and such elements drive tumour evolution and genetic heterogeneity14. Across many cancer types, extrachromosomal amplification is associated with oncogene amplification and worse outcome15.

The mechanism deserves one sentence of its own, because it explains rather than describes. Centromeres are what make mitotic segregation equal. Elements without them are distributed to daughter cells without any mechanism enforcing equality, so copy number drifts at every division. Under selection the population shifts up, and when selection is removed it shifts back down.

That argument is developed in full for the ERBB2 amplicon in Amplicon topology, extrachromosomal DNA, and the mechanistic origin of instability, and it is the reason heterogeneity in HER2-positive disease has a generator rather than merely a description. The point to carry forward from here is that structural mechanism predicts behaviour under treatment. Intrachromosomal amplification is comparatively stable. Extrachromosomal amplification is reversible in the population sense, in both directions.

5 · Chromatin variants as a distinct source of heterogeneity

Sequence is one tier of variation. Chromatin state is another, and it is not reducible to the first.

Two cells with identical genomes can differ in which regions are accessible, which enhancers are active and which transcription factors are loaded. That difference is heritable through division and is reversible, which is precisely what a genetic difference is not. Chromatin and transcriptional state are increasingly treated as a distinct axis of breast cancer heterogeneity alongside genomic alteration16.

The clearest functional demonstration is the drug-tolerant state. A subpopulation survives drug exposure through a chromatin-mediated programme rather than through mutation, and sensitivity returns after passage off the drug17. Non-stem cells in breast populations can also convert spontaneously to a stem-like state, so position in the hierarchy is itself a reversible property18.

Spatial genomics has begun to place these tiers in the same tissue. Mapping clone identity and expression state together in situ shows that genetically defined clones and transcriptional states are not in one-to-one correspondence19. The same clone can occupy different states in different regions.

The measurement consequence is large and is routinely ignored. Sequencing a tumour reports the genetic tier only. A tumour with no detectable subclonal structure at the DNA level may still be composed of functionally distinct populations, and no amount of deeper sequencing will find them.

6 · Proteogenomics and the limits of inferring protein from transcript

Almost every molecular classification in routine use is built on RNA or on DNA. Drugs act on proteins. The gap between those statements is what proteogenomics measures.

Integrated analysis of breast tumours across genome, transcriptome and proteome showed that correlation between messenger RNA and protein abundance is modest and varies widely by gene20. Phosphoproteomic data in the same tumours identified signalling activity that was not predictable from the underlying genomic alterations.

The follow-up analysis of 122 treatment-naive primary breast cancers made the clinical point sharper21. Protein-level and phosphoprotein-level data refined the assessment of retinoblastoma protein status relevant to CDK4/6 inhibitor sensitivity, and identified tumour subsets on the basis of immune features not apparent from expression alone. Detailed analysis of the ERBB2 amplicon at protein level challenged assignments made on standard diagnostics.

Three tiers therefore exist, and each answers a different question. DNA reports what alterations are present. RNA reports what is being transcribed. Protein and phosphoprotein report what is actually signalling. A claim about pathway activation made from expression data is an inference across two tiers.

This is the same discipline the assay chapters enforce elsewhere in this book. A statement about amplification is a statement about DNA, and a statement about staining is a statement about protein Receptor assessment and the measurement problem. The proteogenomic data quantify how often those two disagree.

7 · Clonal architecture at diagnosis and what one biopsy represents

A tumour at diagnosis is a population. What a biopsy returns is a sample of that population, taken from one place at one time.

Single-cell copy number sequencing showed that breast tumours are composed of discrete subclonal populations rather than a continuum22. In triple-negative disease the copy number evolution was punctuated, with an early burst of rearrangement followed by stable clonal expansion23. Multiregion sequencing of primaries found substantial diversification, with driver alterations often confined to a subset of regions within one tumour24. The subclonal structure is already established in the in situ compartment, as Early subclonal diversification already detectable in situ sets out.

Quantifying how much of a tumour any biopsy represents is itself a research problem. Pan-cancer estimates of intratumour heterogeneity depend heavily on sequencing depth, purity and the deconvolution method used, and comparisons across studies using different methods are not straightforward25.

Two consequences are worth separating.

The first is about detection. A subclonal driver present in 15% of cells may fall below the confident calling threshold of a routine assay. Absence of a report is not absence of the alteration.

The second is about action. A clonal alteration, present in every cell, is a target whose removal affects the whole population. A subclonal alteration is a target whose removal affects part of it. Reports rarely distinguish the two, and the distinction determines what a response should be expected to look like. Metastatic and relapse genomes are enriched for alterations that were subclonal in the primary26, which is what selection acting on this structure looks like.

8 · Selection, drift, and the difficulty of calling a driver

The word driver does a great deal of work in this field, and it is worth asking what evidence supports it in any given case.

The formal definition is evolutionary. A driver is an alteration under positive selection. Applying that test across 7,664 tumours from 29 cancer types showed that positive selection dominates in cancer and that negative selection is almost absent outside homozygous loss of essential genes27. The same analysis found an average of about four coding substitutions under positive selection per tumour, and estimated that half of driver substitutions lie in genes not yet recognised as cancer genes.

The consensus catalogue built from 9,423 tumour exomes using 26 algorithms identified 299 driver genes and more than 3,400 putative missense driver mutations, of which experimental validation confirmed 60% to 85% as likely drivers28. The residual uncertainty in a curated, multi-method, pan-cancer catalogue is therefore substantial.

Against that stands neutral evolution. A measurable fraction of tumours show subclonal mutation frequency distributions consistent with neutral growth, in which subclonal variation accumulates without selection29. Where that model holds, a recurrent subclonal alteration may be a passenger carried up by an expanding clone rather than the reason the clone expanded.

Three failure modes follow, and they are the ones to watch for in a report.

Recurrence is not selection. A gene may be recurrently mutated because it is long, late-replicating or in an APOBEC-preferred context.

Clonality is not causation. An alteration present in every cell may have been carried along by the founding clone.

Functional annotation is not evidence in this patient. A variant classified as a driver from pan-cancer data may be doing nothing in a tumour with a different dependency structure.

One further source of false positives is not tumour biology at all. Clonal haematopoiesis becomes common with age30, is shaped by prior cancer therapy31, and contributes variants to plasma cell-free DNA32. A variant called from blood is not automatically a tumour variant, and Liquid biopsy and longitudinal monitoring sets out how to tell them apart.
