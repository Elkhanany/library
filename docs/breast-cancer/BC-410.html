Surgery and reconstruction

A century of removing less, and what each retreat actually traded away.

1 · Breast-conserving surgery and mastectomy

The randomised evidence that settled this question is old, large and unambiguous. It is worth knowing precisely, because the modern argument is about which patients rather than about whether.

In NSABP B-06, 1851 women were randomised to total mastectomy, lumpectomy alone, or lumpectomy with breast irradiation, and followed for 20 years1. The hazard ratio for death with lumpectomy alone against mastectomy was 1.05, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.90 to 1.23. With lumpectomy and irradiation it was 0.97, with an interval of 0.83 to 1.14. Ipsilateral breast recurrence was the outcome that separated the arms, at 14.3% with irradiation and 39.2% without it.

The Milan trial reached the same conclusion by a different route. Among 701 women randomised to quadrantectomy with radiotherapy or to radical mastectomy, 20-year all-cause mortality was 41.7% and 41.2%2. Ipsilateral breast recurrence was 8.8% against 2.3%.

Three further trials agreed. At the Institut Gustave-Roussy, 179 women with tumours up to 20 mm were randomised, and survival, distant metastasis and locoregional recurrence did not differ at 15 years3. In the Danish trial, recurrence-free and overall survival did not differ among 793 correctly randomised patients followed for a median of 19.6 years4. EORTC 10801 randomised 868 women with stage I or II disease, and at 20 years time to distant metastasis and time to death did not differ significantly, with hazard ratios of 1.13 and 1.115.

The overview of all local-therapy trials explains why these trials found no survival difference while the radiotherapy trials did6. Comparisons that changed five-year local recurrence by less than 10% changed 15-year breast cancer mortality little. Where the difference was larger, 7% against 26%, 15-year breast cancer mortality was 44.6% against 49.5%. About one breast cancer death was avoided for every four local recurrences avoided.

Registry comparisons since then have tended to favour conservation. In United States registry data, breast-conserving therapy was associated with higher breast cancer-specific survival than mastectomy7,8. A Norwegian registry of 13,015 women found survival better or equal after conservation in every stage and age group9. Early complications point the same way. In a national surgical quality database of 6682 lumpectomies and 3339 simple mastectomies, wound complications, bleeding and infection were all more frequent after mastectomy10. These are observational comparisons, and the choice of operation was not randomised.

Age is the argument most often raised against conservation, and the data do not support it. In a Danish population cohort of 9,285 women younger than 50, survival after breast-conserving therapy matched survival after radical mastectomy in every age band, despite more local recurrence11. Young age does predict in-breast recurrence12,13. In-breast recurrence is in turn associated with later distant metastasis, and whether it is a marker or a cause remains unclear14. Germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 variants were found in 9% of unselected young women undergoing conservation15, which bears on the operation when the result is available in time.

The inference is the Fisher hypothesis and it is the intellectual foundation of everything that follows. The extent of local surgery determines local control. It does not determine systemic outcome, because distant disease is established or not established before the operation. The history of how that idea displaced the centrifugal model is in Halsted, the centrifugal model, and the logic of radical surgery and Fisher, the alternative hypothesis, and the birth of the randomized trial.

Mastectomy remains the right operation for multicentric disease, for an unfavourable ratio of tumour to breast volume, for inability to receive radiation, and for patients who prefer it. None of those indications rests on a survival argument, and it is worth being explicit with patients that mastectomy is not the safer cancer operation.

Contralateral prophylactic mastectomy has the weakest survival case of all. In registry data it was associated with better breast cancer-specific survival only in women aged 18 to 49 with stage I or II ER-negative disease16. Its association with lower non-cancer mortality was stronger still, which points to selection rather than benefit17. A decision model put the absolute 20-year survival gain below 1% in every group it modelled18. A meta-analysis found that it reduced contralateral cancer without improving survival in women with familial or genetic risk19. An accompanying editorial summarised the position as caveat emptor20.

2 · Margins, re-excision, and the evidence for less

A margin is a property of the specimen and of how the pathologist sampled its surface. It is not a property of the tumour. The surface being assessed was created by the operation, and the tissue on the other side of it is still in the patient.

The consensus standard for invasive disease is no ink on tumour. It was derived from a systematic review and meta-analysis of 33 studies including 28,162 patients21,22. Positive margins roughly doubled the risk of ipsilateral breast tumour recurrence. Wider clear margins did not reduce that risk further. The absence of additional benefit held in young patients, in lobular cancers and in tumours with an extensive intraductal component.

Ductal carcinoma in situ has a different standard, and the difference is instructive rather than arbitrary. A parallel consensus based on 20 studies including 7883 patients set 2 mm as the threshold, because narrower negative margins carried higher recurrence and wider ones did not carry lower23. In situ disease spreads along ducts in a pattern that a two-dimensional margin assessment samples poorly, which is why the empirical threshold sits further out.

Adopting the invasive standard changed practice measurably. At one centre, re-excision after breast-conserving surgery for stage I or II invasive disease fell from 21.4% to 15.1% after the guideline was adopted24. That is a single-institution before-and-after comparison and should be read as one.

The reason a narrow margin is acceptable is not that residual disease is absent. It is that residual microscopic disease is the target of the radiation and the systemic therapy that follow. A margin policy is therefore a statement about the whole treatment package rather than about the operation alone.

3 · Axillary staging and the long retreat from dissection

Each step in this retreat removed information rather than treatment, and understanding that is what makes the sequence coherent.

Dissection was standard because the node count set the stage. At least ten uninvolved nodes were needed to call a patient node-negative with confidence25,26. In a national database, omitting dissection from breast conservation was associated with worse survival, an effect its authors judged not solely due to understaging27.

Sentinel node biopsy replaced dissection for staging. NSABP B-32 randomised 5611 women, and 3989 of them had pathologically negative sentinel nodes28. In that group the hazard ratio for overall survival comparing sentinel biopsy alone with biopsy plus dissection was 1.20, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.96 to 1.50. Eight-year overall survival was 91.8% and 90.3%. The trial had been designed with a standardised sentinel technique so that survival and regional control could be compared directly29. The first randomised comparison, in 516 women in Milan, had shown sentinel node status to be 96.9% accurate against full dissection30. None of the 167 women spared dissection there had developed overt axillary disease at that first report. At a median follow-up of 79 months, one had, against eight expected31. Breast cancer events numbered 18 after dissection and 16 after sentinel biopsy alone, and five-year overall survival did not differ significantly.

Dissection was then dropped for limited sentinel node involvement. ACOSOG Z0011 randomised 891 women with one or two involved sentinel nodes, all treated by lumpectomy and whole-breast radiation32. Ten-year overall survival was 86.3% without dissection and 83.6% with it, against a prespecified non-inferiority margin of 1.3 on the hazard ratio. At the first report, five-year overall survival had been 92.5% and 91.8%33. Ten-year locoregional recurrence was 5.3% without dissection and 6.2% with it34,35.

IBCSG 23-01 made the same step for micrometastases. It randomised 934 patients with one or more sentinel micrometastases, and omitting dissection had no adverse effect on survival36.

AMAROS asked whether radiation could replace dissection rather than whether treatment could be dropped. Among 1425 patients with a positive sentinel node, the 10-year cumulative incidence of axillary recurrence was 0.93% after dissection and 1.82% after axillary radiotherapy37,38. The hazard ratio was 1.71 with a wide interval of 0.67 to 4.39. There was no difference in overall or disease-free survival. Lymphoedema at five years was 24.5% after dissection and 11.9% after radiotherapy. OTOASOR made the same comparison with regional nodal irradiation in place of completion dissection. Axillary recurrence was 2.0% after dissection and 1.7% after irradiation39.

SENOMAC extended the omission to larger tumours and to macrometastases. Among 2540 patients analysed per protocol, estimated five-year recurrence-free survival was 89.7% with sentinel biopsy only and 88.7% with completion dissection40. The hazard ratio was 0.89 with an interval of 0.66 to 1.19.

SOUND removed axillary surgery altogether in small tumours with a negative axillary ultrasound. Among 1405 women, five-year distant disease-free survival was 97.7% with sentinel biopsy and 98.0% with no axillary surgery41. Nodal involvement was present in 13.7% of the patients who had surgery, so the trial did not avoid nodal disease. It declined to measure it.

INSEMA asked the same question in breast-conserving surgery for clinically node-negative disease of 5 cm or less, and it is the largest of the three. Its design is the one to notice. It randomised three to one against surgery, which buys precision where the uncertainty actually sits.

The reason this sequence was possible is that the value of nodal information fell. Adjuvant systemic therapy is now selected largely by tumour biology and by genomic assays Genomic assays and the decision to omit chemotherapy, so counting nodes changes the plan less often than it once did. Occult sentinel metastases found only by immunohistochemistry were not associated with overall survival in Z001042.

Preoperative imaging can take over part of the staging. In a prospective study of 100 patients, ultrasound-guided aspiration had a sensitivity of 79.4% and a positive predictive value of 100%43. Its authors estimated that it could avoid sentinel biopsy in 54% of cases.

Caution

Read what was actually omitted. In ACOSOG Z0011 every patient received tangential whole-breast irradiation, and tangential fields treat part of the axilla. In SENOMAC, nodal radiation was given to 89.9% of the sentinel-only group40.

Neither trial shows that an untreated involved axilla is safe. Both show that surgical clearance is unnecessary when the axilla is being irradiated. Those are different claims, and the second one does not transfer to a patient who will not receive nodal radiation.

The non-inferiority margins here also deserve inspection rather than assumption, and Designing de-escalation trials and the non-inferiority margin problem is where the book examines them.

4 · Axillary management after neoadjuvant therapy

Chemotherapy changes the axilla, and that creates a staging problem that does not exist before treatment. Lymphatic channels are disrupted and residual disease is patchy, so the sentinel node represents the axilla less reliably.

ACOSOG Z1071 measured the size of the problem. Among 525 patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease who had two or more sentinel nodes removed after chemotherapy, the false-negative rate was 12.6%, with a 90% Bayesian credible interval of 9.85% to 16.05%44. Nodal pathological complete response occurred in 41.0% of evaluable patients, which is why the question matters at all.

SENTINA found the same order of magnitude and located the failure more precisely. In the cohort that converted from clinically node-positive to clinically node-negative, the detection rate was 80.1% and the false-negative rate was 14.2%45. The rate was 24.3% when a single node was removed and 18.5% when two were removed. Repeating sentinel biopsy after chemotherapy in patients who had already had one before it performed badly, with a detection rate of 60.8% and a false-negative rate of 51.6%.

Technique therefore matters more here than anywhere else in axillary surgery. Dual tracer, retrieval of three or more nodes, and clipping the biopsy-proven node so it can be targeted all lower the false-negative rate.

Each fix has been measured. With immunohistochemistry mandatory and isolated tumour cells counted as positive, the SN FNAC study found a false-negative rate of 8.4%46. In ACOSOG Z1071, when the clipped node was retrieved within the sentinel specimen, the rate was 6.8%47. Targeted axillary dissection, removing the marked node with the sentinel nodes, gave a false-negative rate of 2.0% in 85 patients who also had a completion dissection48.

The question then moved from surgery to radiation. In NSABP B-51, 1641 patients with biopsy-proven nodal disease who reached ypN0 after neoadjuvant chemotherapy were randomised to regional nodal irradiation or not, and 1556 were included in the primary-event analysis49. Freedom from invasive recurrence or breast cancer death did not differ, with a hazard ratio of 0.88 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.60 to 1.28. Five-year point estimates were 92.7% and 91.8%.

Caution

A false-negative rate is a property of a procedure and a definition, and it is calculable only in patients who also had a completion dissection. It quantifies staging accuracy. It does not quantify the consequence of being missed, because the missed disease in these trials was then removed surgically and the patients went on to receive radiation and systemic therapy.

The clinically relevant question is what proportion of patients are harmed by the error, and no false-negative rate answers that.

5 · Oncoplastic surgery

Oncoplastic surgery combines tumour excision with reshaping, either by displacing local tissue into the defect or by importing tissue from elsewhere. The claim it makes is geometric rather than biological. It widens the range of tumours that can be removed with an acceptable cosmetic result, and it does not alter the disease.

The techniques are classified by excision volume. Below about 20% of breast volume, local reshaping is enough, and above it a mammoplasty is needed50. Volume-displacement techniques borrowed from reduction surgery extend conservation to complex cases51, including central tumours once treated by mastectomy52. In a prospective comparison of 30 oncoplastic operations with 30 quadrantectomies, the oncoplastic excisions removed more tissue and achieved negative margins more often, in 25 against 1753.

For a medical oncologist the consequences are downstream and practical. Tissue rearrangement moves the tumour bed away from the skin scar, so surgical clips placed at the time of excision become the only reliable record of where the tumour was. Radiation boost targeting and partial-breast planning both depend on that record Whole-breast, partial-breast, and chest wall irradiation.

Re-excision is harder after rearrangement, because the original cavity no longer exists as a discrete space. That raises the cost of a positive margin and makes intraoperative margin assessment more valuable in these operations than in a simple lumpectomy.

The evidence base is observational. No randomised trial compares oncoplastic with standard breast-conserving surgery for disease outcome, and the comparison would be difficult to design because the populations differ by construction. Claims that oncoplastic technique improves oncological outcome should be read with that in mind.

6 · Reconstruction options and aesthetic outcomes

The choice is between implant-based and autologous reconstruction, and between immediate and delayed timing. The techniques themselves are described in surgical reviews54,55,56,57. Patient-reported outcome data are now good enough to quote.

One prospective multicentre cohort followed 2013 women undergoing immediate reconstruction, of whom 1217 completed questionnaires at two years58. Autologous reconstruction scored higher than implant reconstruction on the BREAST-Q scales after adjustment for baseline characteristics. The adjusted differences were 7.94 points for satisfaction with breasts, 3.27 for psychosocial well-being and 5.53 for sexual well-being, each on a 0 to 100 scale.

Radiation changes the calculation rather than adding to it uniformly. In the same consortium, at least one breast complication occurred by two years in 38.9% of irradiated implant patients and 25.6% of irradiated autologous patients59. The corresponding figures without radiation were 21.8% and 28.3%. Among irradiated patients, autologous reconstruction carried lower odds of complication, at 0.47 with an interval of 0.27 to 0.82. Among unirradiated patients the two approaches did not differ.

Older series point the same way. In one institution's 1,037 patients, radiation raised the major complication rate of expander-implant reconstruction from 21.2% to 45.4% and did not change it for autologous reconstruction60. Immediate free flap reconstruction followed by radiation produced late complications in 87.5%, against 8.6% when reconstruction waited until radiation was complete61. A review found implant complication rates above 40% with post-mastectomy radiation, and recommended delayed autologous reconstruction, or a temporary expander when radiation is uncertain62. A small series of irradiated pedicled flaps reported no flap losses at 18 months63. A prospective study identified three factors that predicted failure of immediate expander-implant reconstruction followed by radiotherapy64.

The interaction also runs from reconstruction to radiation. An immediate reconstruction compromised the radiation plan in 52% of patients in one analysis, most in left-sided cancers65.

Patient factors carry their own risk. Smoking, obesity, hypertension and age over 65 were independent risk factors for complications of expander-implant reconstruction66. After skin-sparing mastectomy with an immediate implant, implants were lost in 32% of obese smokers and 27% of women with larger breasts67. Breast size, previous irradiation and repeated implants were independent risk factors for expander infection68. Acellular dermal matrix added seroma and reconstructive failure in some series69,70, and gave a low complication rate in selected single-stage reconstructions in another71.

Skin-sparing mastectomy has now been followed long enough to judge. Local recurrence was 5.5% among 565 cancers in one series72, 4.5% at a median of 73 months in another73, and 6.2% in a third, where most recurrences were palpable skin-flap masses74. An early series of 50 patients had none75. In locally advanced disease, local recurrence was 4% in a prospective cohort and 2.6% in a retrospective one76,77. Against modified radical mastectomy, local recurrence at 101 months was 10.4% after skin-sparing, 11.7% after nipple-sparing and 11.5% after conventional mastectomy78.

Nipple-sparing mastectomy adds the question of occult nipple involvement. One review put occult nipple malignancy at 11.5%, more likely within 2 cm of the nipple, and nipple recurrence at 0.9%79. A systematic review of 3,331 total skin-sparing mastectomies found locoregional recurrence of 2.8% where follow-up reached two years, and some nipple necrosis in 9.1%80. At one European centre, 1,001 nipple-sparing mastectomies with nipple radiotherapy produced no recurrences in the nipple81. Early single-centre experience and reviews reached similar conclusions, and patients reported little nipple sensation afterwards82,83,84.

Reconstruction should not delay treatment. Among low-income women with late-stage disease, an interval of 60 days or more between diagnosis and first treatment was associated with worse overall and breast cancer-specific survival85.

In practice

Decide the radiation plan before the reconstructive plan, not after. The interaction above runs in one direction only, and a patient who will need post-mastectomy radiation faces a different choice from one who will not.

Say that reconstruction is usually a sequence of operations rather than one. Revision is expected rather than exceptional, and patients who were told otherwise experience it as a complication.

Nipple-sparing and skin-sparing techniques change the reconstructive result and not the oncological one, provided the margins are clear. Treat them as preference-sensitive.

Delayed reconstruction remains available. Framing immediate reconstruction as the only route pushes a permanent decision into the week of a cancer diagnosis.

7 · Functional morbidity and surgical de-escalation

Every de-escalation in this chapter was made for a reason, and the reason is in this section. If a trial reports non-inferior cancer outcomes and does not measure function, it has answered half the question it was asked.

Lymphoedema is the dominant morbidity. In a systematic review of 72 studies the pooled incidence of unilateral arm lymphoedema after breast cancer was 16.6%, with a 95% confidence interval of 13.6% to 20.2%86. That figure pools all 72 studies. Restricted to the 30 prospective cohorts the estimate rose to 21.4%, with an interval of 14.9% to 29.8%. That restricted estimate is what supports the authors' own headline that more than one in five women who survive breast cancer develop arm lymphoedema. The two are cuts of one review rather than competing numbers. The framing used elsewhere in this book is the prospective-cohort cut Lymphedema and shoulder dysfunction. The estimate also depended heavily on how lymphoedema was defined and measured, reaching 28.2% in studies using more than one diagnostic method. Incidence after axillary dissection was about 19.9% against about 5.6% after sentinel node biopsy.

Randomised data give the same contrast within a single trial. In ALMANAC, 1031 women were randomised to sentinel node biopsy or standard axillary treatment, and at 12 months the relative risk of lymphoedema was 0.37, with absolute rates of 5% and 13%87. Sensory loss showed the same relative risk, at 11% against 31%. Arm functioning and quality of life scores were better throughout the first year. At 18 months, substantial arm swelling was reported by 14% after standard treatment and 7% after sentinel biopsy88.

The other trials agree. In B-32, an arm volume difference of 10% or more at 36 months occurred in 14% after dissection and 8% after sentinel biopsy alone89. Patient-reported arm symptoms were worse after dissection, and mostly resolved over time90. The SNAC trial and a Danish prospective cohort found less arm morbidity with sentinel-based management91,92. In Z0011, adverse surgical effects were reported in 70% after dissection and 25% after sentinel biopsy alone93. Arm oedema is not only an axillary complication. In B-04 it was recorded at least once in 39.1% of women treated by total mastectomy alone94.

Substituting radiation for dissection reduces but does not abolish the problem. AMAROS reported lymphoedema at five years in 24.5% after dissection and 11.9% after axillary radiotherapy38.

Caution

Lymphoedema incidence is not a fixed number. It is a function of the definition, the measurement method and the duration of follow-up, and those three vary more across studies than the surgery does86.

Quoting a single incidence figure to a patient without saying how it was measured overstates the precision of what is known. The comparison between procedures is robust. The absolute number is not.

8 · Can surgery be omitted after exceptional response

This is the last surgical de-escalation and it is not yet established. The logic is the same as elsewhere in Part VIII. If neoadjuvant therapy has eradicated the tumour, the operation removes nothing.

The best available evidence is a single-arm phase 2 trial. Fifty patients with triple-negative or HER2-positive disease had a residual imaging lesion smaller than 2 cm after neoadjuvant therapy95. Each underwent image-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy of the tumour bed with a minimum of 12 cores. No residual invasive or in situ disease was found in 31 patients, which is 62% with a 95% confidence interval of 47.2% to 75.4%. Those 31 patients had radiotherapy and no breast surgery, and at a median follow-up of 26.4 months none had an ipsilateral breast tumour recurrence.

Four limitations should travel with that result. The denominator for the recurrence finding is 31 patients. The follow-up is short relative to the recurrence risk in these subtypes. Selection was narrow, covering unicentric disease with a small residual lesion in two chemosensitive subtypes. And the result rests on a specific biopsy protocol rather than on the idea of biopsy.

Caution

A negative biopsy is evidence about the volume that was sampled. Treating it as evidence about the whole tumour bed is the identical error to reading a single core as representative of a heterogeneous tumour, and the argument is made in full in Spatial heterogeneity.

The question this approach raises is not whether a pathological complete response predicts good outcome. It does. The question is whether a biopsy can identify a pathological complete response reliably enough to act on, and that is a sampling question rather than a biological one.

Answering it requires a randomised comparison with an explicit non-inferiority margin, in a population where the event rate is low. The design problem that creates is developed in Designing de-escalation trials and the non-inferiority margin problem, and the conditions under which the neoadjuvant setting can support such a design are in Response-adapted and de-escalation designs.

Interplay

Surgical de-escalation and the sampling problem are the same argument arriving from opposite directions. Part VI asks what a biopsy represents when a tumour is spatially heterogeneous. This chapter asks what an operation is for when the tumour may already be gone. Both questions reduce to how much of the disease a sampled volume can speak for, and neither has an assay-level answer.
