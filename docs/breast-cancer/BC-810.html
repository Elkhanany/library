Neoadjuvant therapy in hormone receptor-positive disease

The one subtype where the neoadjuvant platform works least well, and what to measure once the endpoint that justifies it elsewhere has failed.

1 · Scope, and why the neoadjuvant setting is harder in this subtype

This chapter covers preoperative systemic therapy for operable and locally advanced hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. Hormone receptor-positive HER2-positive disease is a different entity with its own algorithm, and it has its own chapter in Early triple-positive disease.

Four things are left to neighbours deliberately. The machinery of the neoadjuvant platform, and the surrogacy argument attached to pathological complete response, belong to The neoadjuvant setting as a scientific platform and Endpoints, surrogates, and the overall survival question. Adjuvant endocrine therapy belongs to Adjuvant endocrine therapy in hormone receptor-positive disease, and adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition to Adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition. The decision to omit chemotherapy on a genomic assay is made on the surgical specimen and belongs to Genomic assays and the decision to omit chemotherapy.

The organising fact of this chapter is uncomfortable. Everywhere else in early breast cancer the neoadjuvant setting earns its place because a pathological complete response is both achievable and informative. Here it is neither, because complete responses in this subtype are uncommon. The pooled analysis that established the prognostic value of the endpoint found the association weakest in exactly this group1. A separate pooled analysis of seven randomised trials went further. Complete response was associated with disease-free survival in luminal B HER2-negative tumours. It was not associated with disease-free survival in luminal A tumours at all2.

The endpoint that justifies preoperative treatment elsewhere therefore does not justify it here. That does not make the setting useless. It changes both the reason for entering it and what should be measured on the way out.

Early disease · HR+/HER2- · neoadjuvant

The preoperative window as a measurement and as a treatment

Preoperative treatment in this cell serves as an experiment at least as much as a treatment. Pathological complete response is rare here, so nearly every endpoint read before surgery is a proliferation measurement standing in for an outcome. Almost every question at this position turns on whether that substitution holds, and on whether a tumour's early response can be trusted to decide what happens after the operation.

How far can proliferation be suppressed before surgery, and does the proliferation that survives treatment predict recurrence?

Rationale
Oestrogen drives the cell cycle in this cell, so the fraction of cells still dividing after a short exposure is the most direct read-out of whether the drug is working. A pretreatment biopsy says how fast the tumour was growing. A biopsy taken on treatment says how much of that growth oestrogen was responsible for. CDK4/6 sits immediately downstream of the receptor, so a tumour still proliferating on an aromatase inhibitor is the obvious place to put a cell-cycle drug.
Experiment
Paired biopsies taken before and during preoperative treatment, scored centrally for Ki67. The largest trial randomised a short perioperative course of an aromatase inhibitor against no perioperative treatment, with time to recurrence as its primary endpoint and the paired Ki67 only as a secondary objective. Smaller trials added a CDK4/6 inhibitor to an aromatase inhibitor, either as a randomised arm or as a within-patient step after an endocrine-only run-in, and one ongoing trial profiles serial biopsies at single-cell resolution.
Finding
Endocrine therapy alone leaves a proliferating fraction in most tumours. How large that fraction is after two weeks separates patients across a wide span of recurrence risk, while the two weeks of treatment themselves changed no outcome. The three aromatase inhibitors are indistinguishable on this read-out, so it reports the class and not the molecule. Adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor drives most tumours below the arrest threshold, far more of them than an aromatase inhibitor does alone. The arrest reverses once the drug is withdrawn.
Limitation
Complete cell cycle arrest is an assay threshold, not a biological state. The cut point was calibrated on tumours given endocrine therapy alone, so it was built to grade endocrine dependence. A drug that stops the cycle whether or not the tumour depends on oestrogen crosses that line in nearly everyone, which is precisely what destroys its power to select. The thresholds in use are not even the same number from trial to trial, and local and central scoring of one specimen disagree often enough to move a patient across one. The within-patient designs also confound the added drug with longer time on treatment, because the second biopsy is both on more drug and later.

Does suppressing proliferation more deeply produce a better tumour response at surgery?

Rationale
Ki67 was adopted before surgery because it is quick, cheap and readable in a core biopsy. The whole case for using it rests on the assumption that it stands in for something the patient experiences. If a drug that suppresses proliferation far more does not shrink tumours more or leave less disease behind, then the surrogate and the outcome have come apart. The surrogate is then the thing to distrust.
Experiment
Trials that reported a proliferation endpoint and a clinical or pathological endpoint out of the same randomisation. One added a CDK4/6 inhibitor to letrozole and reported the change in Ki67 beside the clinical response rate. Others carried the combination through a full preoperative course against chemotherapy and reported residual cancer burden, pathological complete response and a composite prognostic index scored on the surgical specimen. One asked directly whether early proliferation suppression converted into that index.
Finding
The proliferation endpoints separated the arms decisively. The clinical and pathological endpoints did not follow. Where both came out of one randomisation, a large difference in Ki67 sat beside a clinical response rate that was no different. In the comparison against chemotherapy the two arms changed places depending on which endpoint was read, chemotherapy ahead on residual disease and the combination ahead on the composite index. Where early suppression was tested directly as a predictor of that index, it did not deliver one.
Limitation
Pathological complete response sits near its floor in this cell, so a trial powered on it has almost no room to move and a null result there means very little. The composite index is not independent of the surrogate, because proliferation is one of its own components, so a drug that suppresses proliferation improves the index partly by construction. The trial that tested the translation most directly has conference and registry material behind it and no peer-reviewed report, so its negative result cannot be weighed as a published trial result. The immune and stromal profiling is single-arm, so what it records on treatment cannot be separated from what would have happened over the same interval anyway.

Can the tumour's response to preoperative treatment be used to withhold chemotherapy?

Rationale
Most of this cell gains little from cytotoxic treatment. The difficulty is telling who. A baseline genomic score infers endocrine dependence from the untreated tumour, while a short preoperative course tests it. A tumour whose proliferation collapses under an aromatase inhibitor has demonstrated the dependence the score was trying to predict.
Experiment
Response-adapted designs: a short endocrine run-in, a central Ki67 read against a prespecified threshold, and a route to chemotherapy for the tumours that stay above it. One programme combined that window with a genomic score and tested whether the endocrine-only group was noninferior. Others dropped chemotherapy altogether in favour of a CDK4/6 inhibitor and compared what was left at surgery, by molecular risk score or by cell-cycle arrest.
Finding
Response does separate the population, and in the direction the biology predicts. Patients routed to endocrine treatment alone on a low genomic score and a responding window did as well as the low-risk control group. Only a minority of tumours reach the endocrine-sensitive state these designs define, whichever endocrine drug is used to produce it. The trials that replaced chemotherapy with a CDK4/6 inhibitor reached the same molecular and proliferative state at surgery as chemotherapy did, at a lower cost in serious adverse events.
Limitation
In the response-guided programme the groups were allocated by score and by response rather than by randomisation, so the comparison is between two differently selected populations and not between two ways of treating the same patient. Its window is preoperative, but the therapy decision it drives is adjuvant, so it does not test preoperative treatment at all. In the chemotherapy-replacement trials the arms produced similar estimates. Similar estimates are not noninferiority, and none of those trials carried a noninferiority margin or the size to test one. The trial designed to detect a doubling of cell-cycle arrest over chemotherapy did not find it, and it randomised only the tumours whose proliferation stayed up after an endocrine run-in, a deliberately endocrine-resistant group and a small fraction of those registered. The trial built to settle the question on distant control has published a design paper and nothing else, so nothing on its record is a result.

Does a better receptor-directed drug improve the tumour's early biological response more than an aromatase inhibitor?

Rationale
The first preoperative comparisons put an aromatase inhibitor against tamoxifen and found more tumour response with the aromatase inhibitor, in postmenopausal women and, with ovarian suppression, in premenopausal women. So the receptor axis had headroom before surgery. Changing between the aromatase inhibitors does not exploit it, because they behave alike. Degrading the receptor rather than starving it should shut down more signalling, and it should keep working where the receptor is mutated.
Experiment
Short preoperative windows of two or four weeks, each ending in a central Ki67 score. One randomised an oral receptor degrader against anastrozole before putting both groups on the same combination. One randomised two doses of a second oral degrader against letrozole. One was a single arm read against an arrest threshold. Against these sits a full-length preoperative phase III trial of an injectable degrader, given alone and added to an aromatase inhibitor, with a prognostic composite scored at surgery.
Finding
One oral degrader suppressed proliferation more than anastrozole over two weeks. The other matched letrozole without beating it. The single-arm study arrested the cycle in a minority of the tumours it treated. Over a full preoperative course the injectable degrader did not improve on an aromatase inhibitor on the trial's own prognostic endpoint, given alone or added to it.
Limitation
A window study is small because a fortnight of treatment is all its design asks for, and its endpoint is a ratio of two Ki67 scores. That ratio measures what the drug does to proliferation. It has never been shown to buy a lower recurrence rate, so a better ratio is a better ratio and nothing more. The comparisons are also not on one footing. The older agent trials scored tumour response by callipers and by ultrasound, and the two methods gave different response rates inside the same trial. One of them permitted concurrent chemotherapy, which contaminates the endocrine comparison. One window study stopped early for slow accrual, and in another the primary comparison rests on the subset with paired central scores rather than on everyone randomised. The phase III trial enrolled a broader population and read a different endpoint entirely, so it does not settle the window question and the window results do not overturn it.

Does invasive lobular carcinoma need its own preoperative endocrine experiment?

Rationale
Lobular tumours lose E-cadherin and infiltrate in single files rather than forming a discrete mass. They are almost always receptor-positive and low grade, and they respond poorly to cytotoxic treatment. Both halves of a neoadjuvant argument therefore change. Endocrine therapy becomes the plausible option rather than one of two, and a tumour with no discrete mass cannot be measured by callipers or by imaging the way a ductal tumour can.
Experiment
A randomised short preoperative exposure to tamoxifen, to an aromatase inhibitor or to fulvestrant in lobular disease, read out as target engagement rather than as tumour response. Separately, a randomised preoperative trial of adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor to endocrine therapy that enrolled receptor-positive disease of any histology, so that lobular and ductal tumours could be compared inside one trial.
Finding
Neither has reported the comparison it was built to make. The short-exposure study has conference and registry material only, and nothing on its record may be read as a published outcome. The only publication on the randomised trial's record is its axillary analysis, which found that clearing the axilla added nothing over sentinel node biopsy in node-positive disease after preoperative endocrine therapy.
Limitation
The short-exposure study is a phase 0 design and it measures whether a drug reaches and engages its target. It cannot rank endocrine agents for efficacy, and reading it that way is the error its design invites. The randomised trial is named for lobular cancer but enrolled any histology, and lobular tumours were a minority of the patients in its published analysis, so it is a comparison inside a mixed population rather than a lobular trial. The endpoints of that publication are ones the paper itself calls prespecified exploratory, so they say nothing about the trial's systemic question. No other trial at this position enrolled a lobular-only population, so the histology argument rests on these two and on subgroups borrowed from elsewhere.

TBCRC 037 PELOPS

Can checkpoint blockade or an antibody-drug conjugate raise response where endocrine escalation has not?

Rationale
Luminal tumours are usually immunologically quiet. The high-grade, receptor-low end of this cell is not, and an adaptive platform trial had already reported a pembrolizumab signal across HER2-negative disease rather than in triple-negative disease alone. Separately, most receptor-positive tumours express HER2 below the amplification threshold, which an antibody-drug conjugate can use as an address rather than as a driver.
Experiment
Two phase III trials added a checkpoint inhibitor to anthracycline and taxane-based preoperative chemotherapy in luminal disease, with pathological complete response as a primary endpoint and event-free survival to follow. Alongside them sit a randomised trial of preoperative radiation dose as an immune primer before checkpoint blockade, and a preoperative trial of an antibody-drug conjugate given with and without an aromatase inhibitor in receptor-positive, HER2-low disease rather than HER2-amplified disease.
Finding
Both phase III trials raised pathological complete response. In both, the increase concentrated where PD-L1 was expressed. The event-free survival that decides whether the increase matters is immature in both. The radiation-priming trial and the antibody-drug conjugate trial have conference and registry material only, so neither yet has a result to read.
Limitation
Pathological complete response is not the surrogate here that it is in triple-negative disease, so a gain in it does not convert into an expectation about recurrence. One of the two phase III trials selected explicitly on grade, on low oestrogen receptor expression and on PD-L1, which is the end of this cell that behaves least like the rest of it, so its result does not generalise to the ordinary luminal tumour. The PD-L1 result there is scored with a single antibody clone against a single cut-off, and a group defined by a marker is not a randomised comparison. The single-arm profiling study at this position found that a CDK4/6 inhibitor on an endocrine backbone diminished adaptive anti-tumour immunity, which is a reason to doubt that the two escalation strategies combine, and a single-arm observation is not a test of that. The radiation-priming trial holds its receptor-positive and triple-negative cohorts separately, so nothing there may be read as a pooled population.
Next question
Does a gain in pathological response, in a cell where pathological response is rare, convert into fewer distant recurrences, and is any trial at this position designed to answer that?

2 · Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and the low pathological complete response rate

Two separate facts with different causes and different consequences are usually run together here.

The first is that complete responses are uncommon. That is a property of the disease rather than of the regimen. Slowly proliferating, oestrogen-driven tumours are not eradicated by cytotoxic therapy at the rate that highly proliferative ones are. Intensifying the regimen moves the rate a little, without moving it into the range where a binary endpoint discriminates usefully between patients.

The second is that, within this subtype, achieving a complete response separates outcomes less sharply than it does elsewhere1. The two facts compound each other, because few patients reach the category and reaching it says less about them than it would in triple-negative disease.

The practical consequence is that the reason for giving chemotherapy before surgery here is almost never the response itself. It is downstaging of the breast tumour to permit conservation, or downstaging of the axilla, or the logistics of a delayed operation. All three are legitimate reasons, provided the expectation set with the patient before the first cycle matches whichever one applies.

What the surgical specimen is for is a separate question whose answer is not the binary endpoint. Residual cancer burden is continuous and remains prognostic in this subtype, with a shallower gradient than in triple-negative disease25,26. The index and its measurement caveats are set out in Residual cancer burden and continuous response measures. A report giving only the presence or absence of a complete response has discarded the part of the measurement that still carries information here Residual cancer burden.

Caution

A low complete response rate is routinely read as chemoresistance, which is a different claim. Adjuvant chemotherapy reduces recurrence in selected hormone receptor-positive disease, in populations where almost nobody would have achieved a complete response had they been treated preoperatively Chemotherapy selection when it is indicated. Sensitivity measured by eradication and benefit measured by recurrence are different quantities. The first systematically understates the second whenever the tumour grows slowly.

3 · Neoadjuvant endocrine therapy and who it suits

Neoadjuvant endocrine therapy is the one preoperative strategy in this subtype whose rationale never depended on a pathological endpoint.

The choice of agent is settled and the answer is dull. A randomised comparison of the three aromatase inhibitors found no difference between them in proliferation suppression or in the post-treatment prognostic index. It carried two of them forward on clinical response alone19. Adding fulvestrant to an aromatase inhibitor does not improve on it either, which a phase 3 trial established in more than a thousand women5.

Who it suits follows from the biology rather than from the trials. Strongly oestrogen receptor-positive, low-grade tumours are the ones that respond. Lobular carcinomas are over-represented among large operable tumours and under-represented among complete responders, so they are a natural fit. Older or less fit patients are the group in whom the toxicity comparison against chemotherapy is most one-sided.

Duration is where practice most often goes wrong, because response accrues over months rather than weeks. The trials that read out used four to six months of exposure. There is no reason to expect an eight-week course to produce comparable downstaging. Shortened courses are the commonest avoidable cause of a disappointing result.

In practice

Fix the goal before the first prescription. Surgical downstaging and measurement of endocrine sensitivity are different objectives with different stopping rules.

Plan at least four months, reassessing by examination and imaging at intervals rather than waiting for a fixed date.

Take a baseline Ki67 and a repeat at two to four weeks only if the result will change a decision. If it will not, the biopsy is not justified by the information.

Treat progression on neoadjuvant endocrine therapy as a significant event rather than an ordinary one. It is uncommon, and it identifies a tumour unlikely to be controlled by the same drug class given for the next five years.

4 · Ki67, PEPI and response measures for a setting where pCR fails

If the binary endpoint carries little information, something has to replace it. Two measures do, answering two different questions.

Ki67 measured after two to four weeks of endocrine therapy is a pharmacodynamic read-out. It reports whether proliferation in that patient's tumour is oestrogen-driven. The strongest evidence that the measurement carries prognostic weight comes from a trial whose intervention achieved nothing, which is worked out in Window-of-opportunity studies and short-term biologic endpoints. A two-week value separated five-year recurrence risk across a nearly fivefold range among treated HER2-negative women13.

The preoperative endocrine prognostic index answers the other question, which is what to do after surgery. It combines pathological tumour size, nodal status, Ki67 and oestrogen receptor status measured in the surgical specimen. It was derived in the P024 trial and validated in IMPACT. A score of zero identified a group at very low risk of relapse on endocrine therapy alone27. That is a prognostic statement and not a predictive one. It does not establish that chemotherapy would have added nothing, because nobody in either cohort was randomised to find out.

The measurement side deserves as much attention as the biology. Ki67 scoring varies between laboratories and observers even under a common protocol28, which is why successive working group statements concentrate on method rather than on cut-point29,30. The largest contemporary test of that is worth the arithmetic. In a trial using post-endocrine Ki67 to allocate treatment, local and central scoring agreed on the binary endocrine-response call for about four women in five31. One in five would have been allocated differently depending on which laboratory read the slide.

Two conventions coexist and they are not interchangeable. Complete cell-cycle arrest is set at a Ki67 of 2.7% or below, which is a pharmacodynamic threshold used in window studies. Endocrine response is set at 10% or below, which is an allocation threshold used to direct treatment. Neither boundary was placed where biology changes. Both were placed where observers could agree.

Caution

A fall in Ki67 is a change in a proliferation marker rather than a change in outcome. The single trial large enough to separate the two found a measurement that predicted recurrence and a treatment that prevented none of it13. Reading a suppressed Ki67 as evidence that the drug is helping that patient runs the two together.

5 · Neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition and window-of-opportunity designs

This is the cleanest demonstration in the book that arresting proliferation and eradicating a tumour are different events.

Adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor to an aromatase inhibitor suppresses proliferation well beyond what endocrine therapy achieves alone. The randomised trials of palbociclib and of abemaciclib show it in the same direction and at similar magnitude. Neither produced a clinical response advantage over the endocrine comparator, with pathological complete responses close to absent in both.

That combination is what a cytostatic agent should produce, because blocking the G1 restriction point stops cells dividing Cell cycle regulation. It does not kill them, so the tumour bed is still cellular at surgery. A trial judging such a drug by a pathological complete response will call an active agent inactive.

NeoPAL makes the point sharpest by disagreeing with itself. Judged on residual cancer burden, the chemotherapy arm did better, with 15.7% against 7.7% reaching class 0 or I. Judged on the preoperative endocrine prognostic index, the combination arm did better, with 17.6% against 8.0% reaching a score of zero. The same 106 randomised patients produce opposite rankings depending on which post-treatment measurement is chosen. Neither measurement has been validated against outcome in this setting.

One finding from these trials bears directly on adjuvant practice. In neoMONARCH, tumours that had reached complete cell-cycle arrest showed Ki67 rebound when treatment stopped more than four days before the specimen was taken12. Suppression requires continuing exposure, which makes the discontinuation and adherence questions in Toxicity, discontinuation, and the cost of two or three years questions about efficacy rather than about administration.

Window-of-opportunity designs sit alongside all of this. Their logic, and the reason a pharmacodynamic effect does not license an outcome claim, is worked out in Window-of-opportunity studies and short-term biologic endpoints using POETIC and is not repeated here.

6 · Immunotherapy in hormone receptor-positive early disease

Two phase 3 trials have added a checkpoint inhibitor to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in this subtype. Both raised the pathological complete response rate against placebo while remaining immature for event-free survival.

Whom they enrolled matters more than what they showed, because both restricted entry to high-grade disease. One admitted grade 2 tumours only where oestrogen receptor expression fell between 1% and 10%3. These are the tumours that behave least like typical luminal disease. Transferring the result to a grade 1, strongly receptor-positive tumour is not supported by either trial.

The effect concentrates further inside those populations. In CheckMate 7FL the response increment among PD-L1-positive tumours was roughly double the increment in the trial as a whole. That is a subgroup observation in a trial not powered for it, and Statistical and clinical significance are different claims sets out what such an observation can and cannot carry.

The endpoint problem now arrives at its sharpest point. A gain in pathological complete response has been demonstrated in the one subtype where that endpoint is least prognostic1,2. The gain may well translate into fewer recurrences. Nothing published so far shows that it has.

A practical conflict is already visible, because adjuvant abemaciclib and adjuvant ribociclib are options for the same high-risk population. Neither is given alongside a checkpoint inhibitor. A patient eligible for both escalations can have only one of them, and no trial compares the two.

Caution

The intuitive reading is that a doubled complete response rate in a poor-prognosis population must translate into fewer recurrences. In triple-negative disease that reading has been supported by subsequent survival data. Here the same inference is being drawn in the subtype where the trial-level link between response and outcome is weakest. Late recurrence also means the follow-up needed to test it is measured in decades rather than years Late recurrence risk and surveillance implications.

7 · Unresolved questions and what reads out next

Four questions are open, and only two of them are being asked by a trial.

The first is whether a response gain translates. Event-free survival from the two checkpoint inhibitor trials is the readout that will settle whether the argument in Immunotherapy in hormone receptor-positive early disease was pessimism or realism. A positive result would make pathological complete response useful in this subtype for the first time. A null result would confirm that the endpoint measured the wrong thing.

The second is whether endocrine response can take the place of chemotherapy in intermediate-risk disease. WSG ADAPTcycle allocates on a genomic score together with the post-endocrine Ki67 described in Ki67, PEPI and response measures for a setting where pCR fails, so its invasive disease-free survival readout tests the allocation rule and the treatment at once. That design makes a null result ambiguous, because the rule and the drug fail together.

The third is what CDK4/6 inhibition does to a human tumour over months rather than weeks. NEOLETRIB samples serially through six months of exposure, so its value is mechanistic rather than clinical.

The fourth question has no trial behind it. Nobody is testing whether preoperative treatment of any kind improves outcome over upfront surgery in this subtype. Nobody has prospectively validated a preoperative endocrine prognostic index of zero as a gate for omitting adjuvant chemotherapy. Both would change practice. Neither is being studied.

Interplay

Part VI argues that a tumour is a population rather than a value, and this chapter is where that argument has a bedside consequence for the commonest subtype. Every response measure used here averages over that population. A complete response asks whether the whole population was eradicated, which in slowly proliferating disease is almost never true. Residual cancer burden keeps the variance and is therefore the more informative instrument25. A two-week Ki67 measures the fraction of the population still cycling under oestrogen deprivation, which is a statement about composition rather than about size13. Read this way, the low complete response rate in hormone receptor-positive disease is not a failure of the drugs. It is what averaging over a heterogeneous, slowly dividing population looks like when the measurement is forced into two categories.
