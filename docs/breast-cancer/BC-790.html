1 · Scope, and how to read the evidence tables in this chapter

This chapter is the evidence compendium for adjuvant endocrine therapy. It collects the randomised trials that settled which agent, for how long, and with or without ovarian suppression. It ends on adherence, because that is the largest modifiable determinant of how much of the benefit is actually delivered.

Three neighbouring chapters carry what is deliberately absent from this one. The pharmacology of each agent class is in Endocrine therapy. The decision architecture for early hormone receptor-positive disease, including the genomic assays and the chemotherapy question, is in Adjuvant chemotherapy and the genomic assay decision. Adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition is a separate decision and has its own chapter in Adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition.

The table below is a filter on registry fields rather than on what a trial randomised. That distinction does more work here than anywhere else in the book, because endocrine therapy was a component of nearly every adjuvant trial run in this subtype for forty years. A trial enters the table if endocrine therapy was anywhere in its design. It did not have to be the variable.

Every randomised adjuvant endocrine trial in the registry for this subtype. Outcomes appear in the per-question tables that follow.

Sorted by what was actually randomised, the thirty-two rows fall into six groups. Five of them are the sections that follow: tamoxifen duration, aromatase inhibitor against tamoxifen, ovarian suppression, extension beyond five years, and the oral degraders. The sixth group has no section of its own, and its rows are worth naming so that none of them goes unexplained.

Four rows sit in the table because endocrine therapy was the constant rather than the variable. NSABP B-20 and SWOG-8814 / INT-0100 added chemotherapy to tamoxifen. WSG-PlanB randomised two chemotherapy regimens. Its assay-driven omission of chemotherapy sat outside that randomisation. WSG-ADAPT HR+/HER2− compared two assay-defined groups who both received endocrine therapy alone, which is a selection rule tested for non-inferiority rather than a randomised drug comparison. All four belong to Genomic assays and the decision to omit chemotherapy, and none of them randomised an endocrine decision.

They do carry one thing the endocrine sections cannot. The 21-gene recurrence score was built and validated in cohorts whose endocrine therapy was tamoxifen alone31,32. The assay now used to place a patient on endocrine therapy without chemotherapy was calibrated against a backbone most of those patients no longer receive. That is a measurement problem rather than a biological one, and Clinical and genomic risk assessment is where it is argued.

NSABP B-14 is the oldest row and the one the rest of the chapter stands on. It randomised tamoxifen against placebo in node-negative receptor-positive disease25. No trial since has carried a no-treatment arm in this population. Every comparison below is therefore between two active strategies, and the absolute benefit of endocrine therapy itself is measured nowhere in this chapter.

Two rows randomised nothing that bears on a drug choice. CALGB 9343 randomised radiotherapy in women aged seventy or older, with tamoxifen in both arms, and belongs to Omission in low-risk and older patients27. POSITIVE is a single-group study of a planned interruption to attempt pregnancy, with its comparison drawn from an external cohort rather than from randomisation28. It is discussed in Pregnancy after breast cancer and interrupting endocrine therapy.

The last two rows are the within-class question, and this section rather than the next one carries them because the filter below is built on the comparison against tamoxifen. FACE randomised letrozole against anastrozole in node-positive disease. MA.27 randomised exemestane against anastrozole under a two-sided superiority design. Neither trial separated the drugs on its primary endpoint. FATA-GIM3 randomised across the same three drugs inside a larger design and also separated them on nothing8. Three randomised comparisons agree that the choice among the three aromatase inhibitors is not an efficacy choice. It is a tolerability and interaction choice, and it should be made on that basis12,16.

What none of the three can show is the manoeuvre a clinic makes after an arthralgia. Nobody randomised a switch between aromatase inhibitors against continuing the first one. Moving a symptomatic patient from letrozole to exemestane therefore rests on the absence of an efficacy difference and on nothing else.

The overview table prints no result column at all, which is deliberate. Outcomes belong to the per-question tables below, where the control arm is stated next to them. Read the arms column of any generated table in this book before reading anything else in it.

Several of the sections below open with the question their trials were built to answer, taken from the [Chapter Stories](stories.html) map, where the whole landscape is laid out as questions. The division of labour is that tables enumerate and prose argues. A figure that sits in a table is not repeated in the text unless the argument is about that figure.

The duration question is not about how long the drug works. It is about how long its effect persists after it stops.

The table holds two rows and one result. ATLAS randomised continuation to ten years against stopping at five, and reported. aTTom sits in the registry as awaited, with no primary duration result on its record and its primary endpoint not stated there. The ten-against- five decision therefore rests on a single trial. The pooled reading of the two that circulates in review articles is an arithmetic performed outside either trial's own publication.

The accounting matters more than the size of the result. ATLAS counts recurrence and breast cancer mortality over years 5 to 1415. That window runs five years past the last tablet in the continuation arm. The hazard of recurrence in this subtype does not fall away when treatment stops33. A duration trial that closed its books at year ten would be reading the effect while it was still arriving.

That is the whole case for extending anything. It is the mirror image of the late recurrence hazard curve set out in Annual hazard of recurrence and the long tail of hormone receptor-positive disease. A treatment whose effect arrives late is worth giving only to a woman whose risk also arrives late.

Two features of the population constrain how the figures transfer. ATLAS randomised women already free of disease at five years, so the estimate is conditional on surviving that far. Its breast cancer outcomes are reported only in the women with ER-positive disease, and the remainder were ER-negative or never assayed, which reflects practice between 1996 and 200515.

The price of the second five years is mostly endometrial and thrombotic. Continuing to ten years raised endometrial cancer by a rate ratio of 1.74 and pulmonary embolus by 1.87, each against stopping at five15. Both are ratios of event rates. The absolute excess depends on the baseline incidence in the woman being counselled, which a ratio does not carry.

What the table cannot show is the comparison a postmenopausal woman is actually offered. ATLAS set continuation against stopping. Nobody randomised ten years of tamoxifen against five years of tamoxifen followed by an aromatase inhibitor, which is the sequence the next two sections make the default. The one analysis that does sit on the aTTom record asks instead whether an expression assay identifies the women who gain29, and Unresolved questions and what reads out next returns to it.

The patient-level overview of the first five years remains the anchor, and it established that a carry-over effect exists at all34. The earlier overviews had already shown that tamoxifen reduces recurrence and mortality in ER-positive and ER-unknown disease, and that the mortality reduction keeps growing to 15 years35,36.

HER2 was once proposed as a marker of tamoxifen resistance37,38,39. In adjuvant trials the benefit of tamoxifen did not depend on HER2 or p53 status40,41. In metastatic disease, HER2-positive tumours responded less to every endocrine treatment in a meta-analysis42, and HER2 amplification with HER1 expression marked lower ER levels and a modestly smaller tamoxifen response43. Quantitative measurement of ErbB-2 carried prognostic and predictive information of its own44. In TransATAC, low ER or PgR and high HER2 marked a higher recurrence risk with either drug, and anastrozole was better than tamoxifen in every molecular subgroup45. Tamoxifen is still the first choice in a premenopausal woman who will not receive ovarian suppression, and in anyone for whom bone loss is the dominant competing risk.

3 · Aromatase inhibitors against tamoxifen, and the menopausal status that decides

Early disease · HR+/HER2- · adjuvant

Choosing the intensity and the duration of endocrine treatment

Does the choice of aromatase inhibitor, or the order it is given in, change the outcome?

Rationale
Three aromatase inhibitors reached the clinic within a few years of each other, and two of them are non-steroidal while the third is a steroidal inactivator. The mechanistic difference was expected to produce a clinical one. The separate question was whether five years of an inhibitor beats two or three years of tamoxifen followed by one.
Experiment
Head-to-head randomisation of one inhibitor against another in the adjuvant setting, and separately, randomisation of an upfront inhibitor against a planned switch from tamoxifen after two or three years.
Finding
No aromatase inhibitor beat another. The switch strategies that randomised patients already disease-free on tamoxifen improved outcomes over continuing it, while the trials that randomised the whole strategy from the start found upfront and switch approaches equivalent.
Limitation
The two designs are not comparable and are routinely read as though they were. A trial that randomises at two years enrols only patients who tolerated tamoxifen and did not relapse on it. That is a selected population, and its prognosis is better than that of the population randomised at diagnosis. The head-to-head agent trials were powered to show superiority and cannot establish equivalence.

Menopausal status is not a patient characteristic in this decision. It is a statement about where oestrogen is being made, and therefore about which blockade will work.

In postmenopausal women an aromatase inhibitor outperforms tamoxifen. A patient-level meta-analysis of 31,920 women found that recurrence rate ratios favoured aromatase inhibitors while the treatments differed, at 0.70 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.64 to 0.77, and not significantly thereafter. Ten-year breast cancer mortality across all comparisons was reduced, with a rate ratio of 0.86 and an interval of 0.80 to 0.9446. The same analysis priced the trade. Ten-year endometrial cancer incidence was 0.4% against 1.2% with tamoxifen, and five-year bone fracture risk was 8.2% against 5.5%.

In premenopausal women the ovary is the dominant source, so an aromatase inhibitor alone does not work. It requires ovarian suppression, and the comparison then changes character. A patient-level meta-analysis of 7,030 women receiving ovarian suppression found that an aromatase inhibitor reduced breast cancer recurrence against tamoxifen, with a rate ratio of 0.79 and an interval of 0.69 to 0.90. The absolute reduction in five-year recurrence risk was 3.2 percentage points, from 10.1% to 6.9%. Breast cancer mortality did not differ, with a rate ratio of 1.01 and an interval of 0.82 to 1.24. Fractures were more common, at 6.4% against 5.1%47. The two estimates do not have the same shape. Mortality fell alongside recurrence in the postmenopausal comparison and did not under suppression, while a named toxicity rises in both. The agents themselves are covered in Endocrine therapy and the suppression trials in Ovarian function suppression and who benefits.

The six randomised trials underneath the postmenopausal estimate agree on one thing, and it is easy to lose in the detail. Wherever tamoxifen exposure is replaced by aromatase inhibition, recurrence falls. Wherever both arms finish on an aromatase inhibitor and only the order differs, nothing separates them. That is one finding about the class rather than six findings about six protocols.

The six divide by control arm rather than by drug. ATAC and BIG 1-98 compared five years of an aromatase inhibitor against five years of tamoxifen from the start. IES and ABCSG-8 / ARNO 95 compared a switch at two to three years against continuing tamoxifen to the end of five. TEAM and FATA-GIM3 compared starting with an aromatase inhibitor against starting with tamoxifen and switching later. Three different questions, habitually read as one.

The component reports are the primary evidence under the meta-analysis. ATAC favoured anastrozole for recurrence at its first analysis, at the completion of treatment and at 100 months48,49,50. BIG 1-98 first reported in 200551. Its sequencing comparison found that neither sequence improved disease-free survival over letrozole alone52. IES reported its first switch result in 200453. ARNO 95 found improved overall survival after switching to anastrozole54, and the Italian ITA trial improved event-free and recurrence-free survival55,56. A meta-analysis of the anastrozole switch trials found an overall survival benefit57. A patient-level meta-analysis of the aromatase inhibitor trials found lower recurrence whether the drug was given from the start or after two to three years of tamoxifen58.

The same trials measured what the benefit costs. In ATAC, patients with normal bone density at the start did not appear to need monitoring beyond that recommended for healthy postmenopausal women59. Endometrial abnormalities were fewer with anastrozole60. Overall quality of life was similar, with different symptom profiles61. Prior chemotherapy did not change the relative benefit62. Tamoxifen lowered anastrozole blood levels without affecting oestradiol suppression63. In BIG 1-98, letrozole increased bone fractures64, and cardiovascular events were infrequent and differed between the arms65.

TEAM constrains practice more than the others and is cited least. Its null result means that starting with an aromatase inhibitor is not superior to starting with tamoxifen and switching later18. FATA-GIM3 reached the same place from a six-arm design8. The case for starting with an aromatase inhibitor therefore rests on tolerability and on the gynaecological and thromboembolic profile. It does not rest on efficacy, because the two trials built to test the order found none.

The decision rests on BIG 1-98. It was double-blind. It randomised the whole five years rather than a switch part-way. It is also the only one of the six whose intention-to-treat overall survival comparison favoured the aromatase inhibitor. ATAC, with ten years of follow-up, did not reproduce that19. The divergence is not anastrozole against letrozole, because FACE randomised those two head to head and separated them on nothing. It is blinding, crossover and duration of follow-up.

BIG 1-98 also shows how much of its own survival claim is model-dependent. After the 2005 analysis, patients still on tamoxifen were unblinded and offered letrozole. A censoring-weighted analysis that removes those crossovers moves the overall survival estimate further in favour of letrozole than the intention-to-treat estimate in the table17. Both are correct estimates of different quantities. Only the intention-to-treat estimate describes the policy that was actually studied.

The two switch trials differ from each other in a way the table hides. IES randomised inside a single protocol with a fixed five-year total and reported overall survival22. The ABCSG-8 and ARNO 95 row is a combined analysis of two trials, reporting event-free survival with no overall survival in the pooled paper26. Their agreement is real. It is agreement between one trial and one pooling, not between two replications.

The measurement point is the one that causes harm in clinic. Menopausal status after chemotherapy is inferred from amenorrhoea and from gonadotrophin and oestradiol assays. Amenorrhoea is not menopause. An aromatase inhibitor given to a woman with recoverable ovarian function lowers oestrogen, raises gonadotrophins and can restart the ovary. In an audit of 45 women with chemotherapy-induced amenorrhoea who started an aromatase inhibitor, 27% showed a return of ovarian function, and the authors advised caution with sensitive oestradiol monitoring66. Markers of ovarian reserve do not settle it either. Chemotherapy lowers Mullerian inhibiting substance rapidly, and the fall did not predict later menstrual function67.

Three things the table cannot show. Every one of the six enrolled postmenopausal women, so none of it transfers to a premenopausal woman who is not suppressed. No trial randomised the length of the tamoxifen lead-in, so the two to three years the switch trials used is a protocol convention rather than an optimum. And bone is not a column here, although the meta-analysis above prices recurrence, mortality and fracture together.

4 · Ovarian function suppression and who benefits

Early disease · HR+/HER2- · adjuvant

Choosing the intensity and the duration of endocrine treatment

How completely does oestrogen have to be removed?

Rationale
Tamoxifen blocks the receptor; an aromatase inhibitor removes the ligand; ovarian suppression removes the source. Each step is a deeper intervention on the same axis, and each was expected to buy more disease control at more cost.
Experiment
Direct comparisons of an aromatase inhibitor against tamoxifen, and of ovarian suppression added to either, in premenopausal and postmenopausal populations separately.
Finding
Deeper suppression lowers recurrence, and the absolute gain tracks baseline risk rather than being uniform across the cell.
Limitation
None of these trials randomised the decision that a clinic actually makes, which is whether this particular patient's risk justifies the menopausal symptoms and the bone loss. Subgroup gradients are not the same as a randomised selection rule.
Next question
If the benefit tracks risk, can risk be measured well enough to allocate the intensity rather than infer it after the fact?

The randomised trials of ovarian suppression. Read the experimental-versus-control column first, because these four ask three different questions.

Four rows, three questions, and only two of these trials randomised suppression at all. SOFT set tamoxifen plus suppression, and exemestane plus suppression, against tamoxifen alone. ASTRRA set two years of goserelin added to tamoxifen against tamoxifen alone. SOFT/TEXT and ABCSG-12 gave a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist to everybody and randomised the partner drug instead. A reader who takes all four as tests of ovarian suppression has read two of them backwards.

Where the four agree is narrower than it looks. Adding suppression to tamoxifen lowers recurrence in premenopausal hormone receptor-positive disease, and in ASTRRA it also moved overall survival. Upgrading the partner drug on top of suppression is a separate claim, and the two trials that tested it disagree.

The decision rests on SOFT, because it is the only trial here that randomised the addition against the thing a clinic would otherwise give, which is five years of tamoxifen. Its population was not pre-selected on recovered ovarian function, which is what makes the row load-bearing. Its first report did not find the addition significant68. The eight-year report reversed that reading on disease-free and overall survival alike10, and the twelve-year report held it69. The final report, at a median of fifteen years, kept the reduction in breast cancer recurrence and lost the survival difference1. No biology changed between the first report and the second. The events needed time to accrue, which is the structural problem with adjuvant endpoints set out in Neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and metastatic design compared.

ASTRRA asks the same addition question in a narrower population. It enrolled women aged 45 or younger who had already had chemotherapy and remained premenopausal or resumed ovarian function, confirmed every six months by follicle-stimulating hormone and bleeding history5. Two years of goserelin improved disease-free survival, and the separation persisted at eight years70. That population is a prospective enrichment of precisely the SOFT stratum in which suppression looked worthwhile, so its agreement with SOFT is close to circular. What it adds is that the effect survives being looked for on purpose. Its overall survival comparison did reach significance, and the interval around that estimate is wide enough that the point estimate should not be quoted to a patient as a survival benefit.

On the partner question the two trials disagree, and the disagreement is not about the drugs. SOFT/TEXT found exemestane better than tamoxifen on disease-free survival and not on overall survival, at twelve years and again at fifteen71,1. ABCSG-12 found anastrozole no better than tamoxifen. Both held suppression constant in every arm, so that is not what divides them. Four other things do. ABCSG-12 gave three years of treatment rather than five. It used anastrozole rather than exemestane. It enrolled a largely chemotherapy-naive and lower-risk population, and its primary report came before most late events in this subtype have occurred20. And its final analysis was powered around a second randomisation, to zoledronic acid, which belongs to the adjuvant bisphosphonate argument rather than to this one72. Its endocrine comparison is a marginal one inside a bone-agent trial, and the patient-level meta-analysis that pools the field averages that result into the total47. Reading the two rows as a verdict on anastrozole against exemestane is the error the arms column exists to prevent.

The subgroups are where the decision lives, and the table carries only the HER2-negative hazard ratio. In HER2-negative disease, 86.0% of the joint population, the 15-year overall survival gain from exemestane over tamoxifen, both with suppression, was larger in women who received chemotherapy. It was 3.9 percentage points in SOFT and 2.5 in TEXT. Young age and high grade also marked a larger gain1. The gain tracks baseline risk rather than the drug, so a single average for this comparison is not usable in clinic.

One inversion inside SOFT is worth more attention than it gets. Exemestane with suppression gave the best eight-year disease-free survival of the three arms and did not give the best eight-year overall survival10. The arm that beat tamoxifen alone on survival was tamoxifen with suppression. The trial was not powered to rank the two suppression arms against each other on survival. Read the recurrence ordering as a survival ordering and you are reading something the design did not test. The final report removed the inversion without settling the ranking, because no arm differed significantly from tamoxifen alone on overall survival1.

Three things nobody randomised are missing from every row, and they are the part of the decision that is hardest in clinic. Duration is one, at two years in ASTRRA, three in ABCSG-12 and five in SOFT and TEXT, never varied inside a trial. Method is the second. ABCSG-12 and ASTRRA specified a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist, and no trial here set one method against another, so oophorectomy and radiation ablation are inferred rather than tested. The third is confirmation of suppression. ASTRRA measured ovarian function repeatedly and the others did not, which means the treatment being compared is biochemically the same drug and not reliably the same state. Discontinuation is not a column either, although it is what decides how much of any of this is delivered.

Caution

Every trial in this table enrolled premenopausal women. Where a benefit was found, it concentrated in those who were young, node-positive, high grade or chemotherapy-treated. That gradient comes from subgroup analysis and not from randomised allocation. Being premenopausal is the entry criterion, not the indication.

A woman with a small grade 1 node-negative tumour belongs to the enrolled population and not to the group that gained. Saying the absolute number out loud before prescribing is the discipline this section asks for.

In practice

Suppression is a risk-stratified decision, not a menopausal-status decision. Receipt of chemotherapy, young age and high grade mark the subgroups with the larger fifteen-year survival gain1.

In a woman with a small, grade 1, node-negative tumour the absolute gain sits at the bottom of that range and the toxicity is not proportionally smaller. Quote the absolute difference before prescribing rather than the hazard ratio.

Remaining premenopausal after chemotherapy is itself a selection marker, because it identifies the women in whom ovarian function was never removed10.

If suppression is used, the aromatase inhibitor is the better partner on recurrence and has not been shown to be better on survival47,1. Fractures rise, so bone management starts at the same visit.

Choosing the intensity and the duration of endocrine treatment

How long should endocrine treatment continue?

Rationale
Recurrence in this cell continues at a roughly constant rate for at least twenty years, so treatment that stops at five years stops while the hazard is still running.
Experiment
Randomised extension beyond five years, in each of the sequences a clinic actually uses. Tamoxifen to ten years, an aromatase inhibitor after tamoxifen, an aromatase inhibitor after an aromatase inhibitor. Then, against the same question from the other side, trials of shorter or intermittent extension.
Finding
Extension lowers recurrence, the effect is smaller than the first five years bought, and several extension trials found that more was not better than less.
Limitation
The extension trials enrolled patients who had already tolerated five years, which is a selected population, and they differ in the drug taken first, so the results are not interchangeable across sequences.
Next question

The case for extension is a statement about hazard. Distant recurrence in this subtype accrues at a roughly constant rate from year 5 to year 2033. The size of that residual risk by tumour size and nodal status is tabulated in Annual hazard of recurrence and the long tail of hormone receptor-positive disease and Late recurrence risk and surveillance implications. Extension is therefore a dormancy intervention rather than consolidation of a falling risk, and Dormancy, residual disease, and late recurrence is where that argument is made properly.

One thing is worth settling before the rows are read. Four of the eight report overall survival, and none of those four shows an advantage for the longer arm. The other four do not report it at all. An extension decision is a recurrence decision, and it should be described to a patient as one.

The eight look inconsistent until they are sorted by control arm, which is the axis that decides what each can answer. MA.17, MA.17R and NSABP B-42 randomised further treatment against placebo, and they are the only three that bear on whether to extend at all. ABCSG-16, DATA, GIM4 and IDEAL randomised a longer extension against a shorter one, so they answer how long rather than whether. SOLE answers neither, because both of its arms were extended.

The three placebo-controlled trials answer whether to extend at all, and they answer it in both sequences. MA.17 extended letrozole after tamoxifen. MA.17R extended it after an aromatase inhibitor, and both separated from placebo on disease-free survival23,14. NSABP B-42 asked the same question in the same place as MA.17R and did not clear its own significance threshold6. Extending after an aromatase inhibitor is therefore not the weaker version of the question, which is how it is usually read.

The four duration-against-duration trials are where the field reads a disagreement that may not be there. DATA and GIM4 asked the same thing, a longer aromatase inhibitor course after two to three years of tamoxifen, and returned disease-free survival hazard ratios of 0.79 and 0.78. Only GIM4 crossed significance, and it read out at a median follow-up of 11.7 years against DATA's five-year adapted endpoint11,4. What separates a positive extension trial from a null one here is events and follow-up rather than drug or sequence. IDEAL admitted women after any five years of endocrine therapy, so each of its arms mixes the two sequences9.

NSABP B-42 is where a convention rather than a biology decided the reported verdict. Its disease-free survival p value fell below 0.05 and above the threshold its own design had prespecified, so the trial is recorded as negative6. The ten-year update reached significance on the same endpoint73. Nothing about letrozole changed between those reports. An interim-analysis alpha changed what the same comparison was permitted to be called, which is worth knowing before quoting either one.

ABCSG-16 is the only row that priced the longer course in fractures as a named secondary endpoint rather than as an adverse event count3. Its two arms differed only in how many further years of anastrozole were given. The fracture excess is therefore attributable to duration and not to starting the drug, which is the one place in this table where the cost of extension is reported as an outcome.

SOLE answers a question the others do not ask. It gave letrozole intermittently against continuously, on the hypothesis that a break restores sensitivity, and found no difference at five years or at seven13,74. How to give an extension is separate from whether to give one. A null schedule trial is not evidence about duration.

The reading that fits is about what the control arm was doing, not about the molecule or the sequence. Against no further treatment, the longer arm lowered recurrence in MA.17 and MA.17R, in neither case with a survival difference. Against a shorter further course, three of the four trials did not separate. MA.17R is the awkward row for any tidy account of diminishing returns. Its patients had already had about five years of an aromatase inhibitor, and most had taken tamoxifen before that.

Caution

MA.17 is quoted as the trial that established extension, and its estimate is more fragile than that reputation suggests. It was stopped at its first interim analysis on short follow-up, and the placebo group was then unblinded and offered letrozole23,75. Its long-term estimate compares five further years against a shorter delayed course, not against none.

Its later reports show the same benefit through the crossover. The updated analysis at a median of 30 months confirmed better disease-free and distant disease-free survival with letrozole76. Analyses adjusted for the crossover favoured letrozole for disease-free, distant disease-free and overall survival, with an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.61 by one method and 0.76 by another77. In a non-randomised cohort analysis, women who started letrozole after a gap since tamoxifen also benefited78. The price was a modest fall in bone density79 and more vasomotor symptoms, without an adverse effect on overall quality of life80. ABCSG-6a gave three years of anastrozole after five of tamoxifen and reduced recurrence against no further treatment81.

The patient-level meta-analysis of twelve aromatase inhibitor extension trials in 22,031 postmenopausal women who had already completed at least five years of endocrine therapy now prices both sides82. Allocation to further aromatase inhibition reduced recurrence, with a rate ratio of 0.73 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.67 to 0.80. What a clinic needs from it is the absolute framing rather than the proportional one, and that arithmetic is in Absolute and relative benefit, and why the difference matters.

The subset that matters most in current practice is the one already treated with an aromatase inhibitor. There, five further years reduced recurrence with a rate ratio of 0.71, taking the risk from year 5 to year 15 from 15.2% to 11.6%. Distant recurrence fell with a rate ratio of 0.73, from 8.6% to 6.6%. Breast cancer mortality fell non-significantly, with a rate ratio of 0.90, an interval of 0.70 to 1.15 and a p value of 0.40, from 5.0% to 4.4%82.

Because the proportional reduction was similar across tumour characteristics, the absolute benefit scales with baseline risk. Ten years against five years took node-positive risk from 20.1% to 16.3% and node-negative risk from 11.8% to 9.1%82. Five-year fracture risk rose with a rate ratio of 1.35, from 3.4% to 4.6%.

One component of the extension benefit is not recurrence at all. Contralateral and second primary breast cancers fall with continued aromatase inhibition, and IDEAL isolates that effect because its two arms differed only in duration9. MA.17R reports the same direction on annual contralateral incidence14. Preventing a new primary is a real benefit. It is not the same achievement as preventing relapse, and Treatment duration and extended therapy sets out why the distinction matters when quoting a disease-free survival difference.

Two populations are missing from every row. No extension trial enrolled premenopausal women, so duration in a woman treated with tamoxifen and suppression is unstudied. And every placebo- controlled trial enrolled women who had already tolerated five years, which selects for adherence and against the patients most likely to stop. No trial in the table allocated extension on a molecular signal, which is the gap Unresolved questions and what reads out next returns to.

In practice

Extended therapy is a recurrence decision with an unproven mortality effect. Present it that way rather than as a survival intervention82.

Anchor the offer to nodal status, because the proportional benefit is flat and the absolute benefit is not. Quote the node-positive and node-negative recurrence risks between years 5 and 15 rather than the rate ratio82.

Put the fracture risk in the same sentence as the recurrence risk. The same analysis prices both, and the five-year fracture rate is what a patient will weigh against the recurrence she cannot see82.

A patient who has tolerated five years badly is unlikely to tolerate ten. Non-adherence in these placebo-controlled trials ran at 39.0% on further treatment and 37.6% on placebo82, which is the honest baseline for what extension means in practice.

6 · Late recurrence risk and surveillance implications

This subtype recurs on a timescale that outlasts most follow-up schedules and most trials. The annual hazard of recurrence does not fall to zero after five years, and the pattern across 24 years of follow-up is set out in83 and84.

The size of that residual risk is now precisely quantified. Among 62,923 women with oestrogen receptor-positive disease who were disease-free after five years of scheduled endocrine therapy, recurrences occurred at a steady rate from year 5 to year 20. Distant recurrence risk over that window was 13% for T1N0 disease, 20% for T1N1-3, 34% for T1N4-9, 19% for T2N0, 26% for T2N1-3 and 41% for T2N4-933.

Grade added moderately within a stage stratum. For T1N0 disease the risk over years 5 to 20 was 10% for low-grade, 13% for moderate-grade and 17% for high-grade tumours33. Progesterone receptor and HER2 status were not predictive of late distant recurrence in that dataset.

Two implications follow, and only one of them is comfortable.

The comfortable one is that the original pathology report retains predictive power for two decades. Size and nodal status are not superseded by anything currently in routine use.

The uncomfortable one is that no surveillance strategy has been shown to convert that knowledge into benefit. Detecting asymptomatic distant recurrence earlier has not improved survival, and the evidence against surveillance intensity is in Rehabilitation and survivorship. Circulating tumour DNA detects molecular relapse before imaging does, and whether acting on that lead time helps is unresolved and is the subject of Liquid biopsy and longitudinal monitoring. Late recurrence is a dormancy problem, and its biology is in Dormancy, residual disease, and late recurrence.

Interplay

Heterogeneity in this subtype is mostly temporal rather than spatial, which is why the chapter reads as a series of duration decisions. The adjuvant setting applies years of selective pressure to a population that cannot be sampled. What emerges at year eight has been shaped by that pressure exactly as the metastatic sequences in Endocrine therapy and the emergence of ESR1 alterations are. Extended endocrine therapy and adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition are not simply longer treatment. They are longer selection, and the ESR1 and RB1 escape routes that define the metastatic setting CDK4/6 inhibition and the evolution of cell cycle escape are being selected for during the years in which the patient is called disease-free.

Can an oral receptor degrader improve on the adjuvant endocrine standard?

Rationale
Oral degraders outperform an aromatase inhibitor in advanced disease, and the resistance mechanism they were built against is a mutation that emerges under aromatase inhibition. If the mutation is selected by the adjuvant course, a degrader given instead of it, or after it, would remove the pressure that creates the problem.
Experiment
Randomisation of a camizestrant or giredestrant course against standard adjuvant endocrine therapy. Two designs are running. One substitutes the degrader upfront in endocrine-naive patients. The other switches into it after two to five years of the standard course.
Finding
None has reported.
Limitation
The premise is inferred from the advanced setting rather than tested in the adjuvant one. The mutation these drugs were designed for is rare at diagnosis. So the adjuvant question is whether the agent is better against unselected disease, not whether it is better against the mutation. Ten-year endpoints in a subtype whose hazard runs for twenty years will read out on immature data.
Next question

8 · Adherence, toxicity, and the gap between prescribed and taken

Every hazard ratio in this chapter is an intention-to-treat estimate over a population in which a large minority stopped taking the drug. That is not a footnote to the effect size. It is part of what the effect size means, and the arithmetic follows from it.

In a cohort of 8,769 women with stage I to III hormone-sensitive breast cancer who filled at least one prescription, 32% had discontinued endocrine therapy by 4.5 years. Among those who continued, 72% were fully adherent by a medication possession ratio of 80% or more. Combining the two, 49% took adjuvant endocrine therapy for the full duration on the intended schedule. Women under 40 had the highest discontinuation risk, with a hazard ratio of 1.51 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.23 to 1.8585.

Compare that against the size of the other lever a clinician controls. Choosing an aromatase inhibitor over tamoxifen in a postmenopausal woman changes recurrence by a few percentage points over a decade. Not taking the tablet removes the entire effect of the drug. Clinic time is spent almost entirely on the smaller lever.

The randomised evidence says most of that loss is not pharmacological. In the placebo-controlled extended-therapy trials pooled by the EBCTCG, non-adherence was 39.0% on further aromatase inhibition and 37.6% on placebo82. Almost as many women stopped an inert tablet as stopped an active one, which says that the burden is the regimen as much as the pharmacology. Symptom management therefore has a ceiling, and the rest of the problem is structural.

The toxicities that drive this are quantified elsewhere in the chapter. Fractures rise with aromatase inhibition in postmenopausal women46 and with ovarian suppression in premenopausal women47. Endometrial cancer and thrombosis rise with tamoxifen46. Arthralgia and vasomotor symptoms are the commonest reasons given for stopping, and the symptom burden is developed in Endocrine therapy and Rehabilitation and survivorship.

Measurement matters here more than usual, because the three common methods disagree by design. Self-report overestimates, and most of all when the prescriber is the one asking the question. Pharmacy refill measures collection from a dispensary rather than ingestion. Serum tamoxifen or endoxifen, and serum oestradiol during aromatase inhibition, measure exposure directly and are almost never used. A trial reporting adherence by refill and a clinic reporting it by self-report are not describing the same quantity, and neither is wrong.

Dose is the one pharmacological lever with randomised evidence behind it. TAM-01 tested 5 mg of tamoxifen daily against placebo and reported durable benefit at ten years86. That trial enrolled breast intraepithelial neoplasia rather than invasive disease. Using low-dose tamoxifen to rescue an intolerant patient with invasive cancer is an extrapolation, and it should be described to her as one.

In practice

Ask about adherence at every visit, and ask in a form that permits a true answer. Roughly half of patients do not complete the prescribed course85.

Flag women under 40 as the highest-risk group for discontinuation and follow them accordingly85.

Set the expectation at the first visit rather than at the first symptom. Say that most women get vasomotor or musculoskeletal symptoms, that these usually settle, and that stopping is the outcome you are trying to prevent.

Treat the symptom before shortening the course. Switching between aromatase inhibitors, adding a vasomotor agent, or moving the dose to bedtime all leave the patient on treatment. Stopping does not.

Quantify the loss out loud when a patient asks to stop early. A woman stopping at year two has taken about two fifths of the intended course, and she is entitled to know roughly what that costs in the absolute framing of Absolute and relative benefit, and why the difference matters.

Assess bone density before and during aromatase inhibition or ovarian suppression, because fractures rise with both46,47. The management belongs in the same visit and is in Bone health and cardiovascular risk after treatment.

Record which adherence measure you used. A refill ratio and a patient's answer are different numbers, and they will disagree.

When quoting a benefit from any trial in this chapter, remember it was achieved under the same adherence that the patient in front of you will have. The number is not an estimate of perfect compliance and should not be presented as one.

9 · Unresolved questions and what reads out next

Early disease · HR+/HER2- · adjuvant

Choosing the intensity and the duration of endocrine treatment

What is known about the patients the adjuvant endocrine trials did not enrol?

Rationale
The adjuvant endocrine record was built almost entirely in postmenopausal women who agreed to take a drug continuously for five years. Two groups sit outside it. Men were excluded from nearly every trial, and women who want to become pregnant were excluded by the design of the intervention.
Experiment
For men, a randomised comparison of three endocrine strategies with an endocrine rather than a clinical endpoint. For pregnancy, a single-arm study of a planned treatment interruption, read against an external cohort.
Finding
Tamoxifen alone raises circulating oestradiol in men, which is the opposite of its intended effect on the axis, and adding gonadotropin-releasing hormone suppression reverses that. A planned interruption to attempt pregnancy was not followed by an excess of events over the comparison cohort at the follow-up reported.
Limitation
Neither design can carry the weight the clinic puts on it. The trial in men measured a hormone rather than a recurrence, so the clinical consequence is inferred. The interruption study had no randomised control and its comparison cohort was assembled separately, and its follow-up is short against a hazard that runs for two decades.

Duration in premenopausal women is close to unstudied. Every aromatase inhibitor extension trial in this chapter required a postmenopausal population. ATLAS carried no such requirement, and it tested tamoxifen rather than the suppression-based regimens these women now receive.

The duration of ovarian suppression has never been compared head to head in a randomised trial. ASTRRA used two years and SOFT used five. The question is not academic, because suppression is the least tolerated component of the regimen in young women Young adults, fertility, and pregnancy.

Whether cell cycle inhibition changes the endocrine question is being approached from two directions here. WSG ADAPTcycle asks whether ribociclib with an aromatase inhibitor can replace chemotherapy in intermediate-risk disease, with entry decided by a recurrence score and by Ki-67 measured after short preoperative endocrine therapy87. A positive result would move part of the chemotherapy decision in Genomic assays and the decision to omit chemotherapy into the endocrine column. That is a larger change than any degrader result would produce.

Its entry rule is also the clearest example in this chapter of a measurement standing in for a biology. Endocrine response is defined by a Ki-67 threshold, scored locally in some centres and centrally in others, and the two readings do not agree in every patient87. Allocation therefore depends partly on which laboratory read the slide. The scoring conventions are set out in Ki67, PEPI and response measures for a setting where pCR fails.

NEOLETRIB is the odd row and the one worth keeping. It is a single-group neoadjuvant study of letrozole with ribociclib, with serial biopsy and single-cell sequencing during treatment88. It will not settle a treatment question and is not built to. It is the only study in this chapter that samples a tumour while endocrine pressure is being applied, which is the measurement the adjuvant setting can never make. The window designs it belongs among are in Neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition and window-of-opportunity designs.

Interplay

Adjuvant endocrine therapy applies selective pressure for five to ten years to a population that cannot be sampled. That is the heterogeneity argument of Part VI running in slow motion. What emerges at year eight has been shaped by the agent chosen at year zero. The ESR1 mutations that define endocrine resistance in metastatic disease CDK4/6 inhibition and the evolution of cell cycle escape are selected by exactly the aromatase inhibitor exposure this chapter recommends. The oral degrader trials in Oral selective estrogen receptor degraders in the adjuvant setting are the first attempt to act on that selection before it has an observable output. They will be reported as duration trials. They are better understood as experiments on which escape route the adjuvant years build.
