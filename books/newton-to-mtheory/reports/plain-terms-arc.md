# The Through-Line: narrative-arc review

*Reading of `src/_throughline.html` — 187 "In plain terms" passages plus four rendered
bridging passages, read start to finish as one document, judged against `PLAIN-TERMS-PLAN.md`.
No files were edited. Every quoted sentence below is grep-findable in the named chapter source.*

---

## Verdict

It reads as one essay, and for long stretches — the whole of Part 0, all of Part I, and 3.1
through 3.4 — it reads as a *good* one, with an argument that genuinely accumulates and a voice
that does not waver. The spine holds because the promises are real: of the fifty-eight forward
promises I can identify, thirty-six are collected and eleven of those are collected twice and
named as collections, which is the single hardest thing to do at this scale and the thing that
most distinguishes this document from a pile of boxes; only four promises whose payoff lies inside
Parts 0 to III are genuinely dropped. It has three structural wounds: chapter 2.4 is
written in a visibly different hand and breaks the register for eight consecutive passages; the
seams between the parts are one fifth the length the plan specifies and say nothing the adjoining
boxes have not just said; and the document ends in the middle of Part III on a subordinate clause
about nonlinearity, while its own header promises a reader an arc "from the definition of a
derivative to what string theory is actually claiming". Two of the four motifs have gone silent —
"falling apart into independent pieces" is named six times in Part 0 and never once in the
following one hundred and twenty-five passages, and "nearly everything is an approximation" does not sound at all
across Part I's twenty-eight boxes. Fix the seams, repair 2.4, and restore the two dropped
drumbeats, and this stands on its own; leave them and it is an excellent book's excellent
by-product rather than the essay it was specified to be.

## §2 Findings

### MAJOR

**1 · Chapter 2.4 is written in a different voice, and eight consecutive passages break the
register.** (`src/ch2-4.html`)

This is the sample chapter, written before the calibration, and it shows on every page of the
Through-Line. It carries both instances of a banned word, the document's only bare formula, its
only authorial "we", its only doubled em-dash paragraph, and a diction ("the vector club", "a
brutal calculation", "a simple visual glance", "poor formatting or a breach of house style",
"profoundly important", "remarkably reliable error detector") that belongs to no other chapter.
Read in place it passes; read in sequence, after 2.3 and before 2.5, it is audibly someone else.

Current: `Complex tensors are simply layered combinations of the two foundational objects you already have.`
Replace: `A tensor with several slots is nothing more than those two objects layered, and the layering introduces no new idea.`

Current: `the covector simply counts how many of its sheets the arrow punches through.`
Replace: `the covector counts how many of its sheets the arrow punches through, and that count is the whole of the pairing.`

Current: `We never dictate what a vector is made of or what it should look like. Instead we establish how its description must adapt when you change your frame of reference.`
Replace: `Nothing here dictates what a vector is made of or what it should look like, and the definition says only how its description must adapt when the frame of reference changes.`

Current: `It also explains the fate of familiar equations such as Newton's F = ma.`
Replace: `It also explains the fate of Newton's second law, the one relating force to mass and acceleration.`
*Why:* §3 forbids symbols where a name will serve. This is the only bare equation in 42,000 words,
and it sits in the chapter whose entire subject is that notation carries meaning.

**2 · The Part III seam says the same thing three times in a row.**
(`src/ch2-6.html`, `throughline.py`, `src/ch3-1.html`)

2.6.10 closes: *"gravity is still a force reaching across empty space, arriving the moment it is
sent, and nothing in this part permits an influence with no delay."* The bridge then says: *"one
force was left behind, still acting instantly across empty space, and the geometry just made that
impossible."* Then 3.1.1 opens: *"The previous part ended with one force unaccounted for, still
reaching across empty space and still arriving the instant it was sent, and the geometry built
there forbids exactly that."* Three consecutive paragraphs, three statements of one fact, in
almost identical words. A reader crossing this seam is told nothing new for about eighty words,
which is exactly where a seam must be strongest.

The box endings are right and should stand. The bridge is the thing to change, and it should stop
restating and start *demanding*. Replacement bridge in full, in the book's voice, at the
specified length:

> Three chapters ago the interval was a fact about a fabric nobody could move. It is about to
> become the thing that moves.
>
> What Part II delivered was a geometry with a speed limit written into it rather than into any
> material, and a way of telling, by looking at the shape of an equation, whether a law was about
> the world or about a laboratory. What it could not deliver was gravity, and the reason is worth
> stating precisely rather than as a slogan. Newton's law of gravitation names two masses and the
> distance between them, and the distance between them at a given moment is exactly the kind of
> quantity the last part showed to be a fact about who is asking. There is no repair available
> that keeps a force and fixes the timing, because the trouble is not the speed at which gravity
> travels but the arithmetic in which the law is written.
>
> What follows takes the one measured coincidence that nobody in three centuries could explain,
> that a body's reluctance to be pushed and its response to gravity are the same number, and
> treats it as the whole of the subject. The price is that the arena stops being furniture.
> Distance becomes a field with equations of its own, the toolkit's warning about comparing an
> arrow here with an arrow there comes due, and by the end of this part the shape of space and
> the contents of space are two halves of one equation.

**6 · The word "sheet" carries two unrelated pictures fifteen passages apart.**
(`src/ch2-4.html`, `src/ch3-2.html`, `src/ch3-3.html`, `src/ch3-4.html`)

In 2.4.3–2.4.5 a covector *is* a stack of evenly spaced parallel sheets. From 3.1.8 onward a sheet
is the flat plane: "a flat sheet of paper", "on a flat sheet we slide arrows about freely", "the
flat sheet in circular labels", "a sheet seen to sag". The plan's own warning is that the same
picture used for two ideas is worse than none, and this is that, in the two chapters where a
reader is being asked to keep arrows and measuring devices apart.

The covector sheets should stay, because they are the standard picture and the plan names them as
the exemplar backward reach. The arena should become the **flat page**, which the essay already
uses at 0.1.3 (*"On a flat page in one dimension the two agree"*). Six substitutions, all
mechanical: `flat sheet of paper` → `flat page`; `the very same sheet` → `the very same page`;
`a fact about the sheet` → `a fact about the page`; `the flat sheet in circular labels` → `the
flat page in circular labels` (twice); `on a flat sheet we slide arrows about freely` → `on a flat
page arrows slide about freely`; `a sheet seen to sag` → `a rubber surface seen to sag`.

**7 · The one picture in the document that is reused for two different theorems, without warning:
the blob that changes shape while keeping its volume.** (`src/ch1-3.html`, `src/ch3-1.html`)

1.3.5: *"A blob released near a pendulum's dividing curve is drawn into a filament wound many
times round, thinner than any resolution you can afford, and its area does not move."* — a
statement about phase space, and about information.

3.1.4: *"A small ball of dust changes shape while holding its volume fixed, and that sentence
turns out to be the law of gravity in empty space, wearing very plain clothes."* — a statement
about ordinary space, and about the vacuum field equation.

Same image, two theorems, no signal that they are different. A reader who has held the first for
sixty passages will assume the second is it returning, and will come away believing that
Liouville's theorem is the vacuum Einstein equation. Add one sentence at the end of 3.1.4's second
paragraph, before "The stretching and squeezing balance":

> Nothing here is the volume theorem of phase space returning in new clothes, since that one
> concerned a cloud of possible states and this one concerns actual dust sitting in actual space;
> what they share is only the shape of the sentence, and the reason two different subjects hand you
> the same shape is that both describe a flow with nothing being made or lost in it.

**10 · The document ends. It does not close.** (`src/_throughline.html`, `throughline.py`)

The last sentence a reader meets is 3.6.7's: *"That difference is why exact solutions are rare,
why approximation is the normal state of affairs, and why the last part of the book finds gravity
so much harder than everything else."* That is a fine closing sentence for a section and a poor
one for a 42,000-word essay, and the header above it has already told the reader the document runs
"from the definition of a derivative to what string theory is actually claiming" and "reads start
to finish as one essay". Both claims are currently false: it stops at 3.6 of a planned 3.8, and
four of the eight bridges do not render because their parts do not exist.

Two repairs, neither of which needs a new box. First, the header line should say what is here:
`<b>187 passages</b> · about 42,542 words · Parts 0 to III, complete through the field equations`.
Second, a closing passage in the same class as the bridges, rendered after the last box, saying
where the argument has got to and what is owed. Full text, ready to drop into `throughline.py` as
a `CLOSING` entry emitted after the final part:

> That is as far as the argument has been carried, and it is worth saying plainly where it has
> stopped rather than letting the page simply run out.
>
> Four things have been built. There is a toolkit, which turned out not to be neutral, since the
> decision to define a derivative as a linear stand-in rather than a slope is what allowed the
> same definition to survive several variables, then whole histories, then curved space. There is
> a mechanics in which forces are not primitive, one number is attached to each history the world

> nothing for it to be measured against, and in which electricity and magnetism stop being two

> there is a gravity that is not a force at all but the shape of the arena, cornered into its
> final form by the demand that nothing on the geometry side of the equation impose a condition on
> matter that matter has never been observed to obey.
>
> What is owed is larger than what has been delivered. The account of gravity has not yet been run
> against a star or against the universe. Nothing here has said what happens when a thing has no
> definite state, although the mathematics that answers it was built in Part 0 for reasons that
> had nothing to do with physics. Nothing has said why there are the particular forces there are,
> although the shape of the answer has been visible since the redundancy in the electromagnetic
> potentials was first noticed and left alone. And nothing has said what to do about the one
> subject that has refused every method that worked for the others, which is the subject the whole
> arc has been walking towards. Those are the promises this document has made and not yet kept,
> and they are kept in the order the book takes them.

## §4 Motif audit

The plan asks for four threads, *named by the same words each time*, so that repetition is felt as
a drumbeat. Two of the four are in good order. Two have stopped.

### Motif A — "a choice of perspective"

**Named in the canonical words: never.** The word *perspective* appears four times in the whole
document, all of them in 2.4.1 and 2.4.2, which is the chapter that departs from the voice in
every other respect as well.

What the essay actually does instead is dress the motif in each part's local vocabulary, which is
what the §2 table asks for: *description* (28 times in Part 0), *relabelling* (Part I and Part
III), *frame* (49 times in Part II), *labels* and *charts* (Part III). That is right, and it
should not be flattened. But a drumbeat needs one phrase that survives the change of clothes, and
the essay has already found it without noticing: **"a fact about X rather than a fact about Y"**,
which recurs fourteen times and in every part — 0.4.4 (*"a fact about your bookkeeping rather than
about the physics"*), 1.2.7 (*"a fact about the path rather than about the naming"*), 2.2.6
(*"it is not a fact about nature"*), 2.5.2 (*"a report about a laboratory rather than a fact about
the world"*), 3.2.3 (*"whatever survives is a fact about the thing, and whatever does not was a
fact about the description"*), 3.3.2, 3.3.8. This is the governing sentence's own construction,
and it is already the strongest recurring music in the document.

**Recommendation:** treat *"a fact about … rather than a fact about …"* as the canonical form and
stop trying to make *perspective* work. Then add the phrase at the two places where the motif is
present in substance and absent in words: 2.3.1 (`not a separate fact about the world but the name
for whatever observers with differently oriented axes agree on` — already almost there) and 3.4.5,
whose closing sentence *"Those twenty are what no viewpoint removes"* is the motif's purest
statement in the whole essay and should say so: replace with `Those twenty are what no viewpoint
removes, which makes them a fact about the world rather than a fact about the observer, and which
is what the first chapter of this part said about the tide.`

**Verdict: present everywhere, audible nowhere as repetition. The paraphrase that should be
promoted to canonical is "a fact about … rather than a fact about …".**

### Motif B — "falling apart into independent pieces"

**Named six times, all of them in Part 0, and not once in the following one hundred and twenty-five passages.**

- 0.4.4 — *"the better description is the one in which the map has fallen apart into independent pieces that no longer interfere"* (the promise)
- 0.5.6 — *"the problem has fallen apart into independent pieces"* (the collection, and it names the phrase as a name: *"it deserves its name"*)
- 0.6.6 — *"So the quadratic part falls apart into independent pieces"*
- 0.8.3 — *"the problem has fallen apart into independent pieces"* (the third performance, and 0.9.3 counts them)
- 0.9.3 — *"Find the description in which the problem falls apart into independent pieces: one idea, and it has now paid for itself four times over"*
- 0.9.7 — *"the theorem that the right description makes a hard problem fall apart into independent pieces"* (the toolkit's own inventory)

Then silence. This is the most damaging single fact in the audit, because 0.9.3 explicitly counts
the performances — *"this is the fourth performance under the fourth different name"* — and
promises, by the act of counting, that there will be a fifth. There are at least four more in the
text, unnamed:

| where | what falls apart | currently called |
|---|---|---|
| 0.7.4 | any array into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part | *"Underneath both operators lies a single decomposition"* |
| 2.4.7 | any two-index object, and the counts ten and six | *"It is a real seam in the thing itself"* |
| 3.4.6 | the curvature into a summed-down part and a trace-free part | *"Boiling down loses information"* |
| 3.5.1 | the forms, one, four, six, four, one | *"One arithmetic fact"* |

**Recommendation:** name it at 3.4.6, which is the one where it does the most work, because the
split there is the split between where matter is and where orbits and waves live. Replace
`Boiling down loses information, and it matters how much.` with `The array has fallen apart into
independent pieces once more, exactly as the toolkit promised it would keep doing, and here the
two pieces are not equally interesting: boiling down loses information, and it matters how much.`
Name it again at 2.4.7, replacing `It is a real seam in the thing itself.` with `It is a real seam
in the thing itself, which is to say the object has fallen apart into independent pieces, and the
two halves go on to have entirely separate careers.`

**Verdict: the drumbeat stops dead at the end of Part 0. Two named recurrences would restore it.**

### Motif C — "nearly everything is an approximation"

**Named nowhere in the canonical words, stated once at full strength (0.3.1), and then inverted.**

In Part 0 the motif is loud and correct: *"physics does not usually solve its problems; it expands
them"*, *"an approximation carrying its own warranty"*, *"A rate of change is a linear
approximation"*, *"the oscillator is the system every hard problem gets expanded around"*.

In Parts I, II and III the word appears five times and **four of those are negations** —
*"not approximately but identically"* (2.1.6), *"with no approximation"* (2.5.4), *"exact rather
than approximate"* (3.1.7), *"the statement inside each cell was approximate. Here it is exact"*
(3.5.5). The later parts use the word to boast about exactness, and in Part I it does not appear
at all. The single positive statement of the motif after Part 0 is the final clause of the
whole document, 3.6.7: *"why approximation is the normal state of affairs"*.

Part I is the worst case: twenty-eight consecutive passages with no beat at all, although the
material is there and unnamed. 1.3.8's Hamilton–Jacobi remark is the motif exactly — *"the leading
term is exactly that same partial differential equation, the discarded terms smaller by one factor
of the constant"* — and never says so.

**Recommendation:** two namings, one in Part I and one in Part II. In 1.3.8, append to the final
paragraph: `That is the toolkit's habit arriving in the middle of mechanics: not a solution but an
expansion, with a named small quantity and a known first thing thrown away, which is very nearly
what every result in the rest of this book will be.` In 2.5.3, after `then corrections falling
away with the square of the speed ratio`, add: `Nothing about that ought to feel like a
concession, since almost every quantity in this book is the leading term of something, and what
distinguishes a controlled statement from a rule of thumb is whether the next term is known.`

**Verdict: strong in Part 0, absent in Part I, inverted in Parts II and III. Needs two deliberate
namings to become a drumbeat rather than a Part 0 habit.**

### Motif D — "the thing that had to exist"

**The healthiest of the four, and the one the essay has genuinely made its own.** The canonical
words are not the plan's; they are **"cornered into existence"**, and they recur with real
discipline across all four parts:

- 0.1.5 — *"The exponential is not a function anyone was fond of; it is one that was cornered into existence."* (the definitive statement, and the best opening sentence in the document)
- 0.3.5 — *"It is forced into existence, it is unimaginably small"* (the Planck length)
- 0.8.2 — *"another object arriving because a requirement cornered it into existence rather than because anyone liked it"*
- 1.1.3 — *"this is the first place in the book where a field is cornered into being real"*
- 2.5.3 — *"It was conscripted, because the demand that a law read alike for everybody left a fourth component lying about"*
- 2.6.9 — *"How nearly the form is forced is what to carry away"*
- 3.3.3 — *"The previous part cornered it into that form by demanding that everyone compute the same value"*
- 3.6.3 — *"This is the section where the law of gravity gets cornered rather than proposed"*
- 3.6.4 — *"the object cornered in the previous section appears unbidden"*

That is nine occurrences in four parts with the same verb, and it lands. Two notes. First, 2.5.3's
*conscripted* is the only paraphrase and it is a good one; it should stay, since the word is
better than *cornered* for a component that was drafted rather than built. Second, there is a
small word collision inside chapter 3.6: *"the corner entry"* of the energy–momentum object
appears in 3.6.1 and 3.6.5, three boxes either side of *"gets cornered rather than proposed"* and
*"slipped through the cornering untouched"*. Change the matrix sense, not the motif sense:
`Energy density is only the corner entry` → `Energy density is only the topmost entry`, and
`not merely its corner entry` → `not merely its topmost entry`.

**Verdict: intact, consistent, and doing exactly what the plan asked. It is the proof that the
other three could be made to work.**

## §5 The bridges

The plan specifies eight bridging passages of 150–250 words each, doing what a box cannot: standing
outside the chapters and saying where the argument has got to and what is about to be demanded of
it. Eight exist in `throughline.py`. **Four render**, because Parts IV to VII have no chapters yet.
And **seven of the eight are between 23 and 43 words** — one fifth to one quarter of the specified
length. That is the single most consistent failure in the document, and it is not a matter of
polish: at 35 words a bridge can gesture but it cannot argue, and the arc's most load-bearing
joints are its weakest writing.

**Bridge 0 · The Toolkit (153 words, renders).** The only one that meets the specification, and it
does its job well. It tells the reader what the document is, quotes the governing sentence in bold
where a reader will find it, and makes the sharpest claim in the front matter: *"the tools are not
neutral, and the choices made in building them decide what can be said later."* That sentence is
the reason Part 0 works. Two things are wrong with it. It promises an arc ending at *"what string
theory is actually claiming"*, which is currently false by four parts, and it does not name the
four motifs — which is precisely a thing a bridge can do and a box cannot, and which would make
every subsequent recurrence audible. One added paragraph would fix both.

**Bridge I · The Action Principle (32 words, renders).** The weakest of the four rendered bridges,
and the only one that repeats a phrase verbatim from the passage immediately above it. 0.9.7's
second paragraph opens *"The toolkit is finished."*; the bridge, some seventy words later, opens
*"The toolkit is finished."* It then says the part throws away the idea most people think physics is
made of, which 0.9.7 has just said in better words (*"the next part begins that job by discarding
the thing most people take to be the foundation of the whole subject. It throws away forces"*).
So the seam adds nothing and repeats something. What it should be doing is naming the price of the
move — that an action is one function supplied by hand, exactly as a force law was, and that the
whole of Part I is the argument that one number per history is a better thing to be handed than a
bundle of arrows. It needs writing from scratch at length.

**Bridge II · Special Relativity (43 words, renders).** The best of the three short ones, because
it does one thing a box did not: it names the *property* Part I bought — *"equations that keep
their shape when you change your description"* — rather than the results, and then says that
property is about to be tested. That is genuine bridge work. It is still a fifth of the length,
and it wastes its only chance to prepare the reader for what actually makes Part II hard, which is
not the speed of light but the abandonment of a shared present. One more paragraph, naming the
shared clock as the thing about to be surrendered, would carry the reader across properly.

**Bridge III · General Relativity (34 words, renders).** The worst seam in the document, for the
reason set out in Finding 2: it is the middle statement of three consecutive statements of one
fact, sandwiched between 2.6.10's closing paragraph and 3.1.1's opening one, all three saying that
gravity still acts instantly and the geometry forbids it. It also contains the document's only
banned intensifier (*"the geometry just made that impossible"*). Finding 2 supplies a full
replacement.

**Bridge IV · Quantum Mechanics (43 words, does not render).** Well aimed and far too short. Its
one good move is the last clause — *"the mathematics that replaces them was built four parts ago
for entirely different reasons"* — which is the payoff for a whole run of Part 0 promises (0.4.7,
0.5.6, 0.5.8) and deserves a paragraph rather than a subordinate clause. When Part IV is written
this bridge is where the register of Part 0 promises comes due, and it should list them.

**Bridge V · Quantum Field Theory (23 words, does not render).** The shortest, and the most
under-argued: *"Relativity and quantum mechanics are each secure and are not compatible. Forcing
them together costs the particle its status as the fundamental object."* Two assertions with no
mechanism between them. Part 0 has already supplied the mechanism, at 0.8.7 — a field is what
infinitely many coupled oscillators turn into — and this bridge should collect that promise
explicitly, since it is the largest single outstanding debt in the register.

**Bridge VI · Gauge Theory (37 words, does not render).** Structurally the same two-beat as the
others but with the right content: *"a single demand, made four times."* It should collect 0.7.7,
which is where the redundancy in the potentials was first called *"the seed from which every force
in the Standard Model grows"*, and 1.4.6, which asked what happens when the phase angle may vary
from place to place. Both are named promises; this is their home.

**Bridge VII · Strings and M-Theory (36 words, does not render).** Honest in tone, which is right
for this part, and its promise of *"an honest account of how much of it is known"* is the correct
note. It is also the last bridge in the book and therefore the last place before the end where
someone can say what the whole arc was, and thirty-six words cannot do that.

**Across all eight: six of them use the identical two-beat construction — "X has now been
settled; Y is about to be demanded."** Read one at a time that is a good shape. Read as the eight
joints of a single essay it is a formula, and a reader who has crossed three seams will predict
the fourth. At the specified length the shape would vary naturally; at thirty-five words there is
room for nothing else.

## §6 What the essay still needs

### The biggest thing: the document does not know that it is unfinished, and says so in the one place a reader will believe it.

The header reads **"187 passages · about 42,542 words · reads start to finish as one essay"**, and
the opening bridge tells the reader they are about to get *"a continuous account of how physics
arrived at its present picture of the world, from the definition of a derivative to what string
theory is actually claiming"*. Neither is true today. What exists is Parts 0 to III, stopping at
3.6 of a planned 3.8, ending on a subordinate clause about why gravity is nonlinear. A smart
colleague who read only this document would come away with an accurate, non-trivial and genuinely
load-bearing understanding of four things — that physics is the search for what survives a change
of description; that forces are not primitive and an action is; that special relativity is a
geometry and electromagnetism is one field in it; that gravity is not a force but the shape of the
arena — and with one wrong belief installed by the document itself: that this is the finished
argument. They would go looking for the string theory they were promised, find it nowhere, and
reasonably conclude that the essay collapsed rather than that it is four parts from done.

That is the biggest thing, and **all of it can be supplied by bridging passages rather than by new
boxes.** Specifically:

1. **An honest header.** One line: *"Parts 0 to III, complete through the field equations. Parts IV
   to VII to follow."* Costs nothing, removes the false claim.
2. **A closing passage**, in the bridges' class and at the bridges' specified length, rendered
   after 3.6.7. Full text is written out in Finding 10 and is ready to drop in. It does three
   things no box can do: it says what has been built, in four sentences that a reader can hold; it
   says what is owed, which converts seventeen open promises from a leak into a plan; and it gives
   the reader the sense that the argument stopped deliberately rather than ran out.
3. **A paragraph in the opening bridge naming the four motifs.** The plan's whole theory of the
   drumbeat is that repetition must be *felt*, and repetition is felt far more reliably when the
   reader has been told what to listen for. This is the cheapest single intervention in the whole
   report: four named threads, forty words, and every one of the seventy-odd subsequent
   recurrences starts working. Draft:

   > Four moves recur, and it is worth knowing their names before meeting them. The first is a
   > choice of perspective, which is what coordinates, bases, frames and gauges all are, and the
   > separation of what depends on that choice from what does not is the book's central habit. The
   > second is a problem falling apart into independent pieces, which happens seven times under
   > seven different names and is one theorem being spent over and over. The third is that nearly
   > everything is an approximation, since physics does not usually solve its problems but expands
   > them, and the honest version of the method always names what was thrown away. The fourth is
   > the thing that had to exist: most of the objects in this book were not invented but cornered,
   > forced into being by a requirement rather than chosen for convenience, and wherever that is
   > true it is worth saying so.

### Second: the seams need to be written, not sketched.

Seven of eight bridges are between a fifth and a quarter of the specified length, and six of eight
use the identical two-beat shape. The rendered ones are the joints at which a reader most needs
help and least receives it. Writing the four rendered bridges to 150–250 words is perhaps 700
words of new prose and would do more for the arc than any thirty box-level edits in §2.

### Third, and much smaller: the two dead motifs.

Four sentences, written out in §4, restore the two drumbeats that stopped. *Falling apart into
independent pieces* needs naming at 2.4.7 and 3.4.6; *nearly everything is an approximation* needs
naming at 1.3.8 and 2.5.3. Those four namings cost less than a hundred words and would make Parts
I to III sound like the same essay as Part 0, which at present, in this one respect, they do not.

### What is *not* needed

No new boxes. The section-level coverage is complete and the form discipline is close to perfect:
every one of the 187 passages is three paragraphs, every one is between 176 and 235 words, there
is not a single rhetorical question or exclamation mark in 42,000 words, and there are sixteen
em-dashes in total with exactly one paragraph carrying two. Nothing in the arc is missing because
a section went uncovered. What is missing is at the joints and in the front and back matter, which
is precisely where the plan said the bridges would do the work.
