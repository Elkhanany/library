Part XI · Metastatic disease: principles and cross-cutting management · Chapter 64

General principles of metastatic management

What gets decided before any drug is chosen, and why the first question is never which agent.

1 · De novo and recurrent metastatic disease as different populations

Two patients can present with identical imaging and be different problems. One has metastatic disease at first diagnosis. The other relapsed after adjuvant treatment. The stage is the same and the prior history is not.

De novo disease has never been exposed to selection. Nothing has been removed from the tumour population by treatment, so the sensitivity profile is closer to the untreated state. Recurrent disease is what survived adjuvant therapy. It is a selected population by definition, and what selected it is recorded in the chart.

Two facts from the adjuvant record therefore carry information. The first is which agents were given. The second is how long the disease-free interval was. Relapse during adjuvant endocrine therapy and relapse eight years after completing it are different statements about dependence. The annual hazard of recurrence in hormone receptor positive disease stays low and persists for well over a decade1. Late relapse is common enough that a long interval should not be read as proof of a dormant, indolent biology.

Where the disease lands also varies with subtype, and the site of first distant relapse differs systematically across subtypes2. Intrinsic subtype is largely but not entirely preserved between primary and metastasis3. The dormancy biology behind the long interval is developed in Dormancy, residual disease, and late recurrence, and the evolutionary account in Temporal heterogeneity and clonal evolution.

Caution

Registry comparisons showing longer survival for de novo than for recurrent disease are not comparing treatments. They are comparing a treatment-naive population with a selected one, over different lead times, with different competing exposures. The difference is real and it does not support any inference about what to give.

2 · Disease burden, tempo, and symptom-driven decisions

Three variables govern the urgency of a metastatic decision and they are routinely collapsed into one. Burden is how much disease is present. Tempo is how fast it is changing. Symptoms are what the patient feels. They are independent, and they can point in different directions.

Large burden with slow tempo and no symptoms does not require an urgent regimen. Small burden with rapid tempo and organ dysfunction does. The common error is to let a bulky scan drive the choice when the rate of change is the variable that predicts what happens over the next three months.

Tempo is the one that is least often recorded. It needs two measurements and a stated interval. A single baseline scan cannot supply it. Where an outside scan exists, obtaining it is usually worth more than ordering a new modality.

In practice

Before choosing a first metastatic regimen, write down four things.

Where the disease is, and which sites are doing the work of making the patient unwell.

How fast the burden changed between the last two assessments, with the interval named.

What the patient reports, separately from what the imaging shows.

What organ function is threatened now, as against what may be threatened in a year.

If tempo and burden disagree, tempo usually determines the next three months. Burden determines what the disease will look like when the first regimen fails.

3 · Biopsy and molecular profiling at recurrence and at progression

A biopsy at recurrence answers three separate questions, and confusing them produces most of the argument about whether it is worth doing. The first is whether the lesion is breast cancer. The second is what its receptor status is now. The third is what actionable alterations it carries.

Tissue confirmation of metastatic disease changes management in a proportion of patients, mainly through receptor results that differ from the primary4. Whether a difference is true conversion or assay variation is the subject of Receptor discordance and conversion and is not settled by repeating the test.

Plasma adds a different kind of information. It samples what is shedding rather than what is accessible to a needle, so it is better at breadth and worse at certainty. Acquired ESR1 mutations are the clearest example, since they appear in patients previously exposed to an aromatase inhibitor and predict failure of further oestrogen deprivation5. Two technical requirements apply. Matched white blood cell sequencing keeps clonal haematopoiesis out of the result6. And a negative plasma test in low-volume disease is uninformative rather than reassuring, for the shedding reasons in Liquid biopsy and longitudinal monitoring.

The ordering rule is narrow. Order what would change the next decision. The specific list, and the reasoning behind each item, is in What to measure at progression and what it will change.

4 · Treatment selection and sequencing principles

The organising question is not which drug is best. It is which dependency is currently load-bearing, and which of the available agents attacks it.

That reframing has a practical consequence. Treatment in this part of the book is written as a set of branch points rather than a numbered sequence. The branches that actually change the answer are ESR1 status, PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN status, HER2 expression level, and germline BRCA status. A new agent slots into a branch. It does not renumber a list.

Three rules follow and they are worth stating plainly.

Match the agent to the failure, not to the line. Progression on endocrine therapy after four years and progression after four months are different failures, and only one of them suggests the class was never right. The distinction is developed in A working taxonomy, intrinsic, adaptive, and acquired resistance.

Do not assume cross-resistance from shared indication. Two drugs that treat the same disease can fail for unrelated reasons. Where dependence is genuinely shared, cross-resistance follows. Where it is not, progression on one predicts little about the other Cross-resistance between classes and what it means for sequencing.

Spend the effective agents while the patient can tolerate them. Sequencing plans that reserve the best option for later assume a later that many patients do not reach.

5 · Response assessment and patterns of progression

Response criteria were built to compare arms in trials. They are used in clinic to decide whether one person should change treatment. Those are different jobs and the tool fits the first better than the second.

Bone-predominant disease is the standard difficulty. Sclerotic change in a healing lytic lesion can appear as a new finding on computed tomography. Increased tracer uptake on bone scintigraphy reflects osteoblastic activity rather than tumour cell number. Both are pictures of response that read as progression.

Immunotherapy adds a second problem. Tumour burden can increase before it falls, which is why the response framework for these agents requires confirmation on a later scan rather than acting on a single assessment7.

The pattern of progression matters as much as the fact of it. Progression in one site while everything else is controlled is a different event from progression everywhere. The first is addressed in Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease. The second means the regimen has failed.

A rising tumour marker with stable imaging and a stable patient is not progression. It is a reason to shorten the interval to the next assessment. The full list of things that masquerade as resistance is in Distinguishing resistance from under-dosing, non-adherence, and pseudo-progression.

6 · Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease

The two terms describe different situations and the shared prefix hides that.

Oligometastatic disease means few deposits at presentation or at relapse. The definition rests on a count, and a count is a property of the imaging used to make it. Sensitive imaging moves patients out of the category without changing their biology. That is a measurement statement, not a biological one, and it should be said out loud before anyone is offered ablative therapy on the strength of a number of lesions.

Oligoprogression means most sites are controlled and one or a few are not. Here the inference is about clonal structure rather than about total burden. A single progressing deposit while the rest responds suggests a resistant subclone in one place, which is exactly the picture developed in Temporal heterogeneity and clonal evolution. Treating that site locally and continuing the systemic regimen is a reasonable response to that inference.

The honest position on evidence is that it is thin. Randomised data supporting local ablative therapy specifically in metastatic breast cancer are limited, and the enthusiasm for the approach runs well ahead of them. Local treatment of a symptomatic or structurally threatening deposit stands on its own merits. Local treatment intended to extend survival in an oligometastatic patient is a reasonable hypothesis rather than an established benefit.

7 · Treatment-free intervals and cumulative toxicity

Metastatic treatment is usually continuous, and the assumption that it must be goes mostly unexamined. Two things make it worth examining.

The first is that toxicity classes differ in whether they accumulate. Endocrine agents and targeted oral drugs produce toxicity that is largely a function of current exposure. Taxane neuropathy, anthracycline cardiac injury and marrow reserve are cumulative. Stopping the first group returns the patient close to baseline. Stopping the second group stops the accumulation and does not reverse it.

The second is that the goal is time at acceptable function, not maximal tumour suppression at every moment. A planned interval off cytotoxic therapy in a patient with controlled, low-tempo disease buys recovery that a dose reduction does not.

The decision is best made explicitly rather than by drift. Drift looks like a dose reduction made during a toxicity episode that is never reversed, followed by a schedule nobody chose. Toxicity management in detail is in Treatment toxicity and supportive care, and the long-horizon version of this argument is in Long-term disease control and the cost of staying on treatment.

In practice

Ask at each cycle which toxicities are cumulative and which are not. Only the cumulative ones argue for a break rather than a reduction.

State a plan for the interval before starting it. Duration, what will be monitored, and what would end it early.

Record the reason a dose was reduced. A reduction made for an episode that resolved is a candidate for reversal, and it will not be reversed unless someone wrote down why it happened.

8 · Clinical trials and early integration of palliative care

Trial enrolment is a line of therapy, not a consolation. It is best discussed at the start of a line rather than after the last option has failed, because eligibility criteria are strictest at the point where they are hardest to meet. Prior therapy limits, organ function thresholds and washout requirements all tighten with each additional line.

Access to trials is not distributed evenly, and that fact belongs in the clinical conversation rather than only in a policy chapter. The determinants are set out in Disparities across the breast cancer continuum and the design consequences in Clinical trials and evidence interpretation.

Early palliative care is an intervention with randomised evidence behind it. In patients with metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer, early integrated palliative care improved quality of life and reduced depressive symptoms at 12 weeks. Fewer patients received aggressive care at the end of life, and median survival was longer at 11.6 months against 8.9 months8. The population was lung cancer and the result should not be transferred to breast cancer as a survival claim. What transfers is the design point. Referral was by diagnosis and timing, not by prognosis or by exhaustion of treatment.

That is the operational lesson. Referral triggered by disease status rather than by the failure of oncology is the part of the model that can be copied. End-of-life care is developed in Palliative and end-of-life care.

Interplay

Every principle in this chapter follows from the tumour being a population rather than a clone. De novo and recurrent disease differ because one population has been selected and the other has not. Oligoprogression is a claim about where a resistant subclone sits, not about how much disease there is. Matching the agent to the failure rather than to the line is the same argument at the bedside. What failed was a dependency in part of the population, not in the whole of it. The clonal account that licenses each of those readings is in Clonal evolution and treatment sequencing, and the reason a single biopsy can misrepresent the population it came from is in Spatial heterogeneity, biopsy selection, and biomarker interpretation.
