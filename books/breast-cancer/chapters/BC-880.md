## BS-7420 Scope, and why the three agents are not interchangeable

This chapter covers CDK4/6 inhibition in metastatic hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative
disease. It takes the first-line combinations and what each has shown for survival, then the
evidence after first progression. It then asks whether to continue the class beyond progression,
and whether to give it first at all. It closes on the resistance mechanisms worth testing for and
the toxicity differences that decide agent choice in clinic.

The endocrine partner is argued in [[BC-870]], which also covers what to do when an ESR1 mutation
appears. Conjugates and chemotherapy are in [[BC-890]]. Cell cycle biology is in [[BC-160]],
resistance mechanism detail in [[BS-2980]], and agent pharmacology in [[BC-450]]. The clinical
framing of the whole branch is in [[BC-560]].

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 status=reported weight=any cols=acronym,phase,n,population caption=Every_reported_trial_in_the_registry_for_this_setting._The_sections_below_take_slices_of_it.
```

That table is the whole reported record for this position. It is not one question asked twenty-five
times. It is six questions, and a row means very little until you know which of the six it belongs
to.

Ten trials added an inhibitor to endocrine therapy and compared that against the same endocrine
therapy alone. They are {{trial:paloma-1-trio-18}}, {{trial:paloma2}}, {{trial:paloma3}},
{{trial:monaleesa2}}, {{trial:monaleesa3}}, {{trial:monaleesa7}}, {{trial:monarch2}},
{{trial:monarch3}}, {{trial:dawna-1}} and {{trial:dawna-2}}. Three set an inhibitor combination
against chemotherapy instead: {{trial:right-choice}}, {{trial:pearl}} and
{{trial:young-pearl-kcsg-br15-10}}. Six asked what to do once the disease had progressed on the
class: {{trial:maintain}}, {{trial:postmonarch}}, {{trial:pace}}, {{trial:palmira}},
{{trial:bioper}} and {{trial:triniti-1}}. Three asked what else belongs in the regimen beside the
inhibitor: {{trial:inavo120}}, {{trial:viktoria1}} and {{trial:ember3}}. Two kept the inhibitor
running and changed the endocrine partner on a circulating tumour DNA signal, {{trial:pada1}} and
{{trial:serena6}}. One randomised the timing rather than the drug, {{trial:sonia}}.

Palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib are routinely described as a class. They inhibit the same
two kinases and they produce broadly similar effects on progression-free survival in the first
line. Three things separate them and none is visible in a class label. They differ in what has been
shown for survival, which is the subject of the next two sections. They differ in dominant
toxicity, which is the subject of the last. They differ in schedule and monitoring burden, which
determines what a year on treatment is like.

What the table cannot show is any comparison between the agents. No row in it randomised one
inhibitor against another, in any line. No trial in the registry that has yet to report does so
either. Every ranking of the three is a contrast across trials that differ in endocrine partner,
enrolment year and what patients were allowed afterwards. {{trial:dawna-2}} was run in Chinese
hospitals alone [@zhang2023], so the fourth member of the class has been tested in one country's
practice rather than in many. This block also admits exploratory work, which is why two single-arm
studies sit among the randomised ones. The sections below take randomised slices and say when they
are doing otherwise.

## BS-7430 First-line combinations and their overall survival results

```story SQ-0460
```

Seven of the twelve rows below added an inhibitor to the endocrine therapy the patient would have
received anyway. The other five are first-line trials that ask something else, and they are in the
table because the filter is a position in the disease rather than a question.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 line=1L status=reported cols=acronym,n,arms,result,os caption=First-line_trials._The_overall_survival_column_is_where_the_three_agents_separate.
```

Across those seven the progression result is the same finding repeated. Adding the inhibitor
lengthened time to progression against the identical endocrine partner given alone. That held for
three agents in wide use and for a fourth. It held in postmenopausal and in premenopausal disease,
and whether the partner was an aromatase inhibitor or fulvestrant. The hazard ratios cluster
tightly enough to read as one property of the class. That is the only class-level claim the first
line supports.

The survival column does not behave like the progression column, and the differences in it are
mostly differences of design. Ribociclib has three survival results pointing one way.
{{trial:monaleesa2}} was positive with letrozole in postmenopausal disease [@hortobagyi2022].
{{trial:monaleesa7}} was positive in pre- and perimenopausal women, all of whom received ovarian
suppression [@lu2022]. {{trial:monaleesa3}} was positive with fulvestrant [@slamon2020]. The third
of those is weaker than it looks as evidence about first-line treatment. It enrolled patients who
were treatment naive or who had already had one endocrine line [@slamon2018]. Its registry entry
also reports a landmark survival rate rather than a median, so it cannot be set beside the other
two as the same quantity [@neven2023]. Palbociclib and abemaciclib each have one first-line
survival result and neither reached significance [@slamon2024paloma2; @goetz2024]. Dalpiciclib has
none at all, because {{trial:dawna-2}} reported a preplanned interim progression analysis and no
survival data [@zhang2023]. {{trial:paloma-1-trio-18}} is the randomised phase II that preceded
{{trial:paloma2}}, and the larger trial supersedes it [@finn2014].

One feature of the palbociclib result is a measurement problem rather than a biological one, and
the authors say so. Survival status was unknown at analysis for a substantial minority of patients,
and for more of them in the placebo arm than in the palbociclib arm [@slamon2024paloma2]. Loss to
follow-up in that direction removes control-arm deaths from view. A negative result with a
censoring imbalance is not the same object as a negative result without one.

Three explanations compete for the pattern and none has been established. The drugs may differ
pharmacologically, since they are not equipotent against the two kinases and are not given on the
same schedule. The trials may differ in conduct and follow-up, which the palbociclib censoring
imbalance demonstrates directly. Or the survival differences may be noise, given how closely the
progression results agree. No first-line trial in this group carried overall survival as its
primary endpoint. Every survival result here is a secondary endpoint from a trial powered for
progression.

The decision to start an inhibitor in the first line does not in fact rest on any of that. It rests
on the seven progression results, which agree. Where the choice is not endocrine therapy alone but
chemotherapy, the load-bearing trial is {{trial:right-choice}}, because it is the only first-line
trial here whose control arm is what such a clinic would otherwise do. It randomised premenopausal
women with clinically aggressive disease, most of whom had symptomatic visceral metastases.
The comparison was ribociclib with endocrine therapy against investigator's choice of combination
chemotherapy. The endocrine arm lengthened progression-free survival against that comparator
[@lu2024]. Overall response rates were close in the two arms. Median time to response was longer
with the endocrine combination, and that is the cost a clinic weighs when the disease is moving.

The remaining rows answer other questions and are argued elsewhere. {{trial:pada1}} and
{{trial:serena6}} both continued the inhibitor in both arms and randomised only the endocrine
partner, triggered by an ESR1 mutation rising in circulating tumour DNA before radiological
progression [@bidard2022pada; @bidard2025]. They are evidence about the receptor rather than about
the kinase, and they belong to [[BC-870]]. Their result also belongs to a sampling scheme rather
than to a drug. {{trial:serena6}} tested every two to three months, and only a positive test opened
the door to randomisation [@turner2026serena6]. A clinic that does not run that surveillance cannot
reproduce the population the result describes. {{trial:inavo120}} enrolled the most
endocrine-resistant first-line population in the table, PIK3CA-mutated disease relapsing during
adjuvant endocrine therapy or within 12 months of completing it, and both of its arms contained
palbociclib [@turner2024inavo120]. It is a trial of a third drug, not of the inhibitor.
{{trial:sonia}} is the next section.

```caution
Comparing overall survival medians across {{trial:monaleesa2}}, {{trial:paloma2}} and
{{trial:monarch3}} is not a comparison of the three drugs. The endocrine partners, the enrolment
years, the permitted subsequent therapies and the follow-up durations all differ. A blank in the
survival column is a statement about maturity and not about effect. The strongest claim the
evidence supports is that ribociclib has the most consistent survival evidence in the first line.
It does not support the claim that palbociclib or abemaciclib shortens life relative to ribociclib.
```

## BS-7440 Second-line and later combinations

The trials after first progression divide into two eras, and conflating them is the commonest
misreading of this table. The dividing line is not the year. It is whether the patients had
already had a CDK4/6 inhibitor.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 line=2L,3L+ status=reported cols=acronym,phase,n,population,result,os caption=Trials_after_first_progression._The_upper_group_predates_routine_first-line_CDK4/6_inhibition;_the_continuation_trials_do_not.
```

Four rows belong to the first era. {{trial:monarch2}}, {{trial:paloma3}} and {{trial:dawna-1}}
added an inhibitor to fulvestrant in patients progressing on endocrine therapy, and the second-line
stratum of {{trial:monaleesa3}} did the same [@sledge2017; @cristofanilli2016; @xu2021a;
@slamon2018]. All of them lengthened progression-free survival. Their survival results differ, and
the difference tracks the population rather than the drug. {{trial:monarch2}} required progression
on endocrine therapy within defined windows and reported a survival benefit [@sledge2020].
{{trial:paloma3}} admitted any menopausal status and patients with primary endocrine resistance. It
missed significance on survival overall. Its benefit sat in the patients with documented
sensitivity to prior endocrine therapy [@turner2018paloma3]. That subgroup finding is consistent
with the resistance definitions in [[BS-4810]] and is one of the few places where the clinical
timing convention predicts something. {{trial:dawna-1}} has reported no survival data at all
[@xu2021a].

This population has almost disappeared. A patient reaching second line today has nearly always had
an inhibitor already. These four trials describe a decision that is now rare, and quoting them to
justify a second inhibitor after first-line inhibitor failure is quoting the wrong evidence.

Two rows ask a different question again and they appear to disagree. {{trial:pearl}} and
{{trial:young-pearl-kcsg-br15-10}} both compared a palbociclib-containing endocrine regimen against
capecitabine, and only the smaller of the two met its endpoint [@martin2020; @park2019a]. The
disagreement is population, not drug. {{trial:pearl}} enrolled postmenopausal women resistant to an
aromatase inhibitor and read its co-primary endpoints in a fulvestrant cohort and in ESR1 wild-type
patients. {{trial:young-pearl-kcsg-br15-10}} enrolled premenopausal women progressing on tamoxifen,
and gave exemestane with ovarian suppression. Its patients therefore came to the trial off
tamoxifen rather than off an aromatase inhibitor. Neither trial found a survival difference
against capecitabine [@martn2022; @ahn2025]. What separates the two options is therefore
tolerability rather than outcome. {{trial:pearl}} is the trial that measured it. Time to
deterioration of global health status favoured palbociclib, at an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.67
against capecitabine [@martin2020].

Four more rows are the post-progression trials, {{trial:maintain}}, {{trial:postmonarch}},
{{trial:pace}} and {{trial:palmira}}. They are the modern second line and they are argued in the
next section.

The last two rows put a third drug on the backbone. {{trial:ember3}} carried a third arm of
imlunestrant with abemaciclib. Among the patients randomised concurrently to those two arms, the
combination lengthened progression-free survival against imlunestrant alone [@jhaveri2025ember3].
Its population had recurred or progressed on an aromatase inhibitor, given with or without a
CDK4/6 inhibitor. Two readings of that arm are possible and the trial does not separate them.
Adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor to a receptor degrader may recover cell cycle control that endocrine
therapy alone cannot deliver. Or two drugs may simply beat one. The toxicity cost of the
combination was substantial and is in [[BS-4840]]. {{trial:viktoria1}} randomised gedatolisib with
fulvestrant, with or without continued palbociclib, against fulvestrant alone, in PIK3CA wild-type
disease and with blinded central review [@hurvitz2026]. The effect sizes are the largest in the
table and the control arm is the reason to read them carefully. Fulvestrant alone ran shorter there
than in any other trial in this table. A comparator that performs unusually badly inflates every
hazard ratio measured against it.

What this table cannot show is the choice a clinic actually makes after first-line inhibitor
failure. Nobody has randomised a post-progression endocrine combination against an antibody-drug
conjugate [[BC-890]]. Nobody has randomised the decision to stay endocrine against the decision to
move to chemotherapy in patients who have already had an inhibitor. The capecitabine
comparisons above predate that population. {{trial:viktoria1}} has short follow-up and no survival
data, so it says nothing yet about durability.

## BS-7450 Continuation beyond progression and the switch question

```story SQ-0520,SQ-0560
```

```story SQ-0610
```

Four randomised trials have asked what to do with the inhibitor at progression, and two single-arm
studies asked it without a control. They are usually summarised as showing that continuing the
class helps. Read together they show something more specific and more useful.

Both trials that introduced a different inhibitor were positive. {{trial:maintain}} switched
patients to ribociclib [@kalinsky2023]. {{trial:postmonarch}} switched to abemaciclib
[@kalinsky2025]. Both trials that kept palbociclib were not. {{trial:pace}} continued it
[@mayer2024], and {{trial:palmira}} restarted it after a break [@llombartcussac2025]. The two
single-arm studies met their own thresholds. {{trial:bioper}} rechallenged with palbociclib and a
changed endocrine partner [@albanell2023], and {{trial:triniti-1}} gave ribociclib with everolimus
and exemestane together [@bardia2021]. Neither threshold was set against a comparator, and
{{trial:triniti-1}} changed three drugs at once, so what it shows cannot be assigned to the kinase.

The divergence is in the designs and it runs deeper than switch against continue. The two negative
trials both continued palbociclib. The two positive trials both introduced a drug the patient had
not had. Whether what matters is the act of switching or the identity of the new agent is not
separated by any of them. The endocrine half moved too, and it moved differently in each.
{{trial:maintain}} switched the endocrine partner in both arms, which isolates the inhibitor at the
cost of making the answer one about a switched partner. {{trial:postmonarch}} held fulvestrant in
both arms after aromatase inhibitor failure. {{trial:pace}} switched both arms to fulvestrant and
varied only the palbociclib. Its third arm added avelumab and sat outside that primary comparison.

The decision rests on {{trial:postmonarch}}. It is the only phase III among them, its control arm
is the fulvestrant a clinic would otherwise give, and nothing but the inhibitor differs between its
arms. Its primary endpoint was investigator-assessed, and blinded central review of the same
comparison returned a considerably larger effect, a hazard ratio of 0.55 against 0.73
[@kalinsky2025]. The trial was double blind, so that gap is reader-to-reader variability in
measuring progression rather than bias from knowing the allocation. It is a reminder that the size
of a small effect in this setting is partly a property of who reads the scans.

Three things none of these trials can show. No trial randomised continuation against switching, so
that contrast is across trials with different endocrine designs. Almost every patient in all of
them had progressed on palbociclib, so what is being tested is a second inhibitor after
palbociclib, not after ribociclib or abemaciclib. None has reported overall survival. One more
caution sits in the control arms. Endocrine monotherapy begun at progression performed differently
across these trials. The spread between their control arms is of the same order as the gains being
argued over.

The mechanistic reason for scepticism is set out in [[BS-2500]]. A tumour that progressed on a
CDK4/6 inhibitor is enriched for cells that no longer need CDK4 and CDK6. Blocking them again is
coherent only if the escape was partial, or if the escape was endocrine rather than cell cycle.
That reasoning has been acted on, by bringing mechanisms from outside both classes into the same
resistant population.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- line=3L+ modality=other weight=any status=reported cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,result caption=Mechanisms_tried_outside_the_endocrine_and_cell_cycle_classes_in_endocrine-resistant_disease._Two_of_the_three_required_progression_on_a_CDK4/6_inhibitor_to_enter._The_androgen_receptor_trial_did_not.
```

No randomised comparison in that table beat its own control, and the three fail in different ways.
{{trial:veronica}} is the only clean test of its own hypothesis, because it randomised the addition
of the BCL2 inhibitor to fulvestrant, and the addition gained nothing [@lindeman2022].
{{trial:tbcrc-041}} randomised only whether fulvestrant was added to an Aurora A kinase inhibitor,
so both arms received the experimental drug. Adding fulvestrant did nothing, and what activity the
trial saw belongs to the kinase inhibitor given alone, which has no control anywhere in the trial
[@haddad2023]. {{trial:enobosarm}} randomised one dose of an androgen receptor agonist against
another, with no standard-therapy arm at all [@palmieri2024]. Its clinical benefit rate describes a
cohort and ranks nothing.

None of the three selected patients on the marker its own hypothesis names, which is why each is
negative about a combination and a schedule rather than about a mechanism. {{trial:veronica}}
looked for a BCL2-defined subgroup only after the fact, in small exploratory analyses
[@lindeman2022]. The same failure mode has been repeated at phase III scale in {{trial:e2112}}. It
added a histone deacetylase inhibitor to exemestane without selecting for anything, and moved
neither progression-free survival nor overall survival. Its patients were mostly CDK4/6 naive, so
it tested the reversal of aromatase inhibitor resistance rather than resistance to the combination
[@connolly2021].

The androgen receptor rows raise a measurement problem rather than a biological one.
{{trial:enobosarm}} read its primary endpoint only in patients whose androgen receptor positivity
was confirmed centrally after randomisation, and a quarter of those randomised fall outside that
population [@palmieri2024]. Positivity is a nuclear staining cut-off fixed by protocol rather than
a biological boundary. The antagonist studies in receptor-negative disease, {{trial:tbcrc-011}} and
{{trial:tbcrc-032}}, state the threshold they used [@gucalp2013; @lehmann2019]. The agonist record
does not state its own, so the two conventions cannot be checked against each other. The stain
does not settle the question in any case. {{trial:tbcrc-032}} concluded that receptor protein
expression alone did not identify which tumours depended on the receptor, and an exploratory
analysis separated responders from non-responders better by transcriptional subtype
[@lehmann2019]. Nothing anywhere compares agonism with antagonism in the same tumour, and the two
pharmacologies were tested in populations defined by opposite oestrogen receptor status.

None of this makes switching the inhibitor a good bet on its own merits. It does mean that outside
the pathway inhibitors and the conjugates there is currently nothing better to switch to.

```practice
Ask first what the escape route was. If an ESR1 mutation has appeared, the escape is endocrine. The
cell cycle drug still has a target. Change the endocrine partner rather than the inhibitor
[[BC-870]].

If the patient progressed within a few months of starting, treat that as pre-existing insensitivity
rather than acquired resistance, and do not expect a second inhibitor to work [[BS-2510]].

If you continue the class, change the agent. Both positive trials gave a drug the patient had not
had. Both negative trials gave back the one that had failed.

Set the expectation before starting. The median gain in these trials is a small number of months. A
patient who expects the response they had first time will be disappointed by a result the trial
would call a success.

Weigh the alternative honestly. In a patient with a PIK3CA, AKT1 or PTEN alteration, or with
visceral disease that needs a response, the comparator is not placebo. It is a pathway inhibitor
[[BC-870]] or a conjugate [[BC-890]].
```

## BS-7460 First-line use against deferred use

```story SQ-0470
```

{{trial:sonia}} is the most important trial in this chapter for practice and the most frequently
misdescribed. It deserves a careful reading of its design before any reading of its result.

The trial randomised patients with no prior therapy for advanced disease to one of two strategies
rather than to two drugs [@sonke2024]. In one, the inhibitor was given first line with an aromatase
inhibitor, and second line was fulvestrant alone. In the other, first line was an aromatase
inhibitor alone, and the inhibitor was added to fulvestrant at second line. Both arms received the
inhibitor. Only the timing differed.

The primary endpoint was second progression-free survival, measured from randomisation to the
second objective progression or death. That endpoint is not a technicality and it is the reason the
trial can answer the question at all. Progression-free survival on first-line therapy would
necessarily favour the arm given two drugs first. Only an endpoint running across both lines can
compare strategies rather than treatments.

Neither that endpoint nor overall survival separated the two strategies [@sonke2024;
@wortelboer2026]. Giving the inhibitor first produced a far longer median duration of inhibitor
treatment and far more grade 3 or higher adverse events [@sonke2024]. Much more drug, a much longer
window of toxicity, and no measurable difference in either endpoint.

Two later analyses restrain the simple reading and both point the same way. A post hoc subgroup
analysis found a survival advantage for first-line use in premenopausal patients and none in
postmenopausal patients, with a significant test for interaction [@wortelboer2026]. A circulating
tumour DNA analysis then divided patients by baseline genome-wide aneuploidy score. Those with a
high score gained on the across-line endpoint from first-line use, and those with a low score did
not [@jongbloed2025]. A trial that is negative overall is not negative in everyone.

```caution
{{trial:sonia}} does not say that first-line CDK4/6 inhibition is unnecessary. It says four narrower
things. Deferral costs nothing measurable in the average patient across both lines. The trial
permitted investigator choice of inhibitor, so its result travels only as far as the agents
actually used. Those agents do not carry the same survival evidence. Premenopausal patients and
patients with high baseline aneuploidy may be exceptions [@wortelboer2026; @jongbloed2025]. And the
trial excluded nobody for disease tempo, so it cannot speak to the patient who needs a response
now. That patient is the subject of [[BS-4890]].
```

One further limit is easy to miss. The deferred arm is underwritten by second-line trials run in
patients who had never had an inhibitor, which is exactly the population that no longer exists
[[BS-7440]]. A patient who defers today is being offered a second line whose evidence base was
generated in someone else.

The economic consequence is the part most likely to change practice at a system level. A strategy
that delivers the same outcome with a fraction of the drug exposure is a different proposition for
a health service than for an individual patient. That distinction is developed in [[BC-700]].

## BS-7470 Resistance mechanisms that change the next choice

Most progression on a CDK4/6 inhibitor is not explained by any measurable mechanism. That sentence
should come first, because the literature on this subject is much larger than the fraction of
patients it accounts for.

Five mechanisms are documented well enough to discuss. They divide into two groups with opposite
implications.

Cell cycle escape removes the drug's target. RB1 loss is the clearest example and it is uncommon,
appearing in the palbociclib arm alone in the paired analysis described in [[BS-2500]]
[@oleary2018]. High cyclin E1 expression predicts poor benefit from palbociclib and describes a
cell cycle driven through CDK2 rather than CDK4 and CDK6 [@turner2019ccne1]. FAT1 loss raises CDK6
through the Hippo pathway [@li2018fat1]. In all three the kinase the drug blocks has become
dispensable, so more of the same class is a poor bet.

Endocrine escape leaves the target intact. ESR1 mutation confers ligand independence without
touching the cell cycle machinery [[BS-2490]]. PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN alterations activate a
parallel growth pathway [[BC-170]]. In both the inhibitor may still be doing useful work. The
alteration then names a drug for the other half of the regimen.

The measurement caveat is the reason none of this is routine. RB1 loss can be found in circulating
tumour DNA or by immunohistochemistry. Neither has an established threshold for action. Cyclin E1
was measured as messenger RNA in a trial analysis, not by any assay in clinical use
[@turner2019ccne1]. FAT1 is not on most commercial panels. Only ESR1 and the PI3K pathway
alterations are both detectable and actionable today.

```interplay target=BC-350
The cell cycle and endocrine escape routes make the same abstract point from opposite directions.
A resistance mechanism is clinically useful only when it is detectable, interpretable and attached
to a drug. ESR1 mutation clears all three bars and has changed practice. Cyclin E1 clears the first
and fails the third, so it explains a failure without preventing one. The gap between understanding
resistance and acting on it is a measurement and pharmacology gap, not a biology gap, which is the
argument [[BS-6710]] makes about precision medicine generally.
```

## BS-7480 Toxicity differences that drive agent selection

Toxicity is where the three agents genuinely diverge. In a patient who will take the drug for years
it is often the deciding variable.

Palbociclib produces neutropenia and comparatively little else. Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia occurred
in 66.4% of patients in {{trial:paloma2}} [@finn2016]. Febrile neutropenia was rare. The
mismatch between those two statements is the point. The neutropenia is a reversible cytostatic
effect on the marrow rather than the cytotoxic depletion seen with chemotherapy, so it produces
alarming counts and few infections.

Ribociclib produces similar neutropenia and adds two monitoring requirements the others do not
have. It prolongs the QT interval, so electrocardiograms are needed at baseline and again in the
first weeks of treatment. It causes transaminase elevation, so liver function requires scheduled
checks. Both are manageable and both need a system rather than an intention.

Abemaciclib is given continuously and its dominant toxicity is diarrhoea. In {{trial:monarch3}} it
affected 81.3% of patients at any grade [@goetz2017]. Neutropenia is less prominent. Serum
creatinine rises in many patients through inhibition of renal transporters, which is not a change
in glomerular filtration and does not by itself require dose reduction.

```practice
Match the agent to the life, not only to the disease.

Choose ribociclib where survival evidence is the priority and the patient can attend for
electrocardiograms and blood tests early in treatment.

Avoid abemaciclib first where baseline bowel frequency is already a problem, where a stoma is
present, or where the patient lives far from support. Prescribe loperamide at the first cycle
rather than on request.

Prefer abemaciclib or ribociclib where marrow reserve is poor after previous chemotherapy, and
review counts early rather than at the end of the first cycle.

Remember that palbociclib and ribociclib have a week off in every cycle and abemaciclib does not.
Patients plan around that week, and it is a reason some prefer the intermittent schedule.

Check the creatinine trajectory rather than the single value on abemaciclib. A rise that plateaus
early and is unaccompanied by electrolyte change is the expected transporter effect.
```

## BS-7490 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 status=ongoing,awaited weight=any cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,status caption=Combination_trials_involving_CDK4/6_inhibition_that_have_not_reported.
```

Not one of those five trials compares inhibitors. They ask what should be given alongside one, and
they fall into three groups.

Three replace the endocrine half of the first-line doublet and hold the inhibitor constant.
{{trial:serena4}} and {{trial:persevera}} test an oral receptor degrader against an aromatase
inhibitor, and {{trial:veritac3}} tests a degrader built as a targeted protein degradation
molecule. All three keep palbociclib in both arms. That makes them the cleanest test yet of whether
the endocrine partner, rather than the kinase, is what limits how long the first-line combination
holds.

{{trial:capitello292}} adds AKT inhibition to a CDK4/6 inhibitor and fulvestrant, in a
biomarker-unselected population spanning first and second line. It will say whether the pathway
should be blocked in everybody or only in the patients an alteration names [[BC-870]].

{{trial:abigail}} asks the tempo question the first-line trials avoided. It compares immediate
abemaciclib with a course of paclitaxel given first, in aggressive disease, which is the same
clinical situation {{trial:right-choice}} addressed from the other direction. It is a phase II, and
its eligibility criteria are not on the record, so whatever it reports will describe a population
that cannot be specified.

Three questions this chapter raises have no trial attached, and they are the ones a reader will
keep meeting in clinic.

No trial compares the three inhibitors head to head, in any line. The survival differences
described above will therefore never be resolved by direct comparison. Any future attempt would
have to be very large to detect a difference of the size at issue.

No trial randomises continuation of the same inhibitor against a switch to a different one. The
inference that switching is what works rests on a contrast across four trials with different
endocrine designs, in patients who had almost all received palbociclib.

No trial has tested deferred first-line use in the populations where {{trial:sonia}} suggested an
exception. A confirmatory trial in premenopausal patients, or in patients selected by baseline
aneuploidy, would convert two subgroup findings into a rule. Until one exists, the safest reading
is that {{trial:sonia}} licenses deferral in the average postmenopausal patient and not in the
patient in front of you who resembles one of its exceptions.
