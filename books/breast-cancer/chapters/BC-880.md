## BS-7420 Scope, and why the three agents are not interchangeable

This chapter covers CDK4/6 inhibition in metastatic hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative
disease. It covers the first-line combinations and what each has shown for survival, the evidence
after first progression, whether to continue the class beyond progression, whether to give it
first at all, the resistance mechanisms worth testing for, and the toxicity differences that
decide agent choice in clinic.

The endocrine partner is argued in [[BC-870]], which also covers what to do when an ESR1 mutation
appears. Conjugates and chemotherapy are in [[BC-890]]. Cell cycle biology is in [[BC-160]],
resistance mechanism detail in [[BS-2980]], and agent pharmacology in [[BC-450]]. The clinical
framing of the whole branch is in [[BC-560]].

Palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib are routinely described as a class. They inhibit the same
two kinases and they produce broadly similar effects on progression-free survival in the first
line. Three things separate them and none is visible in a class label.

They differ in what has been shown for survival, which is the subject of the next two sections.
They differ in dominant toxicity, which is the subject of the last. They differ in schedule and
monitoring burden, which determines what a year on treatment is like.

The story below is the argument the rest of this chapter is evidence for, and it carries no
figures at all. It states what each group of trials was built to find out, what came back, and
what the design cannot settle, so the tables are read as answers to a question rather than as a
list. It is one node of the [Chapter Stories](stories.html) map.

## BS-7430 First-line combinations and their overall survival results

Five randomised phase III trials established the first-line combinations for the three agents in
wide use. All five produced hazard ratios for progression between 0.54 and 0.59. Their survival
results do not agree, and that disagreement is the most consequential fact in this chapter. The
table carries two more rows that do not belong to that set. {{trial:paloma-1-trio-18}} is the phase
II that led to palbociclib's accelerated approval and is superseded by PALOMA-2.
{{trial:dawna-2}} tested dalpiciclib, a fourth inhibitor licensed in China and not elsewhere.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 line=1L status=reported cols=acronym,n,arms,result,os caption=First-line_trials._The_overall_survival_column_is_where_the_three_agents_separate.
```

Ribociclib has three survival results pointing the same way. {{trial:monaleesa2}} was positive in
postmenopausal patients with letrozole [@hortobagyi2022]. {{trial:monaleesa7}} was positive in pre-
and perimenopausal patients with ovarian suppression [@lu2022]. {{trial:monaleesa3}} was positive
with fulvestrant in a population mixing first and second line, so it speaks less cleanly to the
first-line question than the other two [@slamon2020].

Palbociclib has one first-line survival result and it was not significant [@slamon2024paloma2].
Abemaciclib has one and it was also not significant, with a difference between medians of about
13 months and a confidence interval crossing one [@goetz2024].

One feature of the palbociclib result is a measurement problem rather than a biological one, and
the authors say so. Survival status was unknown at analysis for 13.3% of patients in the
palbociclib arm and 21.2% in the placebo arm [@slamon2024paloma2]. Unequal loss to follow-up of
that size, in the direction that removes control-arm deaths from view, limits what the comparison
can support. A negative result with a censoring imbalance is not the same object as a negative
result without one.

Three explanations compete and none has been established. The drugs may differ pharmacologically,
since they are not equipotent against the two kinases and are not given on the same schedule. The
trials may differ in conduct and follow-up, which the palbociclib censoring imbalance demonstrates
directly. Or the survival differences may be noise, given how closely the progression-free survival
results agree. No first-line trial in this group carried overall survival as its primary endpoint.

```caution
Comparing overall survival medians across {{trial:monaleesa2}}, {{trial:paloma2}} and
{{trial:monarch3}} is not a comparison of the three drugs. The endocrine partners, the enrolment
years, the permitted subsequent therapies and the follow-up durations all differ. No head-to-head
trial exists and none is planned. The strongest claim the evidence supports is that ribociclib has
the most consistent survival evidence in the first line, in three separate populations. It does not
support the claim that palbociclib or abemaciclib shortens life relative to ribociclib.
```

## BS-7440 Second-line and later combinations

The second-line evidence divides into two eras and conflating them is the commonest error here.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 line=2L,3L+ status=reported cols=acronym,phase,n,population,result,os caption=Trials_after_first_progression._The_upper_group_predates_routine_first-line_CDK4/6_inhibition;_the_continuation_trials_do_not.
```

{{trial:monarch2}} and {{trial:paloma3}} enrolled patients progressing on endocrine therapy who had
not previously received a CDK4/6 inhibitor [@sledge2017; @cristofanilli2016]. {{trial:monarch2}}
showed a survival benefit [@sledge2020]. {{trial:paloma3}} did not reach significance overall, and
its benefit was concentrated in patients with documented sensitivity to prior endocrine therapy
[@turner2018paloma3]. That subgroup finding is consistent with the resistance definitions in
[[BS-4810]] and is one of the few places where the clinical timing convention predicts something.

The problem is that this population has almost disappeared. A patient today reaching second line
has nearly always had a CDK4/6 inhibitor already. These two trials therefore describe a decision
that is now rare, and quoting them to justify a second-line inhibitor after first-line inhibitor
failure is quoting the wrong evidence.

The evidence that does describe current second-line practice is the continuation and switch
literature, developed in the next section. One further trial belongs here because its combination
arm is genuinely second-line in the modern sense. {{trial:ember3}} randomised a third arm to
imlunestrant with abemaciclib, and the combination outperformed imlunestrant alone irrespective of
ESR1 status [@jhaveri2025ember3]. Most of those patients had received a CDK4/6 inhibitor before.

Two readings of that arm are possible and the trial does not separate them. Adding a CDK4/6
inhibitor to a receptor degrader may recover cell cycle control that endocrine therapy alone cannot
deliver. Or the comparison may simply reflect that two drugs beat one. The toxicity cost of the
combination was substantial and is in [[BS-4840]].

## BS-7450 Continuation beyond progression and the switch question

Four randomised trials have asked what to do with the inhibitor at progression. They are usually
summarised as showing that continuing the class helps. Read carefully, they show something more
specific and more useful.

Two trials switched to a different inhibitor and both were positive. {{trial:maintain}} switched
patients, most of whom had received palbociclib, to ribociclib while also changing the endocrine
partner [@kalinsky2023]. {{trial:postmonarch}} switched to abemaciclib with fulvestrant, again in a
population that had mostly received palbociclib [@kalinsky2025].

Two trials continued the same inhibitor and both were negative. {{trial:pace}} continued
palbociclib with a switch to fulvestrant [@mayer2024]. {{trial:palmira}} rechallenged with
palbociclib alongside a different endocrine partner [@llombartcussac2025].

The pattern is clean and the limits of it should be stated plainly. No trial has randomised
continuation against switching, so the contrast above is across trials rather than within one.
{{trial:maintain}} changed two variables at once, the inhibitor and the endocrine partner, so it
cannot attribute its result to either. The absolute gains in all four trials are measured in
one to three months of median progression-free survival.

The mechanistic reason for scepticism is set out in [[BS-2500]]. A tumour that progressed on a
CDK4/6 inhibitor is enriched for cells that no longer need CDK4 and CDK6. Blocking them again is
coherent only if the escape was partial, or if the escape was endocrine rather than cell cycle.

```practice
Ask first what the escape route was. If an ESR1 mutation has appeared, the escape is endocrine, the
cell cycle drug still has a target, and the right move is to change the endocrine partner rather
than the inhibitor [[BC-870]].

If the patient progressed within a few months of starting, treat that as pre-existing insensitivity
rather than acquired resistance, and do not expect a second inhibitor to work [[BS-2510]].

If you continue the class, switch the agent. Both positive trials switched. Both negative trials
continued the same drug.

Set the expectation before starting. The median gain in these trials is a small number of months,
and a patient who expects the response they had first time will be disappointed by a result that
the trial would call a success.

Weigh the alternative honestly. In a patient with a PIK3CA, AKT1 or PTEN alteration, or with
visceral disease that needs a response, the comparator is not placebo. It is a pathway inhibitor
[[BC-870]] or a conjugate [[BC-890]].
```

## BS-7460 First-line use against deferred use

{{trial:sonia}} is the most important trial in this chapter for practice and the most frequently
misdescribed. It deserves a careful reading of its design before any reading of its result.

The trial randomised 1,050 patients with no prior therapy for advanced disease to one of two
strategies [@sonke2024]. In one, the CDK4/6 inhibitor was given first line with an aromatase
inhibitor, and second line was fulvestrant alone. In the other, first line was an aromatase
inhibitor alone, and the inhibitor was added to fulvestrant at second line. Both arms therefore
received the inhibitor. Only the timing differed.

The primary endpoint was second progression-free survival, measured from randomisation to the
second objective disease progression or death. That endpoint is not a technicality and it is the
reason the trial can answer the question at all. Progression-free survival on first-line therapy
would necessarily favour the arm given two drugs first. Only an endpoint that runs across both
lines can compare strategies rather than treatments. The choice of PFS2 is what makes
{{trial:sonia}} a strategy trial rather than a drug trial.

The result was no significant difference in second progression-free survival and none in overall
survival [@sonke2024; @wortelboer2026]. Giving the inhibitor first produced a median 24.6 months of
inhibitor exposure against 8.1 months, and more grade 3 or worse adverse events [@sonke2024]. Three
times the drug, three times the toxicity window, and no measurable difference in either endpoint.

Two prespecified or post hoc analyses restrain the simple reading and both point the same way.
A post hoc subgroup analysis found a survival benefit from first-line use in premenopausal
patients, with a hazard ratio of 0.53, none in postmenopausal patients, and a test for interaction
giving P equal to 0.01 [@wortelboer2026]. A prespecified circulating tumour DNA analysis of 409
patients divided them by baseline genome-wide aneuploidy score. Those with a high score benefited
from first-line use, with a hazard ratio of 0.58. Those with a low score did not, at 1.36
[@jongbloed2025]. A trial that is negative overall is not negative in everyone.

```caution
{{trial:sonia}} does not say that first-line CDK4/6 inhibition is unnecessary. It says four narrower
things. Deferral costs nothing measurable in the average patient across both lines. The trial
permitted investigator choice of inhibitor, so its result travels only as far as the agents actually
used and those agents do not carry the same survival evidence. Premenopausal patients and patients
with high baseline aneuploidy may be exceptions [@wortelboer2026; @jongbloed2025]. And the trial
excluded nobody for disease tempo, so it cannot speak to the patient who needs a response now.
That patient is the subject of [[BS-4890]].
```

The economic consequence is the part most likely to change practice at a system level. A strategy
that delivers the same outcome with a third of the drug exposure is a different proposition for a
health service than for an individual patient, and that distinction is developed in [[BC-700]].

## BS-7470 Resistance mechanisms that change the next choice

Most progression on a CDK4/6 inhibitor is not explained by any measurable mechanism. That sentence
should come first, because the literature on this subject is much larger than the fraction of
patients it accounts for.

Four mechanisms are documented well enough to discuss and they divide into two groups with
opposite implications.

Cell cycle escape removes the drug's target. RB1 loss is the clearest example and it is uncommon,
appearing in the palbociclib arm alone in the paired analysis described in [[BS-2500]]
[@oleary2018]. High cyclin E1 expression predicts poor benefit from palbociclib and describes a
cell cycle driven through CDK2 rather than CDK4 and CDK6 [@turner2019ccne1]. FAT1 loss raises CDK6
through the Hippo pathway [@li2018fat1]. In all three the kinase the drug blocks has become
dispensable, so more of the same class is a poor bet.

Endocrine escape leaves the target intact. ESR1 mutation confers ligand independence without
touching the cell cycle machinery [[BS-2490]]. PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN alterations activate a
parallel growth pathway [[BC-170]]. In both the inhibitor may still be doing useful work. The
alteration then names a drug for the other half of the regimen.

The measurement caveat is the reason none of this is routine. RB1 loss can be found in circulating
tumour DNA or by immunohistochemistry. Neither has an established threshold for action. Cyclin
E1 was measured as messenger RNA in a trial analysis, not by any assay in clinical use
[@turner2019ccne1]. FAT1 is not on most commercial panels. Only ESR1 and the PI3K pathway
alterations are both detectable and actionable today.

```interplay target=BC-350
The cell cycle and endocrine escape routes make the same abstract point from opposite directions.
A resistance mechanism is clinically useful only when it is detectable, interpretable and attached
to a drug. ESR1 mutation clears all three bars and has changed practice. Cyclin E1 clears the first
and fails the third, so it explains a failure without preventing one. The gap between understanding
resistance and acting on it is a measurement and pharmacology gap, not a biology gap, which is the
argument [[BS-6710]] makes about precision medicine generally.
```

## BS-7480 Toxicity differences that drive agent selection

Toxicity is where the three agents genuinely diverge. In a patient who will take the drug for
years it is often the deciding variable.

Palbociclib produces neutropenia and comparatively little else. Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia occurred
in 66.4% of patients in {{trial:paloma2}}, with febrile neutropenia in 1.8% [@finn2016]. The
mismatch between those two figures is the point. The neutropenia is a reversible cytostatic effect
on the marrow rather than the cytotoxic depletion seen with chemotherapy, so it produces alarming
counts and few infections.

Ribociclib produces similar neutropenia and adds two monitoring requirements the others do not
have. It prolongs the QT interval, so an electrocardiogram is needed at baseline, around day 14 of
the first cycle and at the start of the second. It causes transaminase elevation, so liver function
requires scheduled checks. Both are manageable and both need a system rather than an intention.

Abemaciclib is given continuously and its dominant toxicity is diarrhoea. In {{trial:monarch3}} it
occurred in 81.3% of patients at any grade and 9.5% at grade 3 or 4 [@goetz2017]. Neutropenia is less
prominent. Serum creatinine rises in many patients through inhibition of renal transporters, which
is not a change in glomerular filtration and does not by itself require dose reduction.

```practice
Match the agent to the life, not only to the disease.

Choose ribociclib where survival evidence is the priority and the patient can attend for
electrocardiograms and blood tests early in treatment.

Avoid abemaciclib first where baseline bowel frequency is already a problem, where a stoma is
present, or where the patient lives far from support. Prescribe loperamide at the first cycle
rather than on request.

Prefer abemaciclib or ribociclib where marrow reserve is poor after previous chemotherapy, and
review counts early rather than at the end of the first cycle.

Remember that palbociclib and ribociclib have a week off in every cycle and abemaciclib does not.
Patients plan around that week, and it is a reason some prefer the intermittent schedule.

Check the creatinine trajectory rather than the single value on abemaciclib. A rise that plateaus
early and is unaccompanied by electrolyte change is the expected transporter effect.
```

## BS-7490 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=cdk4-6 status=ongoing,awaited cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,status caption=Combination_trials_involving_CDK4/6_inhibition_that_have_not_reported.
```

The running trials mostly ask what should be combined with the inhibitor rather than which
inhibitor to use. {{trial:serena4}}, {{trial:veritac3}} and {{trial:persevera}} test whether an oral
receptor degrader should replace the aromatase inhibitor in the first-line doublet.
{{trial:capitello292}} and {{trial:viktoria1}} test whether pathway inhibition should be added to
continued CDK4/6 blockade after first progression.

Three questions this chapter raises have no trial attached and they are the ones a reader will keep
meeting in clinic.

No trial compares the three inhibitors head to head, in any line. The survival differences
described above will therefore never be resolved by direct comparison. Any future attempt would
have to be very large to detect a difference of the size at issue.

No trial randomises continuation of the same inhibitor against a switch to a different one. The
inference that switching is what works rests on a contrast across four trials with different
designs.

No trial has tested deferred first-line use in the populations where {{trial:sonia}} suggested an
exception. A confirmatory trial in premenopausal patients, or in patients selected by baseline
aneuploidy, would convert two subgroup findings into a rule. Until one exists, the safest reading
is that {{trial:sonia}} licenses deferral in the average postmenopausal patient and not in the
patient in front of you who resembles one of its exceptions.
