## BS-6900 Scope, and why the neoadjuvant setting is harder in this subtype

This chapter covers preoperative systemic therapy for operable and locally advanced hormone
receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. Hormone receptor-positive HER2-positive disease is
a different entity with its own algorithm, and it has its own chapter in [[BC-840]].

Several neighbours own things this chapter deliberately does not. The machinery of the neoadjuvant
platform belongs to [[BC-540]]. The surrogacy argument attached to pathological complete response
is methodology and belongs to [[BC-740]] and [[BS-6360]]. The decision architecture that puts a
patient into this pathway at all belongs to [[BC-490]]. Adjuvant endocrine therapy belongs to
[[BC-790]], adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition to [[BC-800]], and the genomic assay decision made on the
surgical specimen to [[BS-4300]]. What this chapter owns is the trial record of what is given
before the operation.

The organising fact is uncomfortable. Everywhere else in early breast cancer the neoadjuvant
setting earns its place because a pathological complete response is both achievable and
informative. Here it is neither, because complete responses in this subtype are uncommon. The
pooled analysis that established the prognostic value of the endpoint found the association weakest
in exactly this group [@cortazar2014]. A separate pooled analysis of seven randomised trials found
no association at all between complete response and disease-free survival in luminal A tumours
[@vonminckwitz2012].

Preoperative treatment here is therefore an experiment at least as much as a treatment. Nearly
every endpoint read before the operation is a proliferation measurement standing in for an outcome.
Nearly every question in this chapter turns on whether that substitution holds.

Each section below opens with the question its trials were built to answer, taken from the
[Chapter Stories](stories.html) map, and closes with a table filtered on that same question. One
warning belongs at the front rather than at the back. A table filtered on this subtype also prints
every trial that enrolled without restriction by receptor status, because such a trial legitimately
contained this cell. The rate printed on those rows is a mixed-population rate. Which rows are
receptor-restricted and which are not is argued in each section, and it changes what each row is
worth.

## BS-6910 Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and the low pathological complete response rate

```story SQ-0080
```

Two separate facts with different causes and different consequences are usually run together here.

The first is that complete responses are uncommon. That is a property of the disease rather than of
the regimen. Slowly proliferating, oestrogen-driven tumours are not eradicated by cytotoxic therapy
at the rate that highly proliferative ones are. Intensifying the regimen moves the rate a little.
It does not move it into the range where a binary endpoint discriminates usefully between patients.

The second is that, within this subtype, achieving a complete response separates outcomes less
sharply than it does elsewhere [@cortazar2014]. The two facts compound each other. Few patients
reach the category, and reaching it says less about them than it would in triple-negative disease.

Most of the rows below were not generated in this cell. NSABP B-18, NSABP B-27, GeparSepto and
I-SPY 2 enrolled without restriction by receptor status. ETNA, GeparOLA, INFORM and the I-SPY 2
pembrolizumab arm enrolled HER2-negative disease of both receptor states. Two of those selected on
germline BRCA status or on homologous recombination deficiency rather than on the receptor at all. Only CheckMate 7FL, KEYNOTE-756, NeoPAL and CORALLEEN required hormone receptor positivity to
enter. That distinction, and not the regimen, explains most of the spread in the result column.

Where the randomised record agrees is on timing. Giving the same chemotherapy before the operation
rather than after it changes the operation and leaves disease-free and overall survival where they
were. {{trial:nsabp-b-18}} is the trial that decision rests on, because it randomised the sequence
and nothing else [@fisher1998a; @wolmark2001]. {{trial:nsabp-b-27}} then added the finding that
makes this chapter necessary. Preoperative docetaxel doubled the pathological complete response
rate without moving disease-free or overall survival [@bear2006]. A response endpoint moved, and
the outcome it is supposed to stand for did not.

The two nab-paclitaxel trials look like a disagreement about a drug and are not one.
{{trial:geparsepto-gbg-69}} found nab-paclitaxel ahead of solvent-based paclitaxel.
{{trial:etna}} found the two indistinguishable. GeparSepto enrolled every subtype, including
HER2-positive disease treated with dual antibody blockade, and scored pathological complete
response as ypT0 ypN0. ETNA enrolled HER2-negative disease only and scored ypT0/is ypN0, a
definition that counts residual in situ carcinoma as a response. The nab-paclitaxel dose differed
between them, and GeparSepto reduced its own dose partway through accrual [@untch2016; @gianni2018].
Population, response definition and dose all differ. The drug does not.

The repair-deficiency rows behave as one finding. {{trial:inform}} substituted cisplatin for
doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide in germline BRCA carriers [@tung2020a]. {{trial:geparola}}
substituted olaparib for carboplatin alongside paclitaxel in tumours selected for homologous
recombination deficiency [@fasching2020]. Neither substitution improved on the backbone it
replaced, and in GeparOLA the long-term invasive disease-free and overall survival estimates ran
against the experimental arm rather than with it [@fasching2025a]. Both trials were majority
triple-negative. Neither carries a receptor-positive result of its own.

That leaves four rows whose chemotherapy arm was given to a receptor-restricted, luminal
population. Those control arms are the honest estimate of what cytotoxic therapy achieves at
surgery in this cell, and they are the column to read first. {{trial:ispy2}} is the one row that
connects the endpoint to an outcome, and it does so across all subtypes rather than within this one
[@yee2020].

The harder version of the question is whether response can be used to withhold chemotherapy
altogether. Two families of trial have tried and they did not reach the same standard of proof.
Response-guided omission has a noninferiority result behind it, from a programme that allocated by
genomic score and by endocrine response rather than by randomisation [@nitz2022]. Its window is
preoperative and the therapy it decides is adjuvant, so it does not test preoperative treatment at
all. Substituting a CDK4/6 inhibitor for chemotherapy has only similar estimates behind it, read on
endpoints scored at surgery. Similar estimates are not noninferiority. None of the substitution
trials carried a margin or the size to test one [[BS-6370]].

What the table cannot show is the comparison that matters most. No row randomised chemotherapy
against no chemotherapy in this subtype. No column reports the distribution of residual disease
rather than its absence, and residual cancer burden is continuous and remains prognostic here with
a shallower gradient than in triple-negative disease [@symmans2017; @yau2022]. The index and its
measurement caveats are set out in [[BS-4680]]. A report giving only the presence or absence of a
complete response has discarded the part of the measurement that still carries information
[[term:rcb]]. No column records whether the operation changed, which is the reason chemotherapy is
given before surgery here in the first place.

```caution
A low complete response rate is routinely read as chemoresistance, which is a different claim.
Adjuvant chemotherapy reduces recurrence in selected hormone receptor-positive disease, in
populations where almost nobody would have achieved a complete response had they been treated
preoperatively [[BS-4310]]. Sensitivity measured by eradication and benefit measured by recurrence
are different quantities. The first systematically understates the second whenever the tumour grows
slowly.
```

## BS-6920 Neoadjuvant endocrine therapy and who it suits

```story SQ-0090,SQ-0100
```

Neoadjuvant endocrine therapy is the one preoperative strategy in this subtype whose rationale
never depended on a pathological endpoint.

The six randomised comparisons agree on two things. Three to six months of endocrine therapy
produces measurable regression in a substantial minority of receptor-rich tumours, enough to
convert mastectomy candidates to conservation. And within the aromatase inhibitor class the choice
of drug is immaterial. {{trial:z1031}} randomised exemestane, letrozole and anastrozole against
each other and separated them on nothing but the clinical response rate used to pick two agents
forward [@ellis2011].

The disagreement is entirely between aromatase inhibitor and tamoxifen, and it is a disagreement
about method. {{trial:p024}} and {{trial:stage}} found the aromatase inhibitor clearly ahead.
{{trial:impact}} and {{trial:proact}} found the difference small and not significant. Four features
separate the two pairs and none of them is pharmacological. P024 ran four months in women who were
not candidates for conservation, and scored response by clinical palpation [@eiermann2001]. STAGE
ran twenty-four weeks in premenopausal women on goserelin, with matching placebos, and scored by
callipers [@masuda2012]. IMPACT ran three months and scored by callipers and by ultrasound, and the
two instruments returned different rates inside the one trial [@smith2005]. PROACT ran twelve weeks
and permitted concurrent chemotherapy, which contaminates the endocrine comparison outright
[@cataliotti2006]. Duration, menopausal status, measuring instrument and contamination all vary
across these four. Read the endpoint column before the result column.

The decision rests on P024. It is the randomised double-blind comparison against the drug the
clinic would otherwise have given, and it is the one that moved a surgical endpoint rather than a
biomarker. STAGE is what extends the class below the menopause, because it holds ovarian
suppression constant in both arms and varies only the partner drug.

Escalating within the receptor axis has now been tested twice and has not moved the ceiling. Z1031
found nothing between the three aromatase inhibitors. {{trial:alternate}} gave a full six-month
preoperative course and found that fulvestrant, alone or added to anastrozole, did not improve the
proportion of tumours reaching the endocrine-sensitive state [@ma2024alternate]. The ceiling is low
and it is set by the tumour rather than by the drug.

The oral receptor degraders are absent from this table by design, and the reason is the endpoint
rather than the result. coopERA, AMEERA-4 and ELIPSE ran two-week and four-week exposures whose
read-out is a ratio of two Ki67 scores [@hurvitz2023a; @campone2023; @vidal2025]. Giredestrant
suppressed proliferation more than anastrozole over that window. Amcenestrant matched letrozole
without beating it, in a trial stopped early for slow accrual. Elacestrant was single-arm and read
against an arrest threshold. None of the three scored tumour response, so none of them is
commensurate with the six trials in the table [[BS-4700]].

Who it suits follows from the biology rather than from the trials. Strongly oestrogen
receptor-positive, low-grade tumours are the ones that respond. Older or less fit patients are the
group in whom the toxicity comparison against chemotherapy is most one-sided.

Lobular carcinoma has the strongest prior claim on this strategy and the weakest evidence behind
it. Lobular tumours lose E-cadherin and infiltrate in single files rather than forming a discrete
mass. They are almost always receptor-positive, low grade and poorly responsive to cytotoxic
treatment. Both halves of the neoadjuvant argument therefore change at once, and the instruments
used to score response in the trials above stop working on a tumour with no discrete mass.
{{trial:tbcrc-037}} is the only histology-specific design, and it is a phase 1 target-engagement
study that cannot rank endocrine agents for efficacy however it reads out. {{trial:pelops}} is
named for lobular disease and enrolled any histology, and the only publication on its record is an
axillary analysis whose endpoints the paper itself calls prespecified exploratory [@weiss2025].
The histology argument rests on biology and on subgroups borrowed from elsewhere.

Duration is where practice most often goes wrong, because response accrues over months rather than
weeks. Every trial in the table used three to six months of exposure. There is no reason to expect
an eight-week course to produce comparable downstaging. Shortened courses are the commonest
avoidable cause of a disappointing result.

What the table cannot show is any survival column, because none of these trials had one. Nobody
randomised preoperative endocrine therapy against preoperative chemotherapy in a trial powered to
compare them. Nobody enrolled a lobular-only population.

```practice
Fix the goal before the first prescription. Surgical downstaging and measurement of endocrine
sensitivity are different objectives with different stopping rules.

Plan at least four months, reassessing by examination and imaging at intervals rather than waiting
for a fixed date.

Take a baseline Ki67 and a repeat at two to four weeks only if the result will change a decision.
If it will not, the biopsy is not justified by the information.

Treat progression on neoadjuvant endocrine therapy as a significant event rather than an ordinary
one. It is uncommon, and it identifies a tumour unlikely to be controlled by the same drug class
given for the next five years.
```

## BS-6930 Ki67, PEPI and response measures for a setting where pCR fails

```story SQ-0060
```

If the binary endpoint carries little information, something has to replace it. The four trials
here agree on the biology and disagree on almost everything about the measurement.

The agreement is that endocrine therapy alone leaves a proliferating fraction in most tumours, and
that the size of that fraction after a short exposure grades how far the tumour depends on
oestrogen. {{trial:poetic}} is where that becomes a clinical statement rather than a
pharmacodynamic one. Among treated HER2-negative women, five-year recurrence risk ran from 4.3%
where Ki67 was low before and after treatment to 21.5% where it was high at both points
[@smith2020]. The trial is load-bearing for an unusual reason. Its intervention changed no outcome,
so the prognostic signal cannot be an effect of the two weeks of treatment. The measurement carries
the information and the drug does not.

The divergence is exposure. POETIC read fourteen perioperative days. Z1031 and neoMONARCH read
weeks. ALTERNATE read six months, with a switch to chemotherapy triggered at week four or week
twelve. A threshold calibrated on one of these exposures does not transfer to another, which is why
these four cannot be pooled on a cut-point. {{trial:neomonarch}} sits in this table because Ki67 is
its endpoint rather than because it asks the same question, and what it does to the threshold is
argued in [[BS-6940]].

Two conventions coexist and they are not interchangeable. Complete cell-cycle arrest is set at a
Ki67 of 2.7% or below, which is a pharmacodynamic threshold used in window studies. Endocrine
response is set at 10% or below, which is an allocation threshold used to direct treatment. Neither
boundary was placed where biology changes. Both were placed where observers could agree.

The measurement side deserves as much attention as the biology. Ki67 scoring varies between
laboratories and observers even under a common protocol [@polley2013], which is why successive
working group statements concentrate on method rather than on cut-point [@dowsett2011;
@nielsen2021]. The largest contemporary test of that comes from the trial now using post-endocrine
Ki67 to allocate treatment, where local and central scoring agreed on the binary endocrine-response
call in 80.5% of specimens [@hamann2025]. One reading in five would have sent the patient the other
way.

The preoperative endocrine prognostic index answers a different question, which is what to do after
surgery. It combines pathological tumour size, nodal status, Ki67 and oestrogen receptor status
measured in the surgical specimen. It was derived in P024 and validated in IMPACT. A score of zero
identified a group at very low risk of relapse on endocrine therapy alone [@ellis2008]. That is a
prognostic statement and not a predictive one. It does not establish that chemotherapy would have
added nothing, because nobody in either cohort was randomised to find out. Z1031 later showed the
index behaving as expected across intrinsic subtypes [@ellis2017].

What the table cannot show is who read the slide. There is no column for central against local
scoring, for antibody clone or for scoring protocol, and the preceding paragraph is the reason that
matters. Nor has any trial here randomised the decision rule itself, which is the gap
{{trial:adaptcycle}} was built to fill.

```caution
A fall in Ki67 is a change in a proliferation marker rather than a change in outcome. The single
trial large enough to separate the two found a measurement that predicted recurrence and a
treatment that prevented none of it [@smith2020]. Reading a suppressed Ki67 as evidence that the
drug is helping that patient runs the two together.
```

## BS-6940 Neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition and window-of-opportunity designs

```story SQ-0070
```

This is the cleanest demonstration in the book that arresting proliferation and eradicating a
tumour are different events.

All five trials agree on the pharmacology. Adding a CDK4/6 inhibitor to an aromatase inhibitor
suppresses proliferation well beyond what endocrine therapy achieves alone.
{{trial:pallet}} and {{trial:neomonarch}} show it in the same direction and at similar magnitude,
over fourteen weeks and over two [@johnston2019pallet; @hurvitz2020neomonarch]. Neither produced a
clinical response advantage over its endocrine comparator, and pathological complete responses were
close to absent in both.

The table looks like five trials of one strategy. It is two questions with different control arms.
PALLET and neoMONARCH compared the combination against endocrine therapy alone and read
proliferation. {{trial:neopal}} and {{trial:coralleen}} compared it against chemotherapy and read
the surgical specimen. A reader who lines up the result column across all five is comparing
answers to different questions.

That first combination is what a cytostatic agent should produce, because blocking the G1
restriction point stops cells dividing [[BC-160]]. It does not kill them, so the tumour bed is
still cellular at surgery. A trial judging such a drug by a pathological complete response will
call an active agent inactive.

NeoPAL makes the point sharpest by disagreeing with itself. Judged on residual cancer burden, the
chemotherapy arm did better. Judged on the preoperative endocrine prognostic index, the combination
arm did better [@cottu2018neopal]. One randomisation of one set of patients produces opposite
rankings depending on which post-treatment measurement is chosen. CORALLEEN read a third measure, a
molecular risk score on the surgical specimen, and found the two arms level [@prat2020coralleen].
Neither trial was designed as a comparative test, and neither carried a noninferiority margin.

The composite index deserves a further warning, because proliferation is one of its own components.
A drug that suppresses proliferation improves that index partly by construction. Reading such an
improvement as evidence of deeper tumour control is circular. The molecular risk score has the
opposite problem. It reclassifies the tumour rather than measuring what the patient gained at
surgery.

There is a second reason to distrust the arrest threshold once a cell-cycle inhibitor is in the
regimen. Complete cell-cycle arrest was calibrated on tumours given endocrine therapy alone, and it
was built to grade endocrine dependence. A drug that stops the cycle whether or not the tumour
depends on oestrogen crosses that line in nearly everyone. Crossing it then says nothing about the
receptor, which is exactly what destroys its power to select.

Suppression also requires continuing exposure. Both neoMONARCH and the within-patient NeoPalAna
design recorded Ki67 recovering once the inhibitor was withdrawn before surgery, and in NeoPalAna a
further cycle given immediately beforehand suppressed that recovery again [@ma2017]. NeoPalAna is
single-arm and each patient is her own control, so its comparison confounds the added drug with
longer time on treatment. The rebound observation survives that objection because it runs the other
way. It makes the discontinuation and adherence questions in [[BS-6880]] questions about efficacy
rather than about administration.

{{trial:neoletrib}} is the ongoing row. It profiles serial biopsies at single-cell resolution
through six months of letrozole and ribociclib. It is single-arm, so it will describe what months
of exposure do to a human tumour and will settle nothing about whether that helps anyone. FELINE
asked the translation question directly, whether early proliferation suppression converts into a
better prognostic index, and it has conference and registry material only. Its reported negative
result cannot be weighed as a published trial result.

What the table cannot show is any survival column. No trial here was powered to test whether a
CDK4/6 inhibitor can replace chemotherapy, and pathological complete response sits so near its
floor in this cell that a null result on it means very little. The window logic, and the reason a
pharmacodynamic effect does not license an outcome claim, is worked out in [[BS-4700]] using
{{trial:poetic}}.

## BS-6950 Immunotherapy in hormone receptor-positive early disease

```story SQ-0110
```

All three rows agree on one finding. Adding a checkpoint inhibitor to anthracycline and
taxane-based preoperative chemotherapy raises the pathological complete response rate in this
subtype.

They are not three equivalent estimates of it. {{trial:keynote756}} and {{trial:checkmate7fl}} are
double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials with a concurrent control arm [@cardoso2025k756;
@loi2025cm7fl]. The {{trial:ispy2-pembro}} row is an adaptively randomised platform arm whose rates
are Bayesian estimates read against a control shared with other arms and accrued over a different
period [@nanda2020]. The drugs differ too. The estimation method differs more, and it is what stops
the three response rates being read off against each other.

Whom they enrolled matters more than what they showed, because both phase 3 trials restricted entry
to high-grade disease. CheckMate 7FL admitted grade 2 tumours only where oestrogen receptor
expression fell between 1% and 10% [@loi2025cm7fl]. These are the tumours that behave least like
typical luminal disease. Transferring either result to a grade 1, strongly receptor-positive tumour
is not supported by either trial.

KEYNOTE-756 is the load-bearing row. It is the largest, it is placebo-controlled, and it carries
event-free survival as a dual primary endpoint rather than as an afterthought. That is the endpoint
the decision will eventually rest on. It was not mature at the analysis that reported the response
result.

The effect concentrates further inside those populations. In CheckMate 7FL the response gain was
larger among PD-L1-positive tumours than across the trial as a whole. That is a group defined by a
marker rather than a randomised comparison. The marker was scored with one
antibody clone against one cut-off. [[BS-6390]] sets out what such an observation can and cannot
carry.

The endpoint problem now arrives at its sharpest point. A gain in pathological complete response
has been demonstrated in the one subtype where that endpoint is least prognostic [@cortazar2014;
@vonminckwitz2012]. The gain may well translate into fewer recurrences. Nothing published so far
shows that it has.

A practical conflict is already visible, because adjuvant abemaciclib and adjuvant ribociclib are
options for the same high-risk population. Neither is given alongside a checkpoint inhibitor. A
patient eligible for both escalations can have only one of them, and no trial compares the two.
There is also a mechanistic reason to doubt that the two would combine. NeoRHEA, a single-arm
profiling study, found that a CDK4/6 inhibitor on an endocrine backbone diminished adaptive
anti-tumour immunity [@papagiannis2025]. That is a reason for doubt rather than a test of the
combination.

What the table cannot show is the column that decides the question. There is no event-free survival
and no overall survival in any of these rows. There is no PD-L1 column, so the subgroup that drives
the enthusiasm is invisible where the trials are enumerated. And nobody has randomised a
preoperative checkpoint inhibitor against adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition, which is the choice the
clinic actually faces. The other mechanisms being tried here, an antibody-drug conjugate and
radiation given as an immune primer, have no result to read at all [[BS-6960]].

```caution
The intuitive reading is that a doubled complete response rate in a poor-prognosis population must
translate into fewer recurrences. In triple-negative disease that reading has been supported by
subsequent survival data. Here the same inference is being drawn in the subtype where the
trial-level link between response and outcome is weakest. Late recurrence also means the follow-up
needed to test it is measured in decades rather than years [[BS-4350]].
```

## BS-6960 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

Four questions are open. Five trials below are working on three of them, and the fourth has nothing
behind it at all.

The first is whether a response gain translates. Event-free survival from KEYNOTE-756 and CheckMate
7FL is the readout that will settle whether the argument in [[BS-6950]] was pessimism or realism. A
positive result would make pathological complete response useful in this subtype for the first
time. A null result would confirm that the endpoint measured the wrong thing. No trial in the table
below asks this question, which is worth noticing.

The second is whether endocrine or cell-cycle response can take the place of chemotherapy in
intermediate-risk disease. {{trial:adaptcycle}} allocates on a genomic score together with the
post-endocrine Ki67 described in [[BS-6930]], then randomises ribociclib with endocrine therapy
against chemotherapy, with invasive disease-free survival as the endpoint. Its randomisation is
adjuvant, which is why it is not in the table here. That design makes a null result ambiguous,
because the allocation rule and the drug would fail together. {{trial:ribolaris}} asks a narrower
version. It gives everyone the same preoperative ribociclib and letrozole, routes on a molecular
risk score read at surgery, and carries five-year distant metastasis-free survival in the low-risk
cohort as its endpoint [@cottu2026]. That is the right endpoint and a long wait. It is also
single-arm through the preoperative phase, so the low-risk cohort is compared against an external
benchmark rather than against a randomised control.

The third is what a CDK4/6 inhibitor does to a human tumour over months rather than weeks.
{{trial:neoletrib}} samples serially through six months of exposure with single-cell sequencing
[@fjermeros2024neoletrib]. Its value will be mechanistic rather than clinical.

Two further rows ask whether a different mechanism can raise response where endocrine escalation
has not. {{trial:talent}} moves an antibody-drug conjugate before surgery in untreated hormone
receptor-positive, HER2-low disease, given alone and with an aromatase inhibitor. It uses HER2
expression below the amplification threshold as an address rather than as a driver.
{{trial:p-rad}} randomises preoperative radiation dose as an immune primer before checkpoint
blockade and chemotherapy, holding its hormone receptor-positive and triple-negative cohorts
separate. Neither has a result. {{trial:tbcrc-037}} is the histology-specific row, a randomised
short preoperative exposure in lobular disease read out as target engagement. It will say whether
each drug reaches its target in that histology. It cannot rank the drugs on efficacy.

The fourth question has no trial behind it. Nobody is testing whether preoperative treatment of any
kind improves outcome over upfront surgery in this subtype. Nobody has prospectively validated a
preoperative endocrine prognostic index of zero as a gate for omitting adjuvant chemotherapy. Both
would change practice. Neither is being studied.

The table below prints what each open trial asks rather than what it found, because three of the
five have no primary publication at all. Treating a conference abstract as a trial result is the
specific error this table is built to prevent.

```interplay target=BC-350
Part VI argues that a tumour is a population rather than a value, and this chapter is where that
argument has a bedside consequence for the commonest subtype. Every response measure used here
averages over that population. A complete response asks whether the whole population was
eradicated, which in slowly proliferating disease is almost never true. Residual cancer burden
keeps the variance and is therefore the more informative instrument [@symmans2017]. A two-week Ki67
measures the fraction of the population still cycling under oestrogen deprivation, which is a
statement about composition rather than about size [@smith2020]. Read this way, the low complete
response rate in hormone receptor-positive disease is not a failure of the drugs. It is what
averaging over a heterogeneous, slowly dividing population looks like when the measurement is
forced into two categories.
```
