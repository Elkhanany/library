## BS-5170 Brain metastases, screening, systemic activity, and local therapy

The central nervous system is the one compartment this book gives a chapter of its own. [[BC-930]]
carries the local therapy, the systemic therapy, the delivery argument underneath both, and the
endpoints these trials report. This section says only why the compartment is separated out, and
what a reader of an organ-specific chapter has to carry away.

Three things make it behave unlike bone, liver or lung.

Most of the randomised evidence came from radiation oncology, in trials of brain metastases from
solid tumours generally. A breast-specific estimate out of those trials is a subgroup at best.

The registration trials of the modern systemic agents excluded untreated or progressing brain
metastases by protocol. The claim that a large molecule cannot reach the compartment was therefore
assumed rather than tested. Nobody enrolled the patients who would have tested it.

The endpoints are different objects. An intracranial response rate, a central nervous system
progression-free rate at a fixed timepoint and a response by neuro-oncology criteria are routinely
lined up in one sentence. They are not comparable. What separates them is mostly who was
admitted to each study [[BS-7850]].

Two practical points belong here rather than there. Routine imaging of the asymptomatic brain is
not supported by randomised evidence in any subtype. That should be said rather than implied.
And the lesion in the brain may not carry the target the primary carried, because acquired ERBB2
amplification and intrinsic subtype switching are both described in resected brain metastases
[@priedigkeit2017].

Why the brain is colonised at all belongs to [[BC-320]]. The subtype distribution is in
[@kennecke2010] and the colonisation biology in [@bos2009; @valiente2014].

## BS-5180 Leptomeningeal disease

Leptomeningeal disease is a second compartment inside the first. It is not the same problem as
parenchymal disease. The diagnosis, the drugs and the evidence behind them are in [[BS-7860]].

Two points belong in an organ-specific chapter. The first is that cytology is highly specific and
insensitive. A negative result in a patient with characteristic symptoms and characteristic
imaging does not exclude the diagnosis, and should not be allowed to.

The second is the compartment point. It is the one to carry away. Cerebrospinal fluid is
separated from blood, which means that plasma circulating tumour DNA does not report what is in
it. A patient with undetectable plasma can have detectable disease in the cerebrospinal fluid, so
the two assays answer different questions rather than the same question with different sensitivity
[[BC-390]]. Cerebrospinal fluid circulating tumour DNA improves on cytology where it is available.
The technical account is in [[BS-3520]] [@fitzpatrick2022].

For most patients here the goal is neurological function and symptom control rather than length of
life. Saying so at the outset makes every later decision easier [[BC-680]].

## BS-5190 Bone metastases and skeletal-related events

Bone is the commonest first site of distant relapse in hormone receptor-positive disease
[@kennecke2010]. The colonisation programme behind that is described in [[BS-2750]] and in
[@kang2003].

The endpoint these trials share deserves inspection before any of their results are quoted. A
skeletal-related event is a composite of pathological fracture, radiation to bone, surgery to bone
and spinal cord compression. Two of those four are interventions rather than events. The composite
therefore measures clinical behaviour as well as disease behaviour. That does not invalidate the
endpoint. It does mean the absolute rates travel badly between health systems with different
referral thresholds.

```evidence setting=metastatic modality=bone sort=year cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,result caption=Bone-modifying_agents_in_established_bone_metastasis._One_trial_compares_the_two_drugs_and_two_compare_schedules_of_the_same_drug._The_adjuvant_bone_trials_ask_a_different_question_and_sit_elsewhere.
```

Every trial in that table randomised one active bone-modifying schedule against another. That
shared design is the finding about the class. Where the two agents met head to head, neither
survival nor disease progression separated them. The two interval trials varied the spacing of one
drug rather than testing it against nothing. Nothing here says that a bone-modifying agent
alters the course of the disease outside the skeleton. These are supportive drugs with a
supportive-care justification, however oncological they look on a prescription.

Where the three diverge is in what they measured and in whom.
{{trial:denosumab-versus-zoledronic-acid-in-bm}} measured time to a first skeletal event and
tested it for superiority [@stopeck2010]. The two interval trials measured the proportion of
patients reaching at least one event inside a fixed window. Each tested that proportion against a
non-inferiority margin its investigators chose in advance. A delay in a first event and a
proportion with any event inside a window are different objects. The second carries a judgement
about what size of difference would matter. Neither design was capable of producing the other's
answer.

The agent question rests on the head-to-head, the only randomised comparison of the two drugs
against each other. It makes the choice between them a choice between toxicity profiles rather
than between disease outcomes. Renal adverse events and acute-phase reactions were more frequent
with zoledronic acid. Hypocalcaemia was more frequent with denosumab, which is why calcium is
checked after starting it.

The interval question was asked twice, at different moments in a patient's course.
{{trial:calgb-70604}} randomised the interval from the start of treatment and for the whole two
years, in three cancers enrolled together [@himelstein2017]. {{trial:optimize-2}} randomised it in
breast cancer alone, and only after nine or more monthly doses had already been given
[@hortobagyi2017]. Choosing a twelve weekly interval at the outset and stepping down to it after a
year are different decisions. CALGB 70604 addresses the first and OPTIMIZE-2 the second. The habit
of starting at the longer interval therefore rests on CALGB 70604. The schedules did not separate
within the breast cancer stratum of that trial, which is reassuring rather than powered.

CALGB 70604 found bone turnover less completely suppressed on the longer interval
[@himelstein2017]. That is an assay result. The same trial counted skeletal events. They did
not follow the marker. A pharmacodynamic marker that fails to track the clinical endpoint in
its own trial is not a reason to shorten the interval.

Three things the table cannot show. None of these trials was built around a comparison with no
bone-modifying agent at all, so none of them establishes that the class beats nothing in a patient
who already has bone metastases. Every interval result is a zoledronic acid result. No randomised
trial has tested a longer denosumab interval, so the twelve weekly habit does not transfer across
the class. And the composite gives a radiotherapy referral the same weight as a fracture.

The adjuvant literature makes a different claim and is regularly quoted as though it made the same
one. Adjuvant bisphosphonates reduce bone recurrence and breast cancer mortality in postmenopausal
women [@ebctcg2015]. That is prevention of bone metastasis in patients who do not have it. It says
nothing about the treatment of established bone disease [[BC-640]]. The two bodies of evidence
should not be merged.

Bone is also the hardest compartment in which to call a response. Sclerotic change in a healing
lytic lesion reads as a new finding on computed tomography. Tracer uptake on scintigraphy reflects
osteoblastic activity rather than tumour cell number [[BS-4770]].

```caution
None of these trials randomised when to stop. Entry required established bone metastasis. The
randomised comparisons ran for one or two years. The evidence therefore covers the beginning of
treatment and says nothing about a patient five years into a bone-modifying agent.

The interval result has the same boundary. Non-inferiority measured over two years is not a
statement about year six. The osteonecrosis risk that argues for stopping accumulates with
exposure rather than resetting each year.
```

```practice
Arrange dental assessment before starting a bone-modifying agent. Give calcium and vitamin D
alongside it.

Monitor calcium after starting denosumab. Hypocalcaemia is the toxicity that differs between the
two agents [@stopeck2010].

A twelve weekly schedule is a reasonable default for zoledronic acid. It reduces visits without a
measured cost in skeletal events, whether it is chosen from the start of treatment [@himelstein2017]
or stepped down to after a year of monthly dosing [@hortobagyi2017]. Do not carry the schedule
across to denosumab, which has never been randomised at a longer interval.

Treat a painful bone deposit with radiation. Refer an impending fracture in a weight-bearing bone
for stabilisation before it breaks rather than after.

Record why the agent is still being given at each annual review. No trial covers the decision to
continue indefinitely, so it should at least be a decision.
```

## BS-5200 Liver and lung disease

Liver disease is what most often converts a chronic illness into an acute one. The variable that
matters is function rather than volume. A patient with extensive parenchymal replacement and
normal synthetic function has a different set of options from one with similar imaging and a
rising bilirubin. Drug clearance changes with the second and not with the first.

Visceral crisis is defined clinically rather than radiologically, as rapidly progressive visceral
involvement with organ dysfunction that requires a rapid response. It is the one situation in
hormone receptor-positive disease where chemotherapy has customarily displaced endocrine and
targeted therapy.

That custom has been tested. The question those trials were built to answer is argued in
[[BS-4890]]. The trials themselves are tabulated in [[BC-880]], where the first-line record
belongs.

{{trial:right-choice}} is the one that reaches this section. Its result needs stating carefully.
It enrolled pre- and perimenopausal women with clinically aggressive disease, and progression-free
survival favoured ribociclib with endocrine therapy over combination chemotherapy. Of its 222
patients, 106 were recorded as being in visceral crisis by their own investigator rather than by an
adjudicated protocol definition [@lu2024]. That is a measurement problem before it is a
biological one, because investigator judgement is exactly the thing a clinic is trying to
calibrate. What the result establishes is that the reflex to chemotherapy is wrong more often than
it was assumed to be. It does not establish that it is never right. It does not mark the
threshold at which it becomes right.

The practical form of that is a question about time rather than about drug class. Median time to
response in RIGHT Choice was 3.2 months with combination chemotherapy and 4.9 months with
ribociclib and endocrine therapy. Chemotherapy is the faster of the two. Neither is fast. Where
organ function is failing over days, the ordering of those two medians is the whole decision. Where
it is declining over months it is not.

Two limits sit on that trial. Neither is a criticism of its conduct. It is a randomised phase 2
with an open-label investigator's choice control. Overall survival has not been reported, so the
trade has been measured on progression-free survival alone. And the patient in true visceral
crisis with failing organs is excluded from trials of this kind by performance status and organ
function criteria. That decision will not acquire a randomised answer, which is the reason for
naming it as a judgement rather than dressing it as evidence.

Lung parenchymal metastases are usually asymptomatic until they are extensive, with a tempo slower
than liver disease of comparable burden. The decision is therefore rarely forced by the lung
itself.

Lymphangitic carcinomatosis is a separate problem with a separate tempo. It is the one to
recognise early. Breathlessness out of proportion to the parenchymal findings, with septal
thickening on imaging, is attributed first to infection and second to treatment-related
pneumonitis. In a patient receiving a deruxtecan-based conjugate that second possibility is
urgent, because drug-related interstitial lung disease in this class carries real mortality
[@powell2022]. The distinction changes what happens in the next 24 hours and cannot be made on a
chest radiograph.

Why particular organs are colonised is developed in [[BC-320]], with the lung programme described
in [@minn2005] and the vesicle-based account of preferential seeding in [@hoshino2015].

## BS-5210 Malignant effusions and chest wall disease

A malignant pleural effusion has three management options. The choice between them turns on
prognosis and on preference rather than on efficacy in relieving the symptom.

```evidence setting=metastatic modality=supportive sort=year cols=acronym,n,population,arms,endpoint,result caption=The_two_randomised_comparisons_of_an_indwelling_pleural_catheter_against_talc_pleurodesis._They_chose_different_primary_endpoints,_which_is_why_they_read_as_disagreeing_and_do_not.
```

Both trials randomised the same two strategies against each other. They agree on more than they
are usually credited with. Breathlessness improved on both arms in both trials. Both found
that a catheter reduces the need for a further pleural procedure. Both found more adverse events
with the catheter. None of that is in dispute.

They read as disagreeing because they set different primary endpoints. {{trial:time2}} set a daily
breathlessness score over the weeks immediately after the procedure [@davies2012]. {{trial:ample}}
set total days in hospital out to death or twelve months [@thomas2017]. A short-horizon symptom
score and a year-long count of hospital days cannot contradict each other. The divergence is the
design, not the device.

That also decides which trial answers which question. TIME2 is the one that addresses relief of
breathlessness. It found no difference between the strategies on its own scale. That is a null
result rather than a demonstration of equivalence, because no equivalence margin was set in
advance.
AMPLE is the one the choice actually rests on. It measured time in hospital over a horizon long
enough for the difference to accumulate. Time in hospital is what a patient with months to live is
being asked to trade.

TIME2's late breathlessness difference in favour of the catheter is the single result that looks
like a device effect. It should be read with care. It was a secondary comparison made months
after randomisation, in the patients still alive to complete a score rather than in the population
that was randomised.

This is therefore a preference-sensitive decision rather than an efficacy one. It should be
offered as such. A patient with months to live and a strong wish for time out of hospital chooses
differently from one expecting a longer course.

Three things the table cannot supply. Neither trial enrolled breast cancer alone, so no
breast-specific estimate exists for either endpoint. Neither randomised a catheter against
repeated therapeutic aspiration, which is what a frail patient often actually receives. And
neither reports what managing a drain at home costs the person who does the draining, which is the
burden the catheter moves out of the hospital and onto the household.

Pericardial effusion is dangerous for physiological reasons rather than volumetric ones. Tamponade
depends on the rate of accumulation and on pericardial compliance. A small rapidly accumulating
effusion is more dangerous than a large slow one. Echocardiography answers the question that a
computed tomography report describing a moderate effusion does not.

Malignant ascites is managed by paracentesis, and by an indwelling catheter where drainage is
repeated, on the same logic as the pleural space. That logic is an analogy rather than a result.
Neither space has been randomised. The table above holds nothing outside the pleura.

Chest wall disease produces a symptom burden disproportionate to its extent and disproportionate
to the attention it receives. Ulceration, exudate, odour, bleeding and pain occur together and
each needs specific management. Radiation and systemic therapy address the tumour. The wound needs
dressing selection, odour control and bleeding management. That is a specialist skill.
Referring it to tissue viability or wound care services is not an admission that oncology has
failed. Treating it as one leaves a manageable problem unmanaged.

## BS-5220 Obstructive complications and oncologic emergencies

Metastatic spinal cord compression is the emergency in this disease where hours change the
outcome. The variable predicting function afterwards is function beforehand.

```evidence setting=metastatic topic=cord-compression sort=year cols=acronym,n,population,arms,endpoint,result,os caption=The_two_randomised_trials_of_what_to_do_about_a_compressed_cord._Both_enrolled_metastatic_cancer_of_any_primary_site,_and_breast_cancer_was_a_minority_of_each.
```

The two trials there are not two answers to one question. One asked whether to add decompressive
surgery to radiotherapy. The other asked how many fractions of radiotherapy to give. They share a
population, metastatic cancer of any primary site. They share an endpoint expressed as walking.
They constrain each other in nothing else. Reading them as agreeing or disagreeing is a category
error.

{{trial:patchell-cord-compression-trial}} is why a surgical opinion is sought before radiotherapy
alone is accepted [@patchell2005]. It is load-bearing because it randomised the addition against
the thing every such patient was otherwise going to get. Two limits belong with it. Both are about how far the result
travels. The trial enrolled metastatic cancer generally rather than
breast cancer. It also stopped early at a prespecified interim analysis, which tends to inflate
the estimated effect.

{{trial:scorad}} asked the narrower question. The way it failed is the instructive part. A
single fraction missed its non-inferiority margin for walking at week eight, while meeting that
margin at weeks one, four and twelve [@hoskin2019]. A result that holds before a timepoint and
again after it is more likely to be a property of the timepoint than of the dose. The explanation
is attrition. A large part of the randomised population had died before the week eight assessment,
so the primary endpoint was measured in the survivors. Its survival column points the same way.
The two schedules did not separate on survival, which is what makes the choice of schedule a
logistical decision rather than an oncological one.

Three things neither trial can tell a breast oncologist. Neither enrolled breast cancer alone, so
the estimate a reader of this chapter wants does not exist. The patient with hormone
receptor-positive or HER2-positive disease and years of expected survival is under-represented in
both. Nobody randomised the modern operation, because separation surgery followed by stereotactic
radiotherapy has not been compared with either arm of either trial. And nobody randomised
corticosteroid dose or the interval from symptom to scan, which are the two variables a physician
actually controls in the first hour.

Ambulatory status at presentation carries the same warning. It is the strongest predictor of
ambulatory status afterwards. It is a baseline variable rather than a randomised one. No
treatment choice can improve it. Only a shorter delay can preserve it.

Superior vena cava obstruction in breast cancer is usually subacute rather than immediately
life-threatening. Endovascular stenting relieves it faster than radiation does. The two are
commonly combined. The immediate priorities are airway and cerebral oedema rather than the caval
anatomy itself.

Hypercalcaemia of malignancy is treated with rehydration and a bone-modifying agent. Its
appearance is also information, because it frequently marks a change in disease behaviour. It
should prompt reassessment rather than only correction.

Biliary and ureteric obstruction present the same structural question. The decision to stent is a
decision about what follows the stent. A stent placed without a subsequent systemic plan buys a
laboratory value rather than time.

```practice
New back pain with any neurological sign in a patient with known metastatic disease is imaged the
same day, with magnetic resonance of the whole spine rather than the symptomatic level alone.

Start corticosteroids while arranging the imaging, not after it.

Obtain a surgical opinion for cord compression before settling on radiotherapy alone, particularly
where the patient is still walking or the spine is unstable [@patchell2005].

Choose the radiotherapy schedule from the expected survival. A single fraction is reasonable where
that is short. The eight-week evidence for it is weakest exactly there, because that is where
attrition emptied the assessment [@hoskin2019].

Record ambulatory status at presentation. It is the strongest predictor of ambulatory status
afterwards. It is also the number most often missing from the notes.
```

## BS-5230 Local intervention in the metastatic setting

Local treatment in metastatic disease is given for three different reasons. They should never be
argued as one.

Symptom control. A painful bone deposit, a bleeding chest wall, an obstructing lesion. The
justification is the symptom. No survival claim is required or implied.

Structural protection. An impending fracture, a threatened cord, a compromised airway. The
justification is the event being prevented [[BS-5220]].

Disease control intent. The oligometastatic and oligoprogressive arguments, where local treatment
is given in the hope of changing the course of the disease.

Only the third makes a claim about the course of the disease. That is the claim a randomised
comparison against systemic therapy alone can settle.

```evidence setting=metastatic modality=surgery,radiation line=any sort=year cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,result,os caption=The_randomised_tests_of_local_treatment_given_with_disease-control_intent._One_treated_the_primary_tumour_and_one_treated_the_metastases._Only_the_first_was_conducted_in_breast_cancer.
```

Both trials asked the same question in form. Does local treatment given for disease control change
overall survival, against standard treatment alone? They differ in what was treated, in who was
enrolled, and above all in the standard of proof each set itself. That last difference is the one
that matters. It is a matter of design rather than a disagreement about whether ablating a deposit
helps anybody.

{{trial:ea2108}} is the clearer of the two. It contradicts the stronger intuition. Women
presenting with metastatic disease and an intact primary received systemic therapy for four to
eight months. Those without progression were then randomised to locoregional treatment of the
primary or to continuing systemic therapy alone [@khan2022]. Removing the primary improved local
control while improving neither survival nor quality of life. Local control and survival are
separate outcomes. Improving the first delivered neither of the others.

That design is what makes it load-bearing. It is the only one of the two conducted in breast
cancer. It randomised the comparison a clinic is actually tempted by. And the run-in placed the
intuition in its strongest possible position. Randomising only the patients whose disease had not
progressed selects for systemic control, which is the setting in which clearing a residual primary
should pay if it ever does. It did not pay there either.

{{trial:sabr-comet}} points the other way and is much the weaker design. It was a randomised phase
2 screening study with its significance threshold set at 0.20, against the conventional standard a
phase 3 trial holds itself to [@palma2020]. A threshold of that kind is built for deciding what to
test next rather than for deciding what to do on Monday. Breast cancer was one of four common
primaries in it and a minority of the enrolment, so the breast-specific evidence inside it is
thinner than the headline suggests.

There is a measurement problem underneath it as well. Entry required a countable number of
lesions, all of them treatable by ablation. That count is a property of the scanner and of the
reporting convention as much as of the disease. Randomisation balances indolent biology between
the arms. It does nothing to make the result transferable to a patient whose deposits were merely
counted differently on a more sensitive study.

Three comparisons nobody has randomised follow from that table. Ablation of metastases has never
been tested in breast cancer alone. The patients who progressed during that run-in were never
randomised, so the situation in which a primary is left behind in advancing disease is untested.
And nobody has randomised local treatment of an oligoprogressive site at all.

Oligoprogression is the one situation where a disease-control rationale rests on a coherent
biological argument. A single progressing deposit while everything else responds suggests a
resistant subclone in one place, which is the divergence picture in [[BS-2570]]. Treating that
site and continuing the systemic regimen follows from the inference. It has still not been shown
to extend survival [[BS-4780]].

```interplay target=BC-350
Organ-specific management is where heterogeneity stops being a property of a tumour and becomes a
property of a patient. Different deposits sit in different microenvironments. They are exposed to
different drug concentrations and have taken different evolutionary routes [[BS-2570]]. A bone
deposit is held in a signalling programme the liver does not supply [[BS-2750]]. A brain metastasis
can carry an acquired ERBB2 amplification that the primary did not [@priedigkeit2017]. The
cerebrospinal fluid compartment is invisible to the assay used to monitor everything else
[@fitzpatrick2022]. Mixed response is therefore an expected finding rather than an anomaly. A
treatment decision made on the behaviour of the largest lesion is a decision about that lesion.
This chapter is the clinical form of the argument that a patient with metastatic breast cancer has
several related diseases in several places.
```
