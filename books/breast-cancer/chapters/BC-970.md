## BS-8010 What a positive assay names, and in which compartment

This chapter takes the assay as given. [[BC-390]] establishes what circulating
tumour DNA detects, how well, and how far ahead of clinical relapse. [[BC-330]]
establishes what the detected cells are doing in the years before they declare
themselves. The question here is the one a clinic is left with. A patient has finished
curative-intent treatment and a scan shows nothing. A test says disease is
present.

What that result names is a patient rather than a lesion. There is nothing to
measure, nothing to resect and nothing to irradiate. Every endpoint oncology
normally reaches for is unavailable, and the only outcome that can be observed
is whether relapse arrives.

```evidence setting=mrd,surveillance weight=any
```

Two compartments report the state, and they are not the same test.

**Plasma.** Circulating tumour DNA is the route almost every trial uses. It is
easily sampled and can be repeated. Its prognostic strength is established across
subtypes. [[BC-390]] carries that record.

**Bone marrow.** Disseminated tumour cells are the older observation and the
more direct one. A marrow aspirate finds the cells themselves rather than
fragments of their DNA. {{trial:clever}} is the trial that treated on that
basis, and it is the only randomised intervention in this chapter that acted on
a marrow result.

No trial in this registry randomises one compartment against the other. The two
are usually written about as if they were one assay with two sampling routes,
and that is an assumption rather than a finding. A drug that clears plasma has
not been shown to clear marrow, and the reverse has not been shown either.

```interplay target=BC-330
Residual disease is the clinical face of dormancy. [[BC-330]] argues that the
dormant state is actively maintained, that escape has identifiable triggers, and
that early and late recurrence are different biological problems. This chapter
inherits that argument and one uncomfortable consequence of it. A test that
detects shedding detects cells that have already left dormancy. The population
most worth intercepting may be the one the test cannot see.
```

## BS-8020 Why the surveillance question is not the advanced-disease question

[[BC-390]] states the distinction and this section develops the part that
matters at the prescribing pad. In advanced disease a molecular signal triggers
a switch to a drug with a known effect on measurable disease. After curative
treatment there is no such drug, because no agent has been shown to eradicate
disease detected this way.

So every trial here has had to choose a class on an argument rather than on a
precedent. Three arguments have been made, and each is worth naming, because
each predicts a different kind of failure.

**Checkpoint blockade, on the argument that residual disease is small and
immunologically visible.** {{trial:ctrak-tn}} tested it in triple-negative
disease. [[BC-390]] carries what came back and what three separate things went
wrong.

**Dormancy-directed treatment, on the argument that the target is the
quiescent cell rather than the dividing one.** {{trial:clever}} combined
autophagy inhibition and mTOR inhibition against marrow disease. Its endpoints
were feasibility and safety rather than recurrence, which is the honest design
for a first test and also the reason it settles nothing about outcome.

**Endocrine escalation, on the argument that the disease is still endocrine
dependent and the first course was simply not enough.** {{trial:leader}} is
testing it in hormone receptor-positive disease.

The three arguments are not compatible with each other. Checkpoint blockade
assumes the cells are proliferating enough to be seen. Dormancy-directed
treatment assumes they are not proliferating at all. A field that runs all three
at once is a field that does not yet know what it is treating, and saying so is
more useful than picking a favourite.

```caution
A trial in this setting can fail for a reason that has nothing to do with its
drug. If surveillance begins after adjuvant treatment has finished, the patients
it finds are the ones in whom relapse was already underway. If sampling is
three-monthly in a subtype whose warning is measured in weeks, the same thing
happens. Both faults produce a negative trial of an agent that was never given a
population it could help. [[BC-390]] shows what that looks like in practice.
```

## BS-8030 What the subtype changes about what surveillance could buy

Lead time is not a property of the assay alone. It is a property of how fast the
disease moves between the first detectable shedding and the first visible
deposit, and that differs by subtype. {{trial:alienor}} measured it in one
cohort and found the gap longest in hormone receptor-positive HER2-negative
disease, intermediate in HER2-positive disease, and shortest in triple-negative
disease. [[BC-390]] carries the figures.

Reading that against the three subtype parts gives three different problems.

**Hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease has the most room and the
least urgency.** The recurrence hazard runs for two decades, which [[BC-330]]
establishes and [[BC-940]] takes as the premise of its part. A long lead time
and a long hazard make this the subtype where surveillance has the most time to
act in. It is also the subtype where an effective intervention already exists in
the form of more endocrine therapy, and where [[BC-790]] shows how little the
extra years buy.

**Triple-negative disease has the most to gain and the least warning.** The
recurrence risk is concentrated in the first few years, which [[BC-960]] makes
the organising fact of its part. That is precisely the disease a surveillance
programme would most want to intercept. It is also the disease in which the
interval between detection and relapse is shortest, so a quarterly sampling
schedule may miss the window entirely.

**HER2-positive disease sits between them and is the least studied.** No trial
in this registry surveils it specifically. The subtype has more active agents
than either of the others, which makes the absence of a trial harder to explain
than the absence of a result.

The summary is uncomfortable. The subtype with the most usable lead time has the
least to gain. The subtype with the most to gain has the least usable lead
time.

## BS-8040 Reading an interventional trial in this setting before it reports

{{trial:dare-ctdna}}, {{trial:leader}} and {{trial:survive}} have not reported.
Neither have TRAK-ER and PERSEVERE, which are named in [[BC-390]] and are not in
this registry. A reader will meet the first of these results before this chapter
is next revised, so the useful thing to record now is what to ask of one.

**What was randomised.** A trial that randomises treatment among test-positive
patients tests the treatment. A trial that randomises the test itself tests the
surveillance programme. These answer different questions and only the second one
tells a clinic whether to send the assay.

**When surveillance started, and how often it sampled.** Both are design
parameters rather than incidental detail, and [[BS-8020]] gives the reason. A
schedule has to be read against the lead time of the subtype enrolled.

**What the control group received.** Observation is not the only honest
comparator. In hormone receptor-positive disease the alternative to escalation
is continued endocrine therapy, and a trial that compares escalation against
nothing will overstate what escalation adds.

**Which endpoint carries the claim.** Clearance of the assay is the endpoint
most likely to be reported first and the one that establishes least. It shows
that the drug reaches the cells it can reach. It does not show that the patient
lives longer, and {{trial:swog-s0500}} is the demonstration in metastatic disease that
a strongly prognostic blood marker can support no useful action at all.

**Whether the population was defined before or after the fact.** A cohort
assembled from patients who turned out to be positive is not a programme. A
programme enrols everyone and follows them. The first design reports on the
test. Only the second reports on the strategy.

## BS-8050 What to do before the answer arrives

```practice
Molecular residual disease testing is not recommended outside a trial, and
[[BC-390]] carries the guideline position that says so. That recommendation is
not a hedge. A positive result outside a trial creates a patient who knows that relapse is
coming, has nothing to treat, and has been offered no option that changes the
outcome.

Three things follow for a clinic.

Ask what will be done with the result before the sample is sent. If the answer
is closer imaging or earlier scans, the honest statement is that neither has
been shown to help, and that [[BC-390]] explains why lead time alone is not
benefit.

Offer the trial where one is open. The designs listed in [[BS-8040]] are the
only route to an answer, and a patient enrolled is worth more than a patient
tested.

Treat a result already in hand as prognostic rather than predictive. It changes
what the clinician expects. It does not yet change what the clinician gives, and
the distinction is the whole content of this chapter.
```
