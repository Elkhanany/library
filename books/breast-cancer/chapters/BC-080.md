## BS-0620 Individual and population risk models and their calibration

A risk model answers a question, and different models answer different questions. Most confusion in
this area comes from asking a model something it was not built to estimate.

Two properties of a model have to be judged separately, and they can move in opposite directions.

Calibration asks whether the predicted number of cancers matches the observed number. It is what
makes a threshold such as a 1.66% five-year risk mean anything, because eligibility rules are built
on absolute numbers.

Discrimination asks whether the model ranks individuals correctly. It is measured by a concordance
statistic, and for breast cancer risk models it is modest. In a prospective family cohort of 15,732
women followed for a median of 11.1 years, during which 619 developed breast cancer, concordance
statistics ranged from 0.60 to 0.71 across four widely used models [@terry2019].

Those numbers deserve a plain reading. A model that is well calibrated at the population level can
still order individuals only a little better than chance. That is not a reason to abandon models.
They succeed at allocating an intervention across a population. They fail at telling one woman what
will happen to her.

Population risk and individual risk also diverge in a way that changes policy. Most breast cancers
occur in women who were never in a high-risk category, because that category is small. A programme
that acts only on the top few percent of predicted risk will therefore prevent a minority of the
disease, however well it is targeted.

## BS-0630 Gail, Tyrer-Cuzick, BOADICEA and CanRisk compared

Each of these models was built for a specific population and a specific decision. Reading the design
intent explains most of the disagreement between them.

The Gail model estimates absolute invasive breast cancer risk from age at menarche, age at first
live birth, number of previous breast biopsies and number of affected first-degree relatives
[@gail1989]. It was derived from a screening programme cohort, and the original paper says the
projections are most reliable for women who are examined about annually. It contains almost no
family structure, so it cannot represent a strong pedigree. Its clinical role is eligibility
screening for prevention, and that is the role it was built for.

It was also derived in white women, and that limitation was addressed rather than ignored. A model
rebuilt on data from 1,607 African American women with invasive breast cancer and 1,647 controls
gave higher risk estimates than the original tool [@gail2007]. Applied to screening data from a
prevention trial, 30.3% of African American women met a five-year risk of at least 1.66% by the new
model against 14.5% by the original. An eligibility threshold is only as good as the model feeding
it.

Tyrer-Cuzick was built to combine a detailed pedigree with personal risk factors. It uses Bayesian
inference across the family history to estimate the probability of carrying a predisposing variant,
then refines the estimate with hormonal and reproductive factors [@tyrer2004]. It is the appropriate
choice when family history is substantial and genetic testing has not been done.

BOADICEA was built as a genetic model and has since absorbed everything else. The current version
incorporates truncating variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, CHEK2 and ATM, a 313-variant polygenic
score, a residual polygenic component, lifestyle and reproductive factors, and mammographic density
[@lee2019]. Across the UK population it predicts lifetime risks from 2.8% at the first percentile to
30.6% at the ninety-ninth. It classifies 14.7% of women as moderate risk by the 17% to 30% lifetime
band, and 1.1% as high risk at 30% or above.

CanRisk is the delivery mechanism for that model, not a separate model. It is the web interface
carrying BOADICEA, with a pedigree builder, and it was the first freely accessible cancer risk
prediction tool to carry a CE marking [@carver2021]. The distinction matters when reading a paper,
because a study of CanRisk is a study of BOADICEA plus an interface.

Independent validation ranks them consistently. In the prospective family cohort, the ratio of
expected to observed cancers was 1.05 for BOADICEA and 1.03 for Tyrer-Cuzick [@terry2019]. Both were
well calibrated. The ratio was 0.79 for the Gail-derived tool and 0.59 for BRCAPRO, both of which
underpredicted. Concordance statistics were 0.70 for BOADICEA and 0.71 for Tyrer-Cuzick, against
0.60 for the Gail-derived tool. Models that use multigenerational family history performed better,
even in women at average risk.

```practice
Pick the model by the question. For eligibility for pharmacologic prevention in a woman without
notable family history, a Gail-type five-year absolute risk is the established input and the one
prevention trials used [@fisher1998].

For a woman with a meaningful pedigree, use Tyrer-Cuzick or CanRisk. A Gail estimate in that setting
will understate risk because the model has nowhere to put the family history [@terry2019].

Check whether the model you are using was validated in a population like your patient. The Gail-type
tool required rebuilding for African American women, and the rebuilt version doubled the proportion
meeting the prevention threshold [@gail2007].

Record which model produced the number. A risk figure without its model is uninterpretable, and it
will be reused by the next clinician who reads the note.
```

## BS-0640 Benign breast disease and risk lesions as risk modifiers

Benign breast disease is common, and its risk implications are graded rather than binary.

A cohort of 9,087 women biopsied for benign disease was followed for a median of 15 years
[@hartmann2005]. Relative risk of subsequent breast cancer against a population registry rose across
three histologic groups: 1.27 for nonproliferative lesions (95% CI 1.15 to 1.41), 1.88 for
proliferative lesions without atypia (95% CI 1.66 to 2.12), and 4.24 for atypical hyperplasia (95%
CI 3.26 to 5.41). Nonproliferative
findings accounted for 67% of that cohort. The earlier Nashville series gave the same ordering, with
a relative risk of 5.3 for atypia against nonproliferative findings (95% CI 3.1 to 8.8)
[@dupont1985].

Atypia is where the risk becomes management-relevant. Among 331 women with atypia followed for a
mean of 13.7 years, 66 developed breast cancer, and cumulative incidence approached 35% at 30 years
[@degnim2007]. Multifocal atypia with calcifications carried a relative risk of 10.35 (95% CI 6.13
to 16.4), and women under 45 at diagnosis carried a relative risk of 6.76 (95% CI 3.24 to 12.4).
Risk was similar for atypical ductal and atypical lobular hyperplasia. The management options that
follow from this risk level are set out in the accompanying review [@hartmann2015].

```caution
Family history did not add significant further risk in women who already had atypia [@degnim2007].
This is counterintuitive and it is clinically consequential, because most risk models will add the
two. A woman with atypia is already in a high-risk group, and the pedigree does not move her much
further within it.
```

The risk from atypia is bilateral, not confined to the biopsied breast. That is the observation that
makes atypia a marker of a susceptible field rather than an obligate precursor, and [[BS-0360]]
develops the argument. It also means that unilateral surgery is a poor response to it.

## BS-0650 Breast density and other risk modifiers

Density is discussed as a property of the breast and measured as a property of an image. [[BS-0330]]
sets out what the measurement is. This section is about what it does to a risk estimate.

The association is one of the strongest in the field. A meta-analysis of 42 studies aggregated more
than 14,000 cases and 226,000 non-cases [@mccormack2006]. Relative risks of incident breast cancer
in the general population rose across four percent density categories, each compared against density
below 5%: 1.79 for 5% to 24%, 2.11 for 25% to 49%, 2.92 for 50% to 74%, and 4.64 for 75% or more. The association was much
stronger for percent density than for categorical parenchymal pattern scores, which is a statement
about measurement rather than about biology.

Masking and independent risk have to be separated, and nested case-control data separate them. Women
with density in 75% or more of the mammogram had an odds ratio of 4.7 against women below 10%
[@boyd2007]. The odds ratio was 3.5 for screen-detected cancers and 17.8 for cancers appearing
within 12 months of a negative screen. The excess risk persisted for at least 8 years, which masking
alone cannot explain.

Supplemental imaging follows from the masking component rather than from the risk component, and
that policy question belongs in [[BS-3210]] with the DENSE data [@bakker2019].

Two other modifiers belong in a risk model rather than in a lifestyle discussion. Endogenous hormone
levels predict postmenopausal risk, with a relative risk of 2.00 in the highest quintile of serum
oestradiol against the lowest (95% CI 1.47 to 2.71) in a reanalysis of nine prospective studies
[@key2002]. And the genetic architecture of an individual tumour relates to how it was detected, so
mode of detection is not independent of biology [@lijm2018].

The modern models absorb these rather than leaving them to judgement. BOADICEA takes mammographic
density and a polygenic score as inputs alongside family history [@lee2019], which is why a density
result now changes a computed risk rather than a clinical impression.

## BS-0660 Lifestyle, metabolic, and environmental prevention

This section is short because the evidence is thinner than the volume of advice suggests.

Alcohol has the clearest dose relationship. A collaborative reanalysis pooled 53 studies including
58,515 women with breast cancer and 95,067 without [@hamajima2002]. Relative risk rose by 7.1% for
each additional 10 g of alcohol per day (95% CI 5.5 to 8.7). Relative risk was 1.32 at an intake of
35 to 44 g per day and 1.46 at 45 g or more, each against no alcohol. The same analysis estimated
that about 4% of breast cancers in developed countries are attributable to alcohol. Restricting the
analysis to 22,255 women with breast cancer who reported no alcohol showed no independent effect of
smoking.

Dietary intervention has been tested prospectively and the result is modest. Long-term follow-up of
the Women's Health Initiative dietary modification trial found a reduction in breast cancer
mortality with a low-fat dietary pattern [@chlebowski2020]. That is an effect on mortality after
diagnosis rather than a demonstrated large effect on incidence.

Metabolic intervention has been tested directly and failed. Metformin did not improve invasive
disease-free survival against placebo in early breast cancer [@goodwin2022]. Obesity remains
associated with worse outcomes, including differential benefit from endocrine agents by body mass
index [@sestak2010], but an association of that kind does not establish that intervening on weight
reverses it.

Environmental exposures are where claims most outrun data. Night shift work is a worked example of
how the evidence moves. Analysis of three prospective cohorts, combined with a meta-analysis of
prior publications, found no association between night shift work and breast cancer incidence
[@travis2016]. Earlier positive findings had come largely from retrospective designs.

```caution
The honest summary is that lifestyle modification has a real but small effect on breast cancer
incidence, and the interventions with large effects are pharmacologic and surgical. Presenting
lifestyle advice as the main preventive option is comfortable and it misallocates the conversation.
The options with the largest demonstrated effect are in [[BS-0670]] and [[BS-0680]].
```

## BS-0670 Pharmacologic risk reduction and its persistent underuse

The evidence here is unusually strong for a preventive intervention, and it is almost unused. The
2009 ASCO update found that tamoxifen and, after the menopause, raloxifene reduce risk, and did
not recommend aromatase inhibitors or fenretinide outside a trial [@visvanathan2009].

Tamoxifen was tested in 13,388 women at increased risk. It reduced invasive breast cancer by 49%,
with cumulative incidence through 69 months of follow-up of 43.4 per 1,000 women on placebo against
22.0 per 1,000 on tamoxifen [@fisher1998]. Oestrogen receptor positive tumours fell by 69%, and
receptor-negative tumours did not fall at all. Endometrial cancer rose, with a risk ratio of 2.53
(95% CI 1.35 to 4.97), and thromboembolic events rose, both predominantly in women aged 50 or older.

The durability is what makes the benefit-to-harm calculation work. In 7,154 women followed for a
median of 16.0 years, breast cancer occurred in 7.0% of the tamoxifen group against 9.8% of the
placebo group [@cuzick2015]. The hazard ratio was 0.71 (95% CI 0.60 to 0.83). Protection in the
second decade was as large as in the first, with a hazard ratio of 0.69 after 10 years. Five years of
treatment buys at least sixteen years of protection, while the excess of serious adverse events is
concentrated during treatment.

Aromatase inhibitors are more effective and avoid the endometrial and thrombotic signal. In 3,864
postmenopausal women at increased risk, anastrozole for 5 years reduced breast cancer with a hazard
ratio of 0.47 at a median of 5.0 years (95% CI 0.32 to 0.68) [@cuzick2014]. Long-term follow-up at a
median of 131 months showed 85 cancers on anastrozole against 165 on placebo, a hazard ratio of 0.51
(95% CI 0.39 to 0.66) [@cuzick2020]. Benefit continued after treatment stopped, with no excess of
fractures or cardiovascular disease. Exemestane gave a consistent result in 4,560 women, with 11
invasive cancers against 32 on placebo and a hazard ratio of 0.35 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.70)
[@goss2011].

Dose has been addressed, because toxicity is the stated barrier. In 500 women with breast
intraepithelial neoplasia, tamoxifen at 5 mg daily for 3 years halved neoplastic events against
placebo [@decensi2019]. There were 14 events against 28, a hazard ratio of 0.48 (95% CI 0.26 to
0.92). The five-year number needed to treat was 22 (95% CI 20 to 27). Contralateral events fell by 75%.
Patient-reported outcomes did not differ between arms except for slightly more frequent daily hot
flushes.

Now the failure. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies covering 21,423 women at
increased risk found a pooled uptake of preventive therapy of 16.3% (95% CI 13.6 to 19.0)
[@smith2016]. Uptake was 25.2% within trials and 8.7% outside them. Uptake was higher after an
abnormal biopsy, at higher objective risk, and where a physician had recommended it. Only one study
reported persistence of at least 80% at 5 years.

```practice
Physician recommendation is among the strongest modifiable determinants of uptake [@smith2016]. The
decision is often framed as a patient preference problem. It is at least as much an offer problem.

Offer the aromatase inhibitor to postmenopausal women. It reduces incidence more than tamoxifen does
and carries neither the endometrial nor the thrombotic excess [@cuzick2020].

Quote the benefit as absolute numbers over a defined horizon, and quote the harms the same way. A
49% relative reduction and a fall from 43.4 to 22.0 cancers per 1,000 women are the same finding,
and they are heard differently [@fisher1998].

For a woman who declines because of toxicity, low-dose tamoxifen is an evidence-based alternative
rather than a compromise, at least in intraepithelial neoplasia [@decensi2019].

Say that receptor-negative disease is not prevented by any of these agents [@fisher1998]. A woman
whose family history suggests triple-negative disease should hear that before she starts.
```

## BS-0680 Risk-reducing surgery and the decision calculus

Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy produces the largest risk reduction available, and the size of
the effect is not in doubt.

One series followed 639 women with a family history who underwent bilateral prophylactic mastectomy
at a median age of 42, for a median of 14 years [@hartmann1999]. In the moderate-risk group, 4
breast cancers occurred against 37.4 predicted by a risk model, a reduction of 89.5%. Among
high-risk probands, breast cancer occurred in 3 of 214, which is 1.4%. It occurred in 156 of their
403 sisters who had not had the surgery.

In carriers specifically, breast cancer occurred in 2 of 105 women after bilateral prophylactic
mastectomy, which is 1.9% [@rebbeck2004]. It occurred in 184 of 378 matched carriers who did not
have the operation, which is 48.7%, over a mean follow-up of 6.4 years. Risk reduction was
approximately 90% in women with intact ovaries and approximately 95% in women with prior or
concurrent oophorectomy. A larger
prospective cohort of 2,482 carriers found no breast cancers among 247 women after risk-reducing
mastectomy [@domchek2010].

Salpingo-oophorectomy is the decision with a mortality signal behind it. In the same cohort,
risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy was associated with lower ovarian cancer risk [@domchek2010]. It
was associated with lower risk of a first breast cancer in BRCA1 carriers (hazard ratio 0.63, 95% CI
0.41 to 0.96) and in BRCA2 carriers (hazard ratio 0.36, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.82). All-cause mortality
was lower (hazard ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.61), as were breast cancer specific mortality and
ovarian cancer specific mortality.

The asymmetry between the two operations is the part to carry into the consultation. Mastectomy has
the larger effect on breast cancer incidence and no demonstrated effect on overall mortality in
these cohorts. Oophorectomy has a smaller effect on breast cancer incidence and an association with
lower all-cause mortality. They are not interchangeable, and a patient who understands only the
incidence figures will weigh them wrongly.

Three cautions belong with any of these numbers. All of these data are observational, because no
randomised trial of risk-reducing mastectomy exists or will. Women who choose surgery differ from
women who do not. And the follow-up in the carrier series is short relative to a lifetime of risk,
which matters because the comparator group's risk keeps accruing.

```caution
Contralateral risk-reducing mastectomy after a unilateral cancer is a different decision from
bilateral surgery in an unaffected carrier, and the evidence above does not transfer to it. In a
woman whose prognosis is governed by the cancer she already has, removing the other breast reduces a
second cancer without changing the risk that is actually driving her outcome. The exception is the
carrier with early-stage disease and a long expected survival, where the contralateral risk is high
and durable [@kuchenbaecker2017].
```

## BS-0690 Prevention implementation and unequal access

Everything above works. The gap between efficacy and delivered benefit is where the preventable
burden actually sits, and it is not distributed evenly.

Start with the size of the gap. Preventive endocrine therapy reduces breast cancer incidence by
roughly half in the trials, and about one in six eligible women takes it [@smith2016]. No
biologically plausible improvement in the drugs would produce the benefit that closing that gap
would.

The gap widens along familiar lines, and each mechanism has been measured separately.

Risk assessment itself is unequal. The standard risk model required rebuilding for African American
women, and the rebuilt version identified roughly twice the proportion meeting the prevention
eligibility threshold [@gail2007]. Women assessed with a miscalibrated model are not offered what
they qualify for.

Genomic risk stratification is unequal. A polygenic score derived in European-ancestry populations
discriminated substantially worse in women of African ancestry, with an area under the curve of
0.571 against 0.630 [@du2021; @mavaddat2019]. Expanding gene panels widens the disparity in
uninterpretable results rather than narrowing it [@caswelljin2018].

Access to the pathway is unequal at every subsequent step. Time from an abnormal screening
mammogram to biopsy differs by multiple factors operating at patient, facility and area level
[@lawson2022]. Time from diagnosis to surgery differs by race even within an equal-access health
system, and the difference tracks survival [@eaglehouse2019]. Residence in historically redlined
districts is associated with worse breast cancer outcomes [@plascak2022], and cumulative
physiological stress burden is associated with all-cause mortality after a breast cancer diagnosis
[@obenggyasi2023]. Structural racism is a measured exposure in this literature rather than a framing
device [@bailey2017].

Trial participation is unequal, which feeds back into the evidence base. Structural, clinical and
physician-level barriers account for most of the shortfall in enrolment among under-represented
groups, rather than patient refusal [@unger2019].

```practice
Audit the offer, not the uptake. A clinic that records how many eligible women were offered
preventive endocrine therapy, rather than how many accepted it, will find the modifiable step
[@smith2016].

Check which risk model your service uses and in whom it was validated. A single default model
applied to every patient is a silent source of inequity [@gail2007].

Treat the polygenic component of a risk estimate as ancestry-dependent, and say so when the estimate
is given [@du2021]. [[BC-690]] develops why this matters beyond prevention.

Measure the intervals your service controls, from abnormal screen to biopsy and from diagnosis to
treatment. Those intervals differ by group within single institutions, and they are visible to audit
[@lawson2022; @eaglehouse2019].
```

The concluding claim of this chapter is narrow and should be read strictly. Breast cancer prevention
does not currently need a better drug. It needs the drugs that exist to be offered, and the models
that select for them to work in everyone.
