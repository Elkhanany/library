## BS-4730 De novo and recurrent metastatic disease as different populations

Two patients can present with identical imaging and be different problems. One has metastatic
disease at first diagnosis, while the other relapsed after adjuvant treatment. The stage is the
same and the prior history is not.

De novo disease has never been exposed to selection. Nothing has been removed from the tumour
population by treatment, so the sensitivity profile is closer to the untreated state. Recurrent
disease is what survived adjuvant therapy, which makes it a selected population by definition.
What selected it is written down in the chart.

Two facts from the adjuvant record therefore carry information. The first is which agents were
given. The second is how long the disease-free interval was. Relapse during adjuvant endocrine
therapy and relapse eight years after completing it are different statements about dependence.
The annual hazard of recurrence in hormone receptor positive disease stays low and persists for
well over a decade [@saphner1996]. Late relapse is common enough that a long interval should not
be read as proof of a dormant, indolent biology.

The site of first distant relapse differs systematically across subtypes [@kennecke2010].
Intrinsic subtype is largely but not entirely preserved between primary and metastasis
[@cejalvo2017]. The dormancy biology behind the long
interval is developed in [[BC-330]], and the evolutionary account in [[BC-300]].

```caution
Registry comparisons showing longer survival for de novo than for recurrent disease are not
comparing treatments. They are comparing a treatment-naive population with a selected one, over
different lead times, with different competing exposures. The difference is real and it does not
support any inference about what to give.
```

## BS-4740 Disease burden, tempo, and symptom-driven decisions

Three variables govern the urgency of a metastatic decision and they are routinely collapsed
into one. Burden is how much disease is present. Tempo is how fast it is changing. Symptoms are
what the patient feels. They are independent, and they can point in different directions.

Large burden with slow tempo and no symptoms does not require an urgent regimen. Small burden
with rapid tempo and organ dysfunction does. The common error is to let a bulky scan drive the
choice when the rate of change is the variable that predicts what happens over the next three
months.

Tempo is the one that is least often recorded. It needs two measurements and a stated interval,
which a single baseline scan cannot supply. Where an outside scan exists, obtaining it is usually
worth more than ordering a new modality.

Where tempo is genuinely fast, the question becomes whether the response has to be cytotoxic.
That is a question about how quickly each option works rather than about drug class, and it is
argued with its evidence in [[BS-5200]].

```practice
Before choosing a first metastatic regimen, write down four things.

Where the disease is, and which sites are doing the work of making the patient unwell.

How fast the burden changed between the last two assessments, with the interval named.

What the patient reports, separately from what the imaging shows.

What organ function is threatened now, as against what may be threatened in a year.

If tempo and burden disagree, tempo usually determines the next three months. Burden determines
what the disease will look like when the first regimen fails.
```

## BS-4750 Biopsy and molecular profiling at recurrence and at progression

A biopsy at recurrence answers three separate questions, and confusing them produces most of the
argument about whether it is worth doing. The first is whether the lesion is breast cancer. The
second is what its receptor status is now. The third is what actionable alterations it carries.

Tissue confirmation of metastatic disease changes management in a proportion of patients, mainly
through receptor results that differ from the primary [@amir2012]. Whether a difference is true
conversion or assay variation is the subject of [[BC-290]] and is not settled by repeating the
test.

Plasma adds a different kind of information, because it samples what is shedding rather than what
is accessible to a needle. That makes it better at breadth and worse at certainty. Acquired ESR1
mutations are the clearest example, since they appear in patients previously exposed to an
aromatase inhibitor and predict failure of further oestrogen deprivation [@schiavon2015]. Two
technical requirements apply. Matched white blood cell sequencing keeps clonal haematopoiesis
out of the result [@razavi2019]. And a negative plasma test in low-volume disease is
uninformative rather than reassuring, for the shedding reasons in [[BC-390]].

Whether plasma can stand in for tissue has been asked as a trial question rather than a technical
one. The story below states what those studies were built to find out.

```

Four studies stand behind that question. Not one of them randomised a patient between a plasma
result and a tissue result. Read as a class they agree on a single point. A molecular result can
nominate a patient for a drug. The drug then does something in the group so nominated. What they
do not share is the job the assay was given. That difference is one of design rather than of
biology.

{{trial:plasmamatch}} put the test itself on trial. Patients were registered on a circulating tumour
DNA result. Digital PCR was checked against targeted sequencing, and both were checked against
tissue sequencing. The plasma call then decided which treatment cohort a patient entered
[@turner2020plasmamatch]. It agreed closely with tissue sequencing where the biopsy was
contemporaneous. Patients routed on it responded, at rates that differed between the cohorts rather
than describing the strategy as a whole. The other three accepted a tissue result at entry without
requiring one. {{trial:summit}} also accepted a circulating tumour DNA result, {{trial:nimbus}} a
blood-based panel and {{trial:talazoparib-beyond-brca}} a germline test, and each carried a plasma
measurement alongside the entry result. {{trial:summit}} matched an activating HER2 mutation to
neratinib and tracked acquired HER2 and PIK3CA alterations as the triplet failed
[@jhaveri2023summit]. {{trial:talazoparib-beyond-brca}} scored repair deficiency in plasma as well
as in tumour [@gruber2022]. {{trial:nimbus}} followed tumour fraction serially through treatment
[@barrososousa2025]. Only in the first did every patient enter on a plasma result, which is why the
practice rests on that trial and not on the other three.

The markers themselves also differ in kind, and the difference is one of measurement rather than
of biology. An activating point mutation is a present-or-absent call, and a laboratory can be
asked how often it calls correctly against tissue. A repair deficiency score and a mutational
burden are continuous quantities, and neither selects anyone until it is cut somewhere. A cut
nominated in a small correlative subgroup describes that subgroup before it describes the assay.
Neither of those two trials was built to validate one.

What none of this settles is whether routing on plasma beats routing on what the clinic would
otherwise do. The plasma-routed cohorts were single-arm, so a matched response rate says whom to
treat next and not whether the match earned the slot. SUMMIT is the one exception and a partial one.
It randomised a small substudy against fulvestrant with and without trastuzumab, which ranks the
drugs given to a mutation-selected group. It does not rank the sample the mutation was found in.
That agreement with tissue was also measured where the biopsy was contemporaneous, which is the
favourable case rather than the case the plasma test exists to solve. Sensitivity falls as the
archival block ages. A negative result in bone-only or low-shedding disease reports what the tumour
sheds and not what it carries [[BC-390]]. Apart from {{trial:plasmamatch}}, none of the
mutation-selected trials required the alteration to be present in plasma. The eligibility their
response rates describe is not a plasma result alone, which matters on the day a plasma-only finding
is used to start one of these drugs.

One gap here is not partly answered but wholly unstudied. A patient can carry two of these
alterations at once, and each trial nominated one marker and made no provision for the rest.
Which target to act on first has never been asked. Nor has what the second is worth once the
first has been spent.

The ordering rule is narrow, which is to order only what would change the next decision. The
specific list, and the reasoning behind each item, is in [[BS-3050]].

## BS-4760 Treatment selection and sequencing principles

The organising question is not which drug is best. It is which dependency is currently
load-bearing, and which of the available agents attacks it.

That reframing has a practical consequence for how this part of the book is organised. Treatment
is written as a set of branch points rather than as a numbered sequence. The branches that
actually change the answer are ESR1 status, PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN status, HER2 expression level,
and germline BRCA status. A new agent slots into a branch rather than renumbering a list.

Three rules follow. Each is worth stating plainly.

Match the agent to the failure, not to the line. Progression on endocrine therapy after four
years and progression after four months are different failures, and only one of them suggests
the class was never right. The distinction is developed in [[BS-2960]].

Do not assume cross-resistance from a shared indication, because two drugs that treat the same
disease can fail for unrelated reasons. Where dependence is genuinely shared, cross-resistance follows.
Where it is not, progression on one predicts little about the other [[BS-3030]].

Spend the effective agents while the patient can tolerate them. Sequencing plans that reserve
the best option for later assume a later that many patients do not reach.

That third rule is the one a randomised trial has tested directly. The test was run on the class
where reserving looked most defensible. {{trial:sonia}} randomised the order of CDK4/6 inhibition
rather than the drug, giving it first with an aromatase inhibitor or holding it for second line
with fulvestrant [@sonke2024]. Its endpoint was progression across two lines rather than one,
which is the only endpoint that can adjudicate an order. Overall survival was reported later and
showed no difference between the two orders [@wortelboer2026].

Read that as a statement about what a sequencing trial can measure. Deferring the drug was not
shown to cost survival. Giving it first bought a much longer total exposure to the inhibitor and a
higher count of grade 3 or worse adverse events. The trial also assumed that a second line would
be reached. Neither finding transfers to a patient whose disease is unlikely to allow a second
line at all, which is the patient the third rule is written for.

## BS-4770 Response assessment and patterns of progression

Response criteria were built to compare arms in trials. They are used in clinic to decide
whether one person should change treatment. Those are different jobs and the tool fits the first
better than the second. The lineage runs from the World Health Organization's reporting
recommendations [@miller1981] to the revised RECIST 1.1 criteria, which rest on a data warehouse of
more than 6,500 patients [@eisenhauer2009].

Bone-predominant disease is the standard difficulty, and it fails in both directions. Sclerotic
change in a healing lytic lesion can appear as a new finding on computed tomography. Increased
tracer uptake on bone scintigraphy reflects osteoblastic activity rather than tumour cell
number. Both are pictures of response that read as progression [[BS-5190]].

Immunotherapy adds a second problem, because tumour burden can increase before it falls. That is
why the response framework for these agents requires confirmation on a later scan rather than
action on a single assessment [@seymour2017].

The pattern of progression matters as much as the fact of it. Progression in one site while
everything else is controlled is a different event from progression everywhere. The first is
addressed in [[BS-4780]]. The second means the regimen has failed.

A rising tumour marker with stable imaging and a stable patient is not progression. It is a
reason to shorten the interval to the next assessment. The full list of things that masquerade
as resistance is in [[BS-3040]].

## BS-4780 Oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease

The two terms describe different situations and the shared prefix hides that.

Oligometastatic disease means few deposits at presentation or at relapse. The definition rests
on a count, and a count is a property of the imaging used to make it. Sensitive imaging moves
patients out of the category without changing their biology. That is a measurement statement,
not a biological one, and it should be said out loud before anyone is offered ablative therapy
on the strength of a number of lesions.

Oligoprogression means most sites are controlled and one or a few are not. Here the inference is
about clonal structure rather than about total burden. A single progressing deposit while the
rest responds suggests a resistant subclone in one place, which is exactly the picture developed
in [[BC-300]]. Treating that site locally and continuing the systemic regimen is a reasonable
response to that inference.

The honest position on the evidence is that it is thin. Two randomised tests are usually
quoted, and they are usually read as pointing in opposite directions.

```

They do not point in opposite directions, because they were not aimed at the same target. They
separate on which site was treated, and on what each was built to detect there.

{{trial:ea2108}} treated the intact primary and left the metastatic deposits to systemic therapy
[@khan2022]. {{trial:sabr-comet}} did the reverse, ablating every metastatic lesion in patients
whose primary malignancy was already controlled [@palma2020]. A trial of the breast and a trial of
the deposits are not two answers to one question, but one answer each to two different ones.

Three further differences make the contrast a matter of design rather than of radiotherapy.
The breast cancer trial randomised only patients whose disease had not progressed on months of
systemic therapy. Its population is therefore selected for systemic sensitivity. Its negative
result answers a narrower question than the one usually asked of it. SABR-COMET enrolled four
common primary tumours together. Most of its patients did not have breast cancer, so a
breast-specific estimate from it is a subgroup result in a trial that was never powered for one.
SABR-COMET also used a screening design, whose looser significance threshold exists to decide
whether a phase 3 trial is worth running rather than to license practice. It is quoted as though
it had been that phase 3 trial.

The case for operating on the primary came from registries. In a national database, women with
stage IV disease whose primary was excised with free margins had a hazard ratio for death of 0.61
against those who had no operation [@khan2002]. A Geneva registry found a 40% lower risk of breast
cancer death after complete excision [@rapiti2006], and institutional series associated surgery with
longer progression-free survival [@babiera2006; @rao2008]. In another cohort, surgery remained
independently associated with survival after adjustment for the more favourable disease of the women
who received it [@blanchard2008]. Editorials at the time warned that the
women chosen for surgery were those who would have done well anyway [@morrow2006; @olson2008].

Two earlier randomised trials then disagreed with each other. In Mumbai, 350 women whose disease had
responded to chemotherapy were randomised, and median overall survival was 19.2 months with
locoregional treatment and 20.5 months without it [@badwe2015]. MF07-01 randomised 274
treatment-naive women at diagnosis [@soran2018]. The hazard of death was 34% lower with surgery, but
the difference appeared only with longer follow-up, and its subgroup analyses were unplanned. A
prospective registry of de novo disease in the same era found the 21-gene recurrence score
independently prognostic for time to progression and two-year survival in ER-positive, HER2-negative
disease [@king2016]. Biology, in other words, was sorting these patients before any operation did.

The decision therefore rests on {{trial:ea2108}}, and only for the decision it randomised. A
patient presenting de novo, responding to systemic therapy, with the primary still in place, is
exactly the patient in that trial. Operating on her buys local control rather than time. That is a
defensible reason to operate when the primary is ulcerating or threatening the chest wall. It is
not a reason to operate on a scan.

What the table cannot show is the question this section is actually about. Nobody randomised
oligoprogression. No trial compared ablating the single progressing deposit and continuing
the systemic regimen against changing the regimen. That is the commoner decision, and the one
with a clonal rationale behind it. No column anywhere records the imaging used to count the
lesions, so the entry criterion that defines the whole category is invisible in the row that
reports it. The three rationales for treating locally are set out with their evidence in
[[BS-5230]].

## BS-4790 Treatment-free intervals and cumulative toxicity

Metastatic treatment is usually continuous, and the assumption that it must be goes mostly
unexamined. Two things make it worth examining.

The first is that toxicity classes differ in whether they accumulate. Endocrine agents and
targeted oral drugs produce toxicity that is largely a function of current exposure. Taxane
neuropathy, anthracycline cardiac injury and marrow reserve are cumulative. Stopping the first
group returns the patient close to baseline. Stopping the second group stops the accumulation
and does not reverse it.

The second is that the goal is time at acceptable function, not maximal tumour suppression at
every moment. A planned interval off cytotoxic therapy in a patient with controlled,
low-tempo disease buys recovery that a dose reduction does not.

The decision is best made explicitly rather than by drift. Drift looks like a dose reduction
made during a toxicity episode that is never reversed, followed by a schedule nobody chose.
Toxicity management in detail is in [[BC-640]], and the long-horizon version of this argument is
in [[BS-4910]].

```practice
Ask at each cycle which toxicities are cumulative and which are not. Only the cumulative ones
argue for a break rather than a reduction.

State a plan for the interval before starting it. Duration, what will be monitored, and what
would end it early.

Record the reason a dose was reduced. A reduction made for an episode that resolved is a
candidate for reversal, and it will not be reversed unless someone wrote down why it happened.
```

## BS-4800 Clinical trials and early integration of palliative care

Trial enrolment is a line of therapy, not a consolation. It is best discussed at the start of a
line rather than after the last option has failed, because eligibility criteria are strictest at
the point where they are hardest to meet. Prior therapy limits, organ function thresholds and
washout requirements all tighten with each additional line.

Access to trials is not distributed evenly, and that fact belongs in the clinical conversation
rather than only in a policy chapter. The determinants are set out in [[BC-690]] and the design
consequences in [[BC-740]].

Early palliative care is an intervention with randomised evidence behind it. In patients with
metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer, early integrated palliative care improved quality of life
and reduced depressive symptoms at 12 weeks. Fewer patients received aggressive care at the end
of life. Median survival was longer in the integrated arm [@temel2010]. The population was lung
cancer, and the survival finding should not be carried across to breast cancer as a claim about
this disease.

What transfers is the design point, which is that referral was triggered by diagnosis and timing
rather than by prognosis or by the exhaustion of treatment. That trigger is the part of the model
that can be copied into a breast cancer clinic. End-of-life care is developed in [[BC-680]].
