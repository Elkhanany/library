## BS-6430 Integrated multi-omics

Integration is usually presented as an additive exercise in which more layers give a better picture.
The more accurate framing is that each layer answers a different question and the layers disagree in
informative ways.

Genomics gives what is altered. Transcriptomics gives what is being expressed, averaged over whatever
cells were in the sample unless single-cell methods are used. Proteomics gives what is actually
present, which is what drugs bind. Phosphoproteomics gives pathway activity state. Epigenomics gives
what the cell is permitted to express.

The disagreements are the point. Transcript and protein correlate imperfectly, and the residual is not
noise. Proteomics also disagrees with the receptor classification the tumour was assigned. Spatial
proteomic analysis across 280 tumour regions found receptor phenotypes co-existing within single
tumours, and regional proteomic states that the tumour's immunohistochemical classification does not
resolve [@mardamshina2025]. Proteomic heterogeneity rose with grade and was independent of genomic
heterogeneity. A PIK3CA mutation predicts benefit from a PI3K inhibitor imperfectly, and
phosphoproteomic pathway activity is a different measurement of the same question.

The practical constraint is sample. Multi-omic integration on a single core biopsy integrates several
measurements of one small piece of tissue. The layers agree about that piece. Whether the piece
represents the tumour is a separate question that no amount of integration addresses, and it is the
question Part VI raises.

## BS-6440 Computational pathology and imaging

This is where computation has delivered most, and the delivery is narrower than the enthusiasm.

In screening, artificial-intelligence-supported reading has been tested prospectively. In a randomised
screening trial, AI-supported single reading against standard double reading detected cancers at a
comparable or higher rate while substantially reducing reader workload [@lang2023], with subsequent
analysis characterising the cancers detected [@hernstrom2025]. This is a real result in a real
workflow, and it addresses a resource problem rather than an accuracy ceiling.

In biomarker scoring the story is more instructive, and it corrects a common expectation. Pathologist
concordance for HER2 immunohistochemistry at the low end is poor, with overall percent agreement of
25% in a multi-institutional assessment [@robbins2023], which is the measurement problem set out in
[[BC-110]]. The natural expectation is that computation fixes reproducibility.

It has not yet. Across 10 independently developed computational pathology models applied to 1124
whole-slide images from 733 patients, median pairwise overall percent agreement for the four-level
HER2 categorical score was 65.1%, with a kappa of 0.51. Agreement was high for HER2 3+ against not 3+
at 97.3% and lowest in the HER2-low categories. Pathologists showed numerically higher concordance
than the models [@mckelvey2026].

```caution
An algorithm is reproducible against itself and not necessarily against another algorithm. Ten models
trained on different data with different labels disagree with each other about the same slide. The
reproducibility problem in HER2-low scoring has been relocated from observers to models, not solved.
What computation can deliver here requires reference standards and benchmark datasets that do not yet
exist.
```

## BS-6450 Predictive modeling, foundation models, and the validation burden

Foundation models trained on large unlabelled image or sequence corpora produce general-purpose
representations that can be adapted to specific tasks with modest labelled data. That is a genuine
change in how models are built, and it lowers the data cost of each new task.

It does not lower the validation cost, and the validation cost is where clinical translation fails.
The requirements are unchanged by model architecture. The model must be evaluated on data from
institutions, scanners, staining protocols and populations not represented in training. Performance
must be reported by subgroup, not only in aggregate, because aggregate accuracy can be maintained
while performance collapses in a minority subgroup. The comparison must be against current practice
rather than against a weaker model. And the outcome must be clinically meaningful, which means a
change in management or outcome rather than an area under the curve.

Two failure modes specific to pathology and radiology deserve naming. Batch effects from scanner and
staining differences are learnable, so a model can achieve high accuracy by identifying the site
rather than the biology. And label leakage occurs when the ground truth was itself generated with
knowledge of the outcome, which makes retrospective performance uninterpretable.

A model that has not been tested prospectively in the workflow where it will be used has not been
tested. Retrospective external validation is necessary and is not sufficient.

## BS-6460 Longitudinal data and real-world evidence

Real-world data describe patients who were excluded from trials, which is most patients. Older
patients, those with comorbidity, those with poor performance status and those from underrepresented
groups appear in registries and electronic records and not in the trials discussed in [[BS-6400]].
That is the case for using these data and it is a strong one.

The limits are specific. Treatment assignment is not random, so any comparison is confounded by
indication, and the confounding runs in the direction of making treated patients look better or worse
depending on why they were selected. Outcome ascertainment is incomplete, and progression in
particular is recorded unsystematically because imaging is done when clinically indicated rather than
on schedule. Missingness is informative, since a test not ordered is usually a test not needed or not
affordable, and both are related to outcome.

The specific bias that recurs in oncology real-world analyses is immortal time. A patient classified
by a treatment they received at three months must have survived three months to receive it. Analyses
that do not handle this with time-varying exposure generate survival advantages out of arithmetic.

Real-world evidence is strong for questions randomisation cannot answer: incidence, utilisation
patterns, toxicity in unselected populations, adherence and access. It is weak for comparative
efficacy, which is the use it is most often put to.

## BS-6470 Causal inference, bias, and external validation

Prediction and causation are different tasks and a model that does one well may do the other badly. A
prognostic model estimates outcome under current practice. A predictive model estimates the difference
in outcome between two treatments. Only the second supports a treatment decision, and only a
randomised comparison or a well-specified causal analysis can establish it.

This is the distinction that separates the genomic assays in [[BC-380]] from ordinary prognostic
scores. {{trial:tailorx}} and {{trial:rxponder}} were run because a prognostic score, however accurate,
does not establish that withholding chemotherapy is safe [@sparano2018; @kalinsky2021]. The prognostic
information was already available. The trials supplied the predictive claim.

When randomisation is unavailable, the analysis has to be specified as though it were a trial. Define
eligibility, the treatment strategies compared, the assignment time, the outcome and the follow-up
window before looking at the data. Handle confounding explicitly through propensity methods or
instrumental variables, and state the assumptions those methods require. Negative control outcomes are
an underused check, since a spurious association with an outcome the exposure cannot affect reveals
residual confounding.

External validation is the minimum test that separates a model from a description of its training
data. A model validated only by cross-validation within one institution's data has demonstrated
internal consistency and nothing about transportability.

## BS-6480 Digital twins and in-silico trial simulation

A digital twin is a patient-specific model updated with that patient's data and used to simulate
outcomes under alternative treatments. In oncology the concept remains substantially aspirational, and
being precise about why is more useful than either enthusiasm or dismissal.

The versions that work are narrow. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic models predict exposure under
alternative dosing well enough to guide dose adjustment, and they underpin the dose-optimisation work
in [[BS-6380]]. Radiotherapy planning has used patient-specific physical simulation for decades.
Mathematical models of tumour growth and resistance evolution can predict qualitative behaviour under
alternative scheduling, which is the basis of the adaptive strategies in [[BC-770]].

The version that does not yet work is a model that predicts a specific patient's response to a
specific systemic therapy. It requires knowing the tumour's composition and its evolutionary
trajectory, and both are the quantities Part VI argues are not measurable from routine sampling. A
simulation is not more accurate than the state estimate it starts from.

Synthetic control arms are the nearest application with current traction. Constructing a control arm
from historical or real-world data is feasible where the outcome is objective and standard of care is
stable. It inherits every limitation in [[BS-6460]], and it fails exactly where it is most wanted,
which is in fast-moving settings where the historical control no longer reflects current practice.

## BS-6490 Data governance, privacy, and algorithmic equity

Genomic data are identifying, and de-identification in the usual sense does not apply to them. A
genome is a permanent identifier that also carries information about relatives who did not consent.
Governance frameworks built for de-identified clinical data do not transfer.

Algorithmic inequity in health care is documented rather than hypothetical, and the mechanism is
usually the choice of label rather than the model. A widely deployed algorithm predicting health care
need used cost as a proxy for illness. Because less is spent on Black patients at equal illness
severity, the algorithm assigned them lower risk scores at equal sickness. Correcting the label would
have raised the proportion of Black patients identified for additional support from 17.7% to 46.5%
[@obermeyer2019].

That mechanism generalises directly to oncology. Any model trained on a label shaped by access rather
than biology will encode the access disparity as a prediction. Time to treatment initiation, receipt
of guideline-concordant therapy, trial enrolment and even recorded stage at diagnosis are all shaped
by access, and the disparities are documented in [[BC-690]] and [[BC-700]].

```practice
Before deploying any model, ask what the label actually measures and whether it was generated under
equal access. Then report performance stratified by race, ethnicity, insurance status and site.
Aggregate performance can be preserved while a subgroup's performance degrades, and only stratified
reporting detects it.
```
