## BS-3180 Population screening strategies and the trial evidence

Every randomised trial of breast screening randomised an invitation, not a mammogram. That single
design fact governs how the results should be read.

An independent panel reviewed the randomised evidence and the observational literature and reached a
conclusion that has held up. In a meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials, the relative risk of breast
cancer mortality for women invited to screening against controls was 0.80, with a 95% confidence
interval of 0.73 to 0.89 [@ukpanel2012]. That is a 20% relative reduction. The panel considered the
internal biases and the age of the trials and judged 20% still reasonable.

The absolute version is the one a patient can use. For every 10,000 women in the United Kingdom aged
50 and invited to screening for the next 20 years, about 43 deaths from breast cancer would be
prevented [@ukpanel2012].

Three properties of that estimate should be held together. It is an effect of a programme, diluted
by everyone who was invited and did not attend. It comes from trials conducted before modern
adjuvant therapy, and a better treated population has less to gain from earlier detection. And more
recent observational studies give larger estimates, which the same panel judged likely to be biased.

The disagreement in this field is not mostly about whether screening works. It is about how large
the benefit is, and what it costs in overdiagnosis. [[BS-3230]] takes the second question.

## BS-3190 Risk-adapted and hereditary-risk screening

A uniform screening interval is an administrative decision rather than a biological claim.
Risk-adapted screening asks whether the interval and the modality should follow individual risk, and
it is being tested prospectively.

The WISDOM study is the first population-scale attempt. In its risk-based arm, 21,631 participants
received a polygenic risk score. Adding that score to the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium
clinical model changed the screening recommendation for 14% of women aged 40 to 49, and for 10% of
women aged 50 to 74 [@fergus2025]. Projected net screening encounters at population level were
similar with and without the score.

Read that carefully, because it is more modest than the framing usually suggests. The polygenic
score moved roughly one woman in seven in the younger group, and it did not reduce the total amount
of screening. The trial has not reported whether risk-based screening is as safe as annual
screening.

Hereditary risk is a different problem with a different answer. Carriers of high-penetrance
pathogenic variants are managed with intensified surveillance from a younger age, usually including
magnetic resonance imaging. The models that assign risk, and the variants that drive it, belong to
[[BS-0630]] and [[BC-070]].

```caution
A risk model is calibrated in the population it was built in. Polygenic scores were derived
predominantly in cohorts of European ancestry, and WISDOM had to adapt its score for use in diverse
populations [@fergus2025]. Among the 21,631 participants who received a score, differences between
major racial and ethnic groups were significant at P below 0.001. The report does not quantify how
large those differences were, so what is established is that they exist. A score that is
miscalibrated in a group will
systematically assign the wrong interval to that group, which converts a precision tool into a
disparity mechanism. [[BS-5900]] treats the general problem.
```

## BS-3200 Mammography, tomosynthesis, ultrasound, and MRI

The four modalities fail in different ways, and the differences are physical rather than
philosophical.

Mammography projects a three-dimensional breast onto a plane, so overlapping fibroglandular tissue
can hide a lesion and can also create one. Tomosynthesis acquires a limited-angle arc and
reconstructs planes, which reduces superimposition. It produces many more images per examination and
takes longer to read.

Ultrasound adds no ionising radiation and is highly operator-dependent, with a false positive rate
that limits its use as a primary screening test. Magnetic resonance imaging detects lesions by
contrast kinetics rather than by density, which is why it is the most sensitive modality and also
the least specific.

The endpoint question is where this section earns its place. Cancer detection rate is the outcome
every modality comparison reports, and it is not the outcome that matters. A modality that finds
more cancers without lowering the rate of advanced disease has increased diagnosis and not benefit.
The Tomosynthesis Mammographic Imaging Screening Trial was designed with advanced cancer as its
primary endpoint for exactly this reason, and it has not yet reported that endpoint.

The general rule follows. Detection is a property of the assay. Benefit is a property of what the
detected disease would have done, and only the second can justify a change in policy.

## BS-3210 Dense breasts, supplemental imaging, and notification policy

Breast density is two separate things that share one word. It masks lesions on mammography, which is
a property of the assay. It is also an independent risk factor for developing breast cancer, which
is a property of the tissue. Conflating them produces most of the confusion in this area, and
[[BS-0650]] develops the risk side.

The masking side has been tested. The DENSE trial randomised 40,373 Dutch women aged 50 to 75 with
extremely dense breast tissue and a normal screening mammogram. It allocated 8,061 to an invitation
for supplemental magnetic resonance imaging and 32,312 to mammography alone [@bakker2019]. The
interval cancer rate was 2.5 per 1,000 screenings in the invitation group and 5.0 per 1,000 in the
mammography-only group. The difference was 2.5 per 1,000, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.0 to
3.7 and a P value below 0.001.

The uptake figure is the one that makes this a policy result rather than an assay result. Only 59%
of invited women accepted. Among those who actually underwent magnetic resonance imaging the
interval cancer rate was 0.8 per 1,000, against 4.9 per 1,000 in those who declined [@bakker2019].

The costs are quantified in the same trial. The cancer detection rate on magnetic resonance imaging
was 16.5 per 1,000 screenings, with a 95% confidence interval of 13.3 to 20.5. The false positive
rate was 79.8 per 1,000. Positive predictive value was 17.4% for recall and 26.3% for biopsy [@bakker2019].

```caution
The DENSE endpoint was interval cancer, not mortality. Fewer interval cancers means the disease was
found at screening instead of between screens. It does not establish that any woman lived longer, and
some of the additional cancers found will be overdiagnosed [[BS-3230]].

Density notification legislation creates an obligation to inform without creating an entitlement to
act. A woman told her breasts are dense, in a system that does not fund supplemental imaging, has
been handed a problem rather than a service. [[BS-5980]] covers who can actually obtain the follow-up
test.
```

## BS-3220 Interval cancers and what they reveal about biology

An interval cancer is one diagnosed between scheduled screens. Two entirely different events produce
that label, and separating them is the whole content of this section.

The first is a cancer that was present at the last screen and was not seen. That is a failure of the
assay or the reader, and masking by dense tissue is its commonest cause. The second is a cancer that
was not present, or not detectable, at the last screen and grew to clinical attention within the
interval. That is a statement about growth rate.

Genetic architecture separates the two. One unselected Swedish cohort followed 5,099 women with
breast cancer. Carriers of protein-truncating variants in breast cancer predisposition genes were
younger, and had more aggressive tumours, including oestrogen receptor-negative, high-grade, highly
proliferative, luminal B and basal-like disease [@lijm2018]. Ten-year breast cancer-specific
survival was worse, with a hazard ratio of 1.65 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.16 to 2.36.
Excluding 92 BRCA carriers, the association with high grade and worse survival persisted, with a
hazard ratio of 1.76 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.21 to 2.56.

Common variants pointed the other way. Higher polygenic risk score was associated with less
aggressive tumour characteristics, including oestrogen receptor-positive, low-grade,
low-proliferation luminal A disease [@lijm2018]. Among women with low mammographic density, below
25%, a higher polygenic score was associated with a lower risk of interval compared with
screen-detected cancer. The odds ratio was 0.77, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.64 to 0.93.

That last finding is the argument. In women in whom masking is minimal, genetic risk architecture
still predicts mode of detection. Interval cancers are therefore enriched for fast-growing biology
and not only for dense tissue.

The consequence for policy is specific. Shortening the interval addresses the growth-rate cause.
Changing the modality addresses the masking cause. They are different interventions for different
problems, and a programme that applies one to the other will underperform.

```interplay target=BC-350
Mode of detection is a heterogeneity readout. A screening programme is a filter with a fixed temporal
resolution, and what passes through it is selected for growth rate. Screen-detected disease is
therefore systematically enriched for the slow, receptor-positive end of the spectrum, and interval
disease for the fast, receptor-negative end. That is the same axis that separates early from late
recurrence in [[BS-2870]], and the same axis that determines how much circulating tumour DNA a
deposit sheds [[BS-3460]]. One biological variable, growth rate, is being measured three times by
three instruments that appear unrelated.
```

## BS-3230 Quantifying overdiagnosis and communicating it honestly

Overdiagnosis is the detection of a cancer that would never have become clinically apparent in that
woman's lifetime. It is a counterfactual, so it can never be observed in an individual patient. It
can only be estimated in a population, and only where a control group was never offered screening.

The best estimates come from three randomised trials in which control-group women were not invited
to screen at the end of the active trial period. A meta-analysis of those trials gave two figures.
Excess incidence was 11% as a proportion of the cancers diagnosed in the invited group over the long
term, with a 95% confidence interval of 9% to 12%. It was 19% as a proportion of the cancers
diagnosed during the active screening period, with a confidence interval of 15% to 23%
[@ukpanel2012].

Those two numbers describe the same phenomenon. They differ by a factor of nearly two because they
have different denominators, and both are correct. Quoting one without its denominator is the single
commonest source of argument about overdiagnosis, and a claim that screening overdiagnoses 11% or
19% of cancers means nothing until the denominator is stated [@ukpanel2012].

The absolute frame is better for a conversation. For every 10,000 women aged 50 invited to screening
for 20 years, about 43 breast cancer deaths would be prevented and about 129 cancers, invasive and
in situ, would be overdiagnosed [@ukpanel2012]. That is roughly one death prevented for every three
overdiagnosed cases identified and treated. Just over 1% of women invited to begin screening would
have an overdiagnosed cancer over the following 20 years.

```practice
Give absolute numbers with a shared denominator, not percentages. "For every 10,000 women like you
invited over 20 years, about 43 deaths prevented and about 129 cancers found that would never have
caused trouble" is a sentence a person can weigh [@ukpanel2012]. "A 20% mortality reduction and 19%
overdiagnosis" is not, because the two percentages are of different things.

Say that overdiagnosis cannot be identified in an individual. The woman in front of you cannot be
told whether her cancer is one of the 129, and that is the honest and unwelcome part [@ukpanel2012].

Do not present the estimates as precise. They come from old trials with real limitations, and the
panel that produced them said they should be regarded as an approximate guide [@ukpanel2012].

The active surveillance trials for low-risk ductal carcinoma in situ are the direct clinical response
to this problem, and {{trial:comet}}, {{trial:lord}} and {{trial:loris}} are covered in
[[BS-4250]].
```

## BS-3240 Artificial intelligence in screening, performance and equity effects

Artificial intelligence in mammography screening has now been tested in a randomised trial, which
distinguishes it from most claims in this area.

The {{trial:masai}} trial randomised women in the Swedish national programme to artificial
intelligence-supported screen reading or to standard double reading. Its prespecified clinical
safety analysis covered 80,033 women. The cancer detection rate was 6.1 per 1,000 screened in the
intervention group and 5.1 per 1,000 in the control group. The ratio was 1.2, with a 95% confidence
interval of 1.0 to 1.5 and a P value of 0.052 [@lang2023]. Screen-reading workload fell by 44.3%.

The larger protocol-defined analysis of 105,934 women sharpened the result. The cancer detection
rate was 6.4 per 1,000 against 5.0 per 1,000, a ratio of 1.29 with a 95% confidence interval of 1.09
to 1.51 and a P value of 0.0021 [@hernstrom2025]. Invasive cancers rose from 217 to 270, and in situ
cancers from 45 to 68. The recall ratio was 1.08 and the false positive ratio was 1.01, neither
statistically significant. Positive predictive value of recall rose, with a ratio of 1.19 and a P
value of 0.012. Screen-reading workload fell by 44.2%.

More cancers found, no more false positives, and half the reading time is a genuine result. It is
also not yet an outcome result. The primary endpoint of {{trial:masai}} is interval cancer rate, and it has
not reported. Some of the extra detection was in situ disease, which is where overdiagnosis
concentrates [[BS-3230]].

The equity question is the live controversy, and the most useful fact about it is a gap in the
evidence. {{trial:masai}} did not collect race and ethnicity data [@lang2023]. The strongest trial supporting
artificial intelligence in screening therefore cannot say whether its benefit is distributed
equally.

Two mechanisms pull in opposite directions and neither has been measured in a trial. Artificial
intelligence can substitute for a second reader, which raises the floor in programmes that cannot
staff double reading, and those are disproportionately the programmes serving underserved
populations. A model trained on one population can also perform differently on another, and breast
density, age distribution and tumour subtype distribution all differ across populations [[BS-5870]].

```caution
Do not read the absence of a demonstrated equity harm as evidence of its absence. Subgroup
performance was not measured in the trial that produced the strongest evidence [@lang2023]. A model
can improve average performance and worsen performance in a subgroup at the same time, and an
average is exactly the statistic that hides it. [[BS-6490]] treats algorithmic equity as a general
problem.
```

## BS-3250 Emerging detection technologies

Three families of technology are in development, and each faces a different constraint.

Contrast-enhanced mammography and abbreviated magnetic resonance imaging address the same problem
from opposite directions. The first adds vascular contrast to an existing widely available platform.
The second shortens an expensive protocol to increase throughput. Both aim at the dense-breast
masking problem in [[BS-3210]], and both are limited by access and cost rather than by physics.

Blood-based multicancer early detection tests face an arithmetic problem that is not solved by
improving the assay. Positive predictive value in a screening population is governed by prevalence,
and breast cancer prevalence in a screening round is low. A test with excellent specificity still
generates mostly false positives when prevalence is low enough, and each one initiates a diagnostic
workup across multiple organ systems.

There is also a biological ceiling specific to circulating tumour DNA. Shedding scales with cell
turnover and vascular access, so a small early tumour contributes very little DNA to plasma
[[BS-3460]]. The tumours these tests are least able to detect are precisely the tumours a screening
test exists to find. Improving analytic sensitivity narrows that gap without closing it, because the
signal is bounded by the biology rather than by the instrument.

None of these technologies has a randomised trial showing a reduction in advanced-stage disease or
in mortality. The evidence bar set in [[BS-3180]] applies to them as it applied to mammography.

## BS-3260 Early detection in resource-constrained settings

Where mammographic screening is not feasible, the question becomes whether a cheaper test can
downstage disease enough to matter.

A cluster-randomised trial in Mumbai answered it more precisely than anything else in this
literature. Twenty geographically distinct clusters and 151,538 women aged 35 to 64 were allocated
to one of two arms. The screening arm received four rounds of clinical breast examination by trained
female primary health workers every two years. The control arm received cancer awareness and active
surveillance [@mittra2021].

Downstaging was clear. The proportion of women diagnosed with stage III or IV disease was 37% in the
screening arm and 47% in the control arm, with a P value of 0.001 [@mittra2021].

Mortality was not. The overall breast cancer mortality rate ratio was 0.85, with a 95% confidence
interval of 0.71 to 1.01 and a P value of 0.07, which is a non-significant 15% reduction. A post hoc
subset analysis found a rate ratio of 0.71 in women aged 50 and older, with a 95% confidence
interval of 0.54 to 0.94 and a P value of 0.02. In women under 50 the rate ratio was 0.93, with a
confidence interval of 0.79 to 1.09. All-cause mortality showed a rate ratio of 0.95, with a
confidence interval of 0.81 to 1.10 [@mittra2021].

The honest reading is that the trial met its downstaging endpoint and missed its mortality endpoint,
and that the positive subgroup was defined after the fact.

The structural point is larger than the trial. Early detection converts a late diagnosis into an
early diagnosis. It converts an early diagnosis into a life saved only where diagnostic pathology,
surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy are available and reachable. Detection programmes built
ahead of treatment capacity generate diagnoses that the system cannot act on, and [[BS-6100]] makes
the case that pathology infrastructure is the rate-limiting step. The resource-stratified framework
for deciding what to build first is in [[BS-6060]].
