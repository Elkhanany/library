## BS-7890 The biology this part assumes

This chapter carries no figures. It is the spine of the part, and the chapters
after it are the evidence. Where a claim here rests on a trial, the section that
argues that trial is named.

The defining fact of this subtype is a dependency. The tumour uses oestrogen
receptor signalling as a growth input, and removing that input is the single
most effective thing oncology can do to it. Nothing else in breast cancer
returns as much benefit for as little toxicity. The mechanism is set out in
[[BC-140]], and the drugs that act on it in [[BC-430]].

Three properties follow from the dependency, and each shapes a decision in this
part.

It is slow. Proliferation is lower than in the other subtypes, so the disease
takes longer to recur and longer to kill. That is why the recurrence curve in
this subtype does not flatten at five years, and why the risk of a first distant
recurrence is still real at fifteen. [[BC-330]] is the chapter about that tail,
and it is the reason extended endocrine therapy is a question at all.

It is measurable, but only as a continuum forced into a category. The receptor
is scored by immunohistochemistry against a threshold, and the threshold was set
by convention rather than by biology. A tumour at one per cent and a tumour at
ninety-five per cent are both called positive and do not behave alike.
[[BC-110]] is the measurement chapter, and most of the apparent disagreement
between trials in this part is a disagreement about which populations their
thresholds admitted.

It is losable. Endocrine dependence is a state rather than a fixed property, and
the tumour can leave it. It can mutate the receptor so that the drug no longer
fits, bypass the receptor through a parallel growth pathway, or abandon the
lineage that made it dependent in the first place. [[BC-140]] covers the first
two and [[BC-310]] the third. Which of these has happened is what the later-line
chapters are trying to tell apart.

## BS-7900 What the subtype does to the treatment decision

Every decision in this part is a version of one question. How much treatment
does this particular tumour need, given that the treatment works and that the
patient will be taking it for years?

That framing is unusual in oncology and it is worth stating plainly. In the
other two subtypes the dominant question is what to add. Here it is what to
leave out, because the backbone already works and its costs accumulate over a
decade rather than a course.

Four things follow.

**The comparator is never nothing.** A trial in this subtype adds to, or
substitutes into, an endocrine backbone that the patient would have received
anyway. A regimen that looks impressive against no treatment may add little to
the backbone. This is why the control arm column is the first thing to read in
every table in this part.

**Risk is what allocates, and risk is estimated rather than measured.**
Clinicopathological risk, genomic assays and nodal status all estimate the same
unobserved quantity, which is the probability that micrometastatic disease is
already present. [[BC-380]] covers the assays. The chapters here treat a risk
score as an eligibility criterion rather than as a fact about the patient.

**Adherence is a treatment variable.** An endocrine course runs five to ten
years, and a substantial fraction of it is not taken. Every effect size in this
part was achieved under real adherence rather than perfect adherence, which
means the trial result already includes the loss. It cannot be claimed twice.

**Time is the currency in advanced disease.** The disease is usually incurable
once it has spread and often measured in years. What a line of treatment buys is
time before the next one, and time spent on a better-tolerated regimen. Deciding
the order of treatments is therefore a real decision rather than a technicality,
and [[BC-550]] states the principles this part assumes.

## BS-7910 The spine, from diagnosis to last line

Read in order, the part answers six questions.

**Does this early tumour need chemotherapy?** The default is endocrine therapy.
Chemotherapy is added where the risk is high enough that endocrine therapy alone
is likely to be insufficient, and genomic assays exist to find the patients for
whom it is not. [[BC-490]] argues the assay trials and the populations they
enrolled.

**Which endocrine agent, and for how long?** The choice follows the source of
oestrogen, so it follows menopausal status. The duration question is separate
from the agent question and has its own trial record. [[BC-790]] carries both,
including the extension trials and the fracture cost that comes with them.

**Should a cell cycle inhibitor be added after surgery?** Four trials asked this
and two answered yes. The disagreement between them is not about the class, and
[[BC-800]] is the chapter that establishes what it is about instead.

**Is there a reason to treat before surgery?** In this subtype the neoadjuvant
setting is a weaker instrument than it is in the others, because response is
lower and pathological complete response carries less prognostic information.
[[BC-810]] says what the setting still buys, and [[BC-540]] says what it is for
in general.

**What happens at first metastatic presentation?** Endocrine therapy with a
CDK4/6 inhibitor, for almost everyone, with the argument about who can safely be
spared it. [[BC-560]] is the decision chapter and [[BC-880]] carries the trial
record for the class.

**What happens when that fails?** The answer depends on how it failed. A mutated
receptor is a reason to change the endocrine agent [[BC-870]]. An activated
parallel pathway is a reason to add a pathway inhibitor, which [[BC-870]] also
carries. A tumour that has left endocrine dependence altogether is a reason to
stop trying to restore it, and [[BC-890]] is the chapter about what replaces it.

## BS-7920 Where this part disagrees with itself

Four places, and a reader who notices them is reading correctly.

**The neoadjuvant and adjuvant chapters price response differently.** In
[[BC-810]] a pathological complete response is a weak signal in this subtype. In
[[BC-490]] a response-guided strategy is treated as a live allocation tool. Both
are defensible, and the difference is that the second is measuring a change in
proliferation rather than a clearance of disease.

**The duration evidence and the escalation evidence point in opposite
directions.** [[BC-790]] shows that more endocrine therapy buys less than the
first five years did. [[BC-800]] adds two years of a second drug on top. The
patient populations differ, and the chapters do not reconcile this because the
trials do not.

**First line and later line disagree about what a CDK4/6 inhibitor is for.**
[[BC-880]] treats the class as a first-line standard and also carries the
evidence that continuing it past progression is weakly supported. The commonest
escape mechanism does not need the kinase, which is the biological reason the
two positions can both hold.

**The advanced chapters disagree about how much a biomarker should decide.**
[[BC-870]] argues that an alteration should select the patient. [[BC-890]] argues
that in later lines the prior-therapy history places the result more reliably
than the biology does. Both are arguments about where the evidence is thin, not
about what the drugs do.
