## BS-3710 Radiobiology and treatment planning

Two numbers govern every fractionation decision, and both are estimates rather than constants.

The first is fraction-size sensitivity, summarised by the ratio of the linear to the quadratic term
in the standard dose-response model. A low ratio means a tissue is hurt disproportionately by larger
fractions. Historically, tumours were assumed to have a high ratio and late-responding normal
tissues a low one, which is the argument for many small fractions.

Breast cancer breaks that assumption, and the evidence is a direct measurement rather than an
inference. In {{trial:start}} Trial A, 2236 women were randomised between 50 Gy in 25 fractions and
two 13-fraction schedules designed to measure fraction sensitivity [@starta2008]. The fitted ratio
was 4.6 Gy for tumour control, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.1 to 8.1. For late change in
breast appearance on photographic assessment it was 3.4 Gy, with an interval of 2.3 to 4.5. Because
those two estimates sit close together, larger fractions do not buy tumour control at the cost of
late normal tissue effect in this disease.

An earlier randomised trial had reached the same answer. It assigned 1410 women to 50 Gy in 25
fractions or to 39 Gy or 42.9 Gy in 13 fractions, all over five weeks [@owen2006]. Ten-year
ipsilateral relapse was 12.1%, 14.8% and 9.6%. The fitted fraction sensitivity for tumour was 4.0 Gy,
similar to the estimate for late effects in healthy breast tissue.

The second number is the dose delivered to organs that are not the target. That is a planning
property rather than a biological one, and it is discussed in [[BS-3780]].

Dose homogeneity within the breast is also a planning property, and randomised trials show that
patients feel it. In 331 analysed patients, breast intensity-modulated radiotherapy reduced moist
desquamation from 47.8% with a wedged technique to 31.2% [@pignol2008]. In a second trial, simple
intensity modulation reduced suboptimal overall cosmesis at five years, with an odds ratio of 0.68.
It also reduced telangiectasia, with an odds ratio of 0.58 [@mukesh2013].

```caution
The fraction-sensitivity estimate above is a model parameter fitted to two schedules in one
population, with a confidence interval wide enough to include very different biology. It licenses
hypofractionation of a breast or chest wall in the doses that were tested.

It does not license extrapolating the same arithmetic to much larger fractions, to reirradiation, or
to volumes that were not in the trials. Each of those has been tested separately, and where it has
not been tested the parameter is being used outside its range.
```

## BS-3720 Whole-breast, partial-breast, and chest wall irradiation

Radiation after breast-conserving surgery halves recurrence and reduces breast cancer death by
about a sixth. The meta-analysis that established this covers 10,801 women in 17 randomised trials
[@ebctcg2011rt]. The 10-year risk of any first recurrence fell from 35.0% to 19.3%, an absolute
reduction of 15.7 percentage points. The 15-year risk of breast cancer death fell from 25.2% to
21.4%, an absolute reduction of 3.8 percentage points.

The relationship between those two figures is the most useful number in this chapter. About one
breast cancer death was avoided by year 15 for every four recurrences avoided by year 10, and that
ratio held across prognostic subgroups [@ebctcg2011rt]. It converts a local control benefit into a
mortality expectation, and it is what makes omission decisions calculable rather than intuitive.

A boost to the tumour bed adds a smaller increment, and its size depends on age. The EORTC boost
trial randomised women with a microscopically complete excision, after 50 Gy to the whole breast, to
a further 16 Gy or none [@bartelink2001]. Five-year local recurrence fell from 7.3% to 4.3%. In
women aged 40 or younger it fell from 19.5% to 10.2%. The relative reduction was similar at every
age, so the absolute gain shrinks as the baseline risk falls [@antonini2007]. Young age and
high-grade invasive ductal carcinoma were the strongest risk factors for local relapse, and margin
status had no significant influence [@jones2009b]. At 20 years, the boost reduced ipsilateral
recurrence from 31% to 15% in women aged 50 or younger with adjacent ductal carcinoma in situ
[@vrieling2017].

The price is cosmetic. On panel assessment at three years, the boost was one of the factors
associated with a worse cosmetic result [@vrieling2000]. Technique matters less than dose: in one
institution's experience of 552 patients, electron, photon and interstitial implant boosts gave
similar local control and cosmesis [@frazier2001].

After mastectomy the benefit depends on nodal burden. A meta-analysis of 8135 women in 22 trials
compared chest wall and nodal irradiation with no radiation after mastectomy and axillary dissection
[@ebctcg2014pmrt]. In women with one to three positive nodes, overall recurrence and breast cancer
mortality fell, with rate ratios of 0.68 and 0.80. In women with no positive nodes there was no effect on recurrence or
mortality. In women with four or more positive nodes the rate ratios were 0.79 and 0.87.

The trials behind that meta-analysis reported survival benefits a decade earlier. In the Danish 82b
trial, 1708 premenopausal women with stage II or III disease received CMF with or without chest wall
and nodal irradiation after mastectomy [@overgaard1997]. Ten-year overall survival was 54% with
radiotherapy and 45% without it. The companion 82c trial, in postmenopausal women taking tamoxifen,
found 45% against 36% [@overgaard1999]. The British Columbia trial showed the same direction at 20
years, with breast cancer-specific survival of 53% against 38% [@ragaz2005].

Radiotherapy changed the pattern of failure in the Danish trials. Fewer patients had a locoregional
recurrence as the first event, and fewer developed distant metastases overall [@nielsen2006]. An
editorial accompanying the 82b report read this as local control stopping metastases at their source
[@hellman1997]. ASCO's guideline on post-mastectomy radiotherapy then graded each indication by the
level of evidence behind it [@recht2001]. The failure pattern without radiotherapy made the case.
Among 1,031 patients treated by mastectomy and doxorubicin-based chemotherapy alone, 10-year isolated
locoregional recurrence was 4%, 10%, 21% and 22% with none, one to three, four to nine, and ten or
more involved nodes, and the chest wall was the commonest site [@katz2000].

After neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the evidence for post-mastectomy radiotherapy is retrospective, and
it points to presentation as well as response. In one institutional analysis, radiotherapy improved
locoregional control and survival for clinical T3 tumours, stage III disease and four or more
positive nodes, whatever the response to chemotherapy [@huang2004]. Among 106 patients with a
pathological complete response treated by mastectomy, 10-year locoregional recurrence in clinical
stage III disease was 7.3% with radiotherapy and 33.3% without it [@mcguire2007]. A small phase II
protocol of neoadjuvant MVAC for locally advanced disease had shown locoregional relapse of 7% with
chest wall radiation and 31% without it [@abdelwahab1998]. After breast-conserving surgery, in 751
patients, locoregional control after neoadjuvant chemotherapy depended on subtype and on whether a
complete response was achieved [@swisher2016]. Whether nodal response can now remove the indication
is the question {{trial:nsabp-b51}} asked, in [[BS-3730]].

Partial-breast irradiation restricts the target to the tumour bed. Two large trials reached
different verdicts on almost the same absolute difference, and the reason is instructive.
{{trial:import-low}} randomised 2018 women and found five-year local relapse of 1.1% with
whole-breast radiotherapy, 0.2% with a reduced-dose approach and 0.5% with partial-breast
radiotherapy [@coles2017]. Both experimental arms met the non-inferiority criterion.
{{trial:nsabp-b39}} randomised 4216 women and found a 10-year cumulative incidence of ipsilateral
recurrence of 4.6% with accelerated partial breast irradiation and 3.9% with whole-breast
irradiation [@vicini2019]. The hazard ratio of 1.22 did not meet the prespecified equivalence
criterion.

```caution
Those two trials used different techniques, different schedules and different populations, and they
set different statistical thresholds. One declared non-inferiority and one failed equivalence, with
absolute differences in local recurrence of under one percentage point in both.

This is the clearest illustration in the book that a de-escalation verdict is a joint product of the
data and the margin. Neither trial found a large difference. Only one of them was designed in a way
that could call the difference acceptable. [[BS-6370]] sets out why the margin, not the result,
often decides.
```

Technique and schedule decide the toxicity of partial-breast irradiation. The RAPID trial randomised
2135 women to external-beam partial-breast irradiation of 38.5 Gy in ten twice-daily fractions, or to
whole-breast irradiation [@whelan2019]. Eight-year ipsilateral recurrence was 3.0% and 2.8%, which
met non-inferiority. Adverse cosmesis was already more frequent with the partial-breast schedule at
three years, at 29% against 17% on nurse assessment [@olivotto2013].

The Florence trial gave 30 Gy in five once-daily fractions with intensity modulation to 520 women
[@meattini2020]. Ten-year ipsilateral recurrence was 3.7% against 2.5% after whole-breast
irradiation, with less acute and late toxicity and a better cosmetic result. A trial of 258 women
treated by multicatheter brachytherapy or electrons reported 20-year ipsilateral recurrence of 9.6%
against 7.9% [@polgar2021]. Its brachytherapy arm had better cosmetic results than whole-breast
irradiation. In {{trial:import-low}}, patients reported fewer adverse effects over five years after
partial-breast and reduced-dose radiotherapy than after whole-breast radiotherapy
[@bhattacharya2019].

Patient selection for partial-breast irradiation is set out in consensus statements. The ASTRO
consensus statement, first published in 2009, was updated to lower the age of its "suitable"
group from 60 to 50 years [@correa2017]. The update placed low-risk ductal carcinoma in situ
meeting RTOG 9804 criteria in that group. It advised telling patients that intraoperative
radiotherapy carries a higher risk of ipsilateral recurrence than whole-breast irradiation, and
restricted it to invasive cancers considered suitable.

## BS-3730 Regional nodal irradiation

Two randomised trials define what regional nodal irradiation adds, and they agree more than their
headline conclusions suggest.

{{trial:ma20}} randomised 1832 women with node-positive or high-risk node-negative disease treated
by breast-conserving surgery [@whelan2015]. At 10 years, overall survival was 82.8% with nodal
irradiation and 81.8% without it, with a hazard ratio of 0.91 and an interval of 0.72 to 1.13.
Disease-free survival was 82.0% against 77.0%, with a hazard ratio of 0.76. Grade 2 or higher acute
pneumonitis occurred in 1.2% against 0.2%, and lymphoedema in 8.4% against 4.5%.

{{trial:eortc22922}} randomised 4004 patients to internal mammary and medial supraclavicular
irradiation or not [@poortmans2015]. At 10 years, overall survival was 82.3% against 80.7%, with a
hazard ratio of 0.87 and an interval of 0.76 to 1.00. Disease-free survival was 72.1% against 69.1%.
Breast cancer mortality was 12.5% against 14.4%, with a hazard ratio of 0.82.
At 15 years, breast cancer mortality and any breast cancer recurrence remained lower with nodal
irradiation, but overall survival was 73.1% against 70.9%, with a hazard ratio of 0.95
[@poortmans2020].

Read together, nodal irradiation improves disease outcomes by a few percentage points and does not
clearly improve overall survival. The decision is therefore a trade against lymphoedema, pneumonitis
and cardiac dose rather than a straightforward benefit.

The internal mammary chain is the most contested part of that target. A Danish cohort allocated
internal mammary irradiation by laterality, treating right-sided cancers and sparing the heart in
left-sided ones [@thorsen2022]. Among 3089 women, 15-year overall survival was 60.1% with it and
55.4% without it, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.86. A Korean randomised trial of 735 women found
no significant disease-free survival gain from adding it to nodal irradiation, with a hazard ratio of
0.80 [@kim2022]. An ad hoc subgroup with medial or central tumours did better with it.

Which nodes are in the target is also a planning decision. Consensus guidelines define the volumes
for elective nodal irradiation [@offersen2016]. When the RTOG breast atlas was tested against the
location of involved nodes, it could miss axillary disease in patients with clinically involved
nodes [@gentile2015].

The target is now being redefined by response rather than by anatomy. {{trial:nsabp-b51}} randomised
1641 patients whose biopsy-proven nodal disease had cleared to ypN0 after neoadjuvant chemotherapy,
and 1556 of them entered the primary-event analysis [@mamounas2025]. Regional nodal irradiation
gained them nothing measurable. The hazard ratio for
invasive recurrence or breast cancer death was 0.88, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.60 to 1.28.
The axillary surgical counterpart of this shift is in [[BS-3660]].

## BS-3740 Fractionation, hypofractionation, and acceleration

Three generations of trials shortened the same treatment, and each generation was validated before
the next began.

A Canadian trial randomised 1234 women to 50 Gy in 25 fractions or 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions
[@whelan2010]. Ten-year local recurrence was 6.7% and 6.2%, an absolute difference of 0.5 percentage
points with a 95% confidence interval of minus 2.5 to 3.5. Good or excellent cosmetic outcome was
71.3% and 69.8%.

{{trial:start}} tested 13-fraction and 15-fraction schedules against 25 fractions [@haviland2013].
At 10 years, locoregional relapse in Trial A was 6.3% with 41.6 Gy, 8.8% with 39 Gy and 7.4% with
50 Gy. In Trial B it was 4.3% with 40 Gy in 15 fractions against 5.5% with 50 Gy in 25 fractions.
Breast induration, telangiectasia and oedema were less common in the lower-dose arms.
The first report of Trial B, at five years, had shown locoregional relapse of 2.2% after 40 Gy and
3.3% after 50 Gy, with fewer late effects on photographs and in patients' own assessments
[@startb2008].

A Danish trial tested 40 Gy in 15 fractions in 1854 women with node-negative cancer or ductal
carcinoma in situ [@offersen2020]. Three-year breast induration was 9.0% against 11.8% with 50 Gy,
and nine-year locoregional recurrence was 3.0% against 3.3%.

The earlier FAST trial gave five fractions once a week to 915 women [@brunt2020b]. At ten years, normal tissue
effects after 28.5 Gy did not differ significantly from those after 50 Gy in 25 fractions. After 30
Gy they were more frequent.

{{trial:fast-forward}} compressed treatment to one week [@brunt2020]. Among 4096 patients, the
five-year incidence of ipsilateral breast tumour relapse after 40 Gy in 15 fractions was 2.1%, and
both five-fraction schedules met the non-inferiority criterion. The two five-fraction arms differed
from each other in normal tissue effects rather than in tumour control. Moderate or marked
clinician-assessed effects at five years were reported in 9.9% after 40 Gy, 15.4% after 27 Gy and
11.9% after 26 Gy.

That last comparison is the useful one. Total dose separated the two five-fraction arms, and
fraction number did not. Acceleration is not intrinsically toxic. The dose chosen to accompany it
is what determines late effect.

```practice
Shorter schedules matter to the medical oncologist for reasons that are not radiobiological. They
shorten the interruption to systemic therapy planning and reduce the number of visits.

For patients travelling long distances, or paying for transport and time away from work, the
difference between 25 visits and 5 is often the difference between completing radiation and not
completing it. The access argument is developed in [[BS-6010]] and [[BS-6020]].

Do not assume a longer schedule is safer. In these trials the shorter schedules produced equal or
fewer late normal tissue effects.
```

## BS-3750 Integration with systemic therapy and reconstruction

Most of the accepted sequencing rules rest on toxicity reasoning and consensus rather than on
randomised comparison, and saying so is more useful than repeating them as though they were proven.

Concurrent anthracycline and radiation is avoided because both damage the heart and the skin.
Taxanes are generally completed before radiation starts. Trastuzumab is continued through radiation.
Endocrine therapy is commonly given concurrently. The evidence for each of these differs in
strength, and the first is better supported than the last.

The order of chemotherapy and radiation after breast-conserving surgery has been randomised. Giving a
12-week chemotherapy course first produced more local and fewer distant or regional first
recurrences at five years, a difference of borderline significance [@recht1996]. With longer
follow-up there was no significant difference in any event, distant metastasis or death, although
the trial could not rule out a clinically important difference [@bellon2005]. For tamoxifen, three
non-randomised comparisons found no difference in local recurrence or survival between concurrent
and sequential use [@pierce2005; @harris2005; @ahn2005]. A meta-analysis of 11 studies compared 1291
women treated concurrently with 1179 treated sequentially [@li2016]. It found no difference in skin
toxicity, pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis, local or distant recurrence, or survival, with tamoxifen
or an aromatase inhibitor. For HER2-directed therapy, a systematic
review found no serious adverse events reported with concurrent radiation, most of the data
concerning trastuzumab [@mignot2017]. It also judged the data too heterogeneous for reliable
conclusions.

Newer agents raise questions that have not been answered. CDK4/6 inhibitors and PARP inhibitors both
suppress the marrow, and both are increasingly given in the adjuvant setting alongside or adjacent
to radiation [[BC-450]]. Overlapping myelosuppression is the practical constraint, and the trials
that established these agents were not designed to answer the sequencing question.

Reconstruction and radiation interact in one direction. Among patients receiving post-mastectomy
radiation, autologous reconstruction carried lower odds of a breast complication at two years than
implant reconstruction, at 0.47 with an interval of 0.27 to 0.82 [@jagsi2018]. Unirradiated patients
showed no difference between the approaches. The reconstructive decision therefore needs the
radiation decision to have been made first, which is the point made in [[BS-3680]].

The other interaction worth naming is delay. Every additional component in the sequence lengthens
the interval from surgery to the completion of local therapy. Whether that interval matters is not
well characterised, and treating it as harmless is an assumption rather than a finding.

## BS-3760 Omission in low-risk and older patients

Omission is the most developed de-escalation in this book, and the trials share a structure. Each
accepts a defined increase in local recurrence in exchange for avoided toxicity, on the argument
that local recurrence in these populations is salvageable and that mortality is dominated by
competing causes.

{{trial:calgb9343}} randomised 636 women aged 70 or older with clinical stage I, oestrogen receptor
positive disease treated by lumpectomy and tamoxifen [@hughes2013]. At 10 years, 98% of the
irradiated group and 90% of the tamoxifen-only group were free from local and regional recurrence.
There was no difference in mastectomy rate, time to distant metastasis, breast cancer specific
survival or overall survival. Ten-year overall survival was 67% and 66%. The five-year report had
shown locoregional recurrence of 1% with irradiation and 4% without it [@hughes2004].

A second trial asked the question from age 50, in 769 women with node-negative tumours of 5 cm or
less [@fyles2004]. Five-year local relapse was 0.6% with radiotherapy and tamoxifen and 7.7% with
tamoxifen alone, with no significant difference in distant relapse or overall survival.

{{trial:prime2}} randomised 1326 women aged 65 or older with node-negative, hormone receptor
positive tumours of 3 cm or less, all receiving endocrine therapy [@kunkler2023]. The 10-year
cumulative incidence of local recurrence was 9.5% without radiotherapy and 0.9% with it, a hazard
ratio of 10.4 with an interval of 4.1 to 26.1. Distant recurrence as a first event was 1.6% and
3.0%. Overall survival at 10 years was 80.8% and 80.7%. At five years, ipsilateral recurrence had
been 4.1% without radiotherapy and 1.3% with it [@kunkler2015].

{{trial:lumina}} selected by biology rather than by age alone [@whelan2023]. Five hundred women aged
55 or older with T1N0, grade 1 or 2, luminal A disease defined by a centrally assessed Ki-67 index
of 13.25% or less received endocrine therapy without radiotherapy. Local recurrence at five years
was 2.3%, with a 90% confidence interval of 1.3% to 3.8%, against a prespecified acceptability
boundary of 5%.

```caution
A hazard ratio of 10.4 for local recurrence and an identical overall survival are both accurate
descriptions of {{trial:prime2}} [@kunkler2023]. They are descriptions of different quantities, and
quoting either one alone misleads.

The absolute difference in 10-year local recurrence was 8.6 percentage points. That is the number a
patient is deciding about. The ratio describes how much more likely recurrence became, and in a
population where the baseline risk is low, a large ratio can describe a small absolute change [@kunkler2023].

Note also what omission does not omit. Every patient in these trials received endocrine therapy, and
the local control they achieved depends on it. Omitting radiation in a patient who will not take
endocrine therapy is a different decision with no trial behind it.

{{trial:lumina}} is a single-arm cohort tested against a prespecified acceptability boundary rather
than against a randomised comparator [@whelan2023]. That design choice is what its result rests on,
and [[BS-6370]] explains why the boundary carries the argument.
```

Molecular selection is the next step and is not yet in routine use. A 16-gene signature developed for
this purpose identified patients whose 10-year locoregional recurrence without radiation was 6% in
one validation cohort and 7% in another [@sjostrom2023]. Radiation gave that group no significant
benefit, while the high-risk group did benefit. The interaction test between the signature and
radiation in the combined validation cohort gave a p value of 0.066, which is the honest summary of
where the evidence stands. Selection for omission in early hormone receptor positive disease is taken
up in [[BC-490]] and in older adults in [[BC-620]].

## BS-3770 Reirradiation and metastatic applications

Reirradiation after a second lumpectomy is now an alternative to mastectomy for in-breast recurrence
in selected patients. {{trial:rtog1014}} enrolled 58 evaluable patients with a unifocal recurrence smaller than 3 cm,
arising more than a year after breast-conserving therapy [@arthur2020]. Each received partial breast
reirradiation to 45 Gy in 30 twice-daily fractions. The five-year cumulative incidence
of further in-breast recurrence was 5%, with an interval of 1% to 13%. The five-year cumulative
incidence of mastectomy was 10%. Grade 3 late adverse events occurred in 4 of 58 patients, and no
higher grade late events were reported. The wider management of locoregional recurrence is in
[[BC-530]].

In metastatic disease radiation does three separable jobs. It relieves symptoms, it prevents
structural catastrophe in bone and spinal cord, and it treats the central nervous system. Those
applications belong to [[BC-600]] and are not repeated here.

The fourth application is ablative intent in oligometastatic disease, and its evidence base is
thinner than its adoption. {{trial:sabr-comet}} randomised 99 patients with one to five metastases
across several primary tumour types, 18 of them breast, in a 1:2 ratio to standard care or standard
care plus stereotactic ablative radiotherapy [@palma2020]. Five-year overall survival was 17.7% and
42.3%.

```caution
{{trial:sabr-comet}} was designed as a randomised phase 2 screening study with a significance
threshold set at 0.20, and it enrolled 99 patients across four common primary tumours [@palma2020].
Roughly a fifth of them had breast cancer.

A survival difference of that size in a trial of that design is a reason to run a phase 3 trial. It
is not evidence that ablating all visible metastases benefits a patient with breast cancer. The
definition of oligometastatic disease it relied on is itself an imaging convention rather than a
biological state. The argument is developed in [[BS-4780]].
```

## BS-3780 Cardiac and pulmonary sparing and late effects

Cardiac dose is the late effect that has changed practice most, because it is measurable before
treatment starts and can be modified.

One population-based case-control study covered 2168 women treated between 1958 and 2001, of whom
963 had a major coronary event and 1205 were controls [@darby2013]. The rate of major coronary
events rose by 7.4% per gray of mean heart dose, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.9% to 14.5%.
The relationship was linear with no apparent threshold. The excess began within five years of treatment
and continued into the third decade. The average of the mean heart doses in that cohort was 4.9 Gy.

Three consequences follow. Mean heart dose is a number that can be reported, so it can be asked for.
Any technique that lowers it, including breath-hold, prone positioning and restriction of the target
volume, is a treatment decision rather than a technical detail. And the absence of a threshold means
there is no dose below which cardiac sparing stops being worth pursuing.

Position is one of those techniques. In women with large breasts, a randomised comparison of prone
with supine hypofractionated treatment found less acute skin toxicity in the prone position
[@mulliez2013]. Ipsilateral lung dose and mean left anterior descending artery dose were also lower.

Second cancers are the other durable late effect. A meta-analysis of 13 cohort studies included 762,468 patients [@grantzau2015]. More than five years
after diagnosis, radiotherapy was associated with an increased risk of second non-breast cancer,
with a relative risk of 1.12. The relative risk
was 1.39 for lung cancer, 1.53 for oesophageal cancer and 2.53 for sarcoma. Lung cancer risk was
highest at 15 years or more, at 1.66. No association was found with thyroid cancer.

Those are relative risks against unirradiated breast cancer patients, drawn from cohorts treated
largely with older techniques and larger fields. They describe the direction of the hazard reliably
and the current magnitude poorly.

Pulmonary toxicity is mostly a function of the volume of lung in the field, which is why it rises
with nodal irradiation. {{trial:ma20}} reported grade 2 or higher acute pneumonitis in 1.2% against
0.2% [@whelan2015]. Late cardiac and pulmonary surveillance in survivors is developed in
[[BS-5480]] and [[BS-5490]].

## BS-3790 Immune effects of radiation and candidate radiosensitivity biomarkers

Radiation kills by damaging DNA, and the same damage is an immune stimulus. Cytosolic DNA generated
by irradiation is sensed by cGAS, activates STING, and drives type I interferon production. That
pathway is the bridge between genomic instability and immune recognition developed in [[BS-1490]].

The dose relationship is counterintuitive and worth knowing. In cancer cell lines, the exonuclease
Trex1 is induced by doses above roughly 12 to 18 Gy per fraction and degrades the cytosolic DNA that
would otherwise trigger sensing [@vanpouillebox2017]. Repeated irradiation at doses below that
induction threshold amplified interferon beta, recruited Batf3-dependent dendritic cells, and was
required for the CD8 T cell priming that produced systemic tumour rejection with checkpoint
blockade. Larger fractions were immunologically worse rather than better.

Those are preclinical data in mouse models and cell lines, and they should be labelled as such.
Abscopal responses in patients are rare, and no randomised trial in breast cancer has shown that
adding radiation to checkpoint blockade improves survival. The immunotherapy context is in
[[BC-470]] and the luminal immune deficit that constrains it is in [[BS-1700]].

Radiosensitivity biomarkers remain investigational, and the two with the most support point in
opposite directions. One genomic model converts physical dose into a predicted biological effect. Across 1615 patients in
11 pooled cohorts it was associated with time to first recurrence and with overall survival, with
hazard ratios of 0.98 and 0.97 [@scott2021]. Physical dose alone was associated with neither. The 16-gene signature discussed in [[BS-3760]] identifies patients unlikely to benefit from
radiation at all [@sjostrom2023].

```interplay target=BC-350
Radiation is usually taught as a local modality and modelled as a dose to a volume. The mechanistic
picture in Part VI makes it something else as well. A treatment that generates cytosolic DNA,
activates innate sensing and alters antigen presentation is acting on the microenvironment and not
only on the irradiated cells. The dose and fraction size that optimise cell kill are not necessarily
the ones that optimise that second effect, and the preclinical evidence suggests they may conflict.
```

The honest summary is narrow. No radiosensitivity assay is in routine clinical use. The candidates
with the strongest data predict who can safely receive less rather than who should receive more,
which is the same direction of travel as the rest of this chapter.
