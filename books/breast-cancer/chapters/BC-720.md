## BS-6160 Translating evidence into practice

A trial establishes that an intervention can work under specified conditions. Whether it does work in
routine care is a different question, and it has its own literature, its own methods and its own
failure modes.

Implementation frameworks exist because the reasons an effective intervention fails to spread are
numerous and overlapping. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research organises them into
five domains: characteristics of the intervention itself, the outer setting, the inner setting, the
characteristics of the individuals involved, and the process of implementation [@damschroder2009].
Its value is diagnostic. Before choosing an implementation strategy, a team has to say which domain
the obstruction sits in, because strategies aimed at one domain do nothing to another.

The distinction that matters most in practice is between effectiveness and implementation outcomes.
Effectiveness outcomes are clinical: survival, recurrence, toxicity. Implementation outcomes are
different quantities: adoption, reach, fidelity, sustainment. An intervention can have excellent
effectiveness and zero reach, and a programme reporting only the first will describe a success that
no patient experienced.

Two examples in this part make the point concretely. Clinical breast examination downstaged breast
cancer in a randomised trial and the effect on mortality did not reach significance [@mittra2021].
Patient navigation improved timely care overall and produced no benefit at all in the first 90 days
[@freund2014]. Neither is a negative result. Both are results about where in a pathway an
intervention acts, which is an implementation question rather than an efficacy one.

## BS-6170 Multidisciplinary care models and referral systems

The multidisciplinary team is the standard organisational form for breast cancer care, and what it
actually delivers is worth separating into three distinct functions.

It pools expertise, so a decision reflects surgical, oncological, radiological and pathological
input. It standardises, so two patients with the same disease at the same institution receive the
same recommendation. It creates an audit point, because a documented team decision can be measured
against a guideline.

Those three functions are not equally well evidenced, and only the third is straightforwardly
measurable. A team meeting that reviews sixty cases in an hour has performed the third function and
possibly not the first.

The referral system around the team is what determines who reaches it. Its performance is
measurable at three transitions: primary care to diagnostic service, diagnostic service to team
review, and team decision to treatment start. Each transition has its own loss rate.

The loss rates are not small. Among 46 185 abnormal screening mammograms recommended for biopsy,
34.6% were not resolved within 30 days and 12.2% were not resolved within 90 days [@lawson2022]. The
identity of the screening facility accounted for part of the racial difference in that delay, which
means the transition is a property of the institution rather than only of the patient [@lawson2022].

```practice
Measure the transitions rather than the meeting. A team that reviews every case it receives can
still sit downstream of a referral pathway that loses a tenth of its patients before review. The
quantity to audit is the proportion of patients with an abnormal result who reach a documented team
decision, and the interval in which they reach it.
```

## BS-6180 Guideline adherence and justified variation

Not all variation from a guideline is error, and a quality programme that treats adherence as the
outcome will suppress the variation that should exist.

Three categories of departure need separating, and only one of them is a quality problem.

Justified variation follows from a patient characteristic the guideline did not address. Comorbidity,
frailty, competing risk of death and informed patient preference all produce departures that are
correct. A guideline derived from trials that excluded patients over 75 does not govern an
85-year-old with cardiac failure.

Unjustified variation reflects clinician habit, local convention or resource availability presented
as clinical judgement. This is the target of a quality programme.

Evidentiary variation arises where the guideline itself rests on weak evidence, and two reasonable
clinicians reach different conclusions from the same data. Enforcing adherence here converts
uncertainty into false consensus.

The distinction cannot be made from an adherence rate alone, which is why a bare percentage is
uninformative. It requires recorded reasons for departure. A service that documents the reason can
separate the three categories. A service that records only concordance cannot, and will improve its
measured adherence by discouraging justified departures.

There is also a floor below which the question does not arise. In a population-based cohort of 2998
patients with invasive breast cancer, 62% of those eligible had no 21-gene assay performed
[@dunn2024]. That is not a variation in judgement about what the assay shows. It is the absence of
the input on which the judgement would be made.

## BS-6190 Quality indicators and outcome measurement

An indicator is a measurement convention. It is chosen for reproducibility across institutions
rather than because it marks the point where outcome changes, and reading it as the second is the
same error described for assay thresholds in [[BC-280]].

Indicators divide into three types and they behave differently under pressure.

Structure indicators describe what a service has: a linear accelerator, an accredited laboratory, a
specialist nurse. They are easy to verify and weakly connected to outcome.

Process indicators describe what a service does: the proportion receiving an assay, the proportion
starting treatment within a target interval. They are actionable, and they are the type most
vulnerable to being met without being achieved.

Outcome indicators describe what happens to patients: survival, recurrence, patient-reported
outcome. They are the point of the exercise and they require risk adjustment, which imports every
problem of the model used to adjust.

The Global Breast Cancer Initiative indicators are a clean worked example of process measurement
designed deliberately, since all three are health system quantities rather than clinical ones
[@boucheron2025]. Their weakness is instructive too. An attempt to measure them across 21 Asian countries found that
over 60% had no published national data on staging or on the intervals from presentation to
diagnosis and from diagnosis to treatment. Only five of the 21 reported data on treatment
completion. Definitions of delayed diagnosis and of treatment completion varied between countries
[@ong2023].

```caution
Two failure modes attach to any process indicator and both look like improvement in the report.
Denominator management excludes difficult patients from the eligible population, which raises the
measured rate without changing what anyone received. Threshold clustering concentrates performance
just inside the target, so a 30-day indicator produces a spike of treatment starts at day 28 and no
improvement at all for the patients waiting 90 days. The second is the more damaging, because the
tail is where the harm was.
```

## BS-6200 Policy as natural experiment, coverage expansion and access reform

Coverage reform is one of the few interventions in this part that can be studied with something
approaching a control group. Where a policy is adopted by some jurisdictions and not others at a
known date, a difference-in-differences design compares the change in adopting jurisdictions with
the change in non-adopting ones.

The design is not randomised and its assumption is specific. It requires that, absent the policy, the
two groups' outcomes would have moved in parallel. Jurisdictions choose whether to adopt, and they
choose for reasons that may also affect outcomes.

The clearest breast cancer result concerns Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act. Among
9322 patients diagnosed with de novo stage IV breast cancer in states that expanded Medicaid in
January 2014, survival was compared before and after. Before expansion, White patients
had better overall survival than patients from racial and ethnic minority groups, with an adjusted
hazard ratio of 1.22 and limits of 1.10 to 1.35. After expansion that difference was no longer
observed, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.96 and limits of 0.86 to 1.08 [@malinowski2022].

That is a survival disparity narrowing after a change in coverage and not in treatment. It is the
strongest available evidence that a substantial part of the gap is payment rather than biology,
which is the argument of [[BS-5860]] arriving through a policy door.

The result is also narrower than it is usually reported, and the qualification matters. A separate
analysis of breast, colon, lung and prostate cancer found that Medicaid expansion produced no change
in receipt of stage-appropriate definitive treatment for racial and ethnic minority patients
compared with non-expansion states. It did produce an increase of 3.62 percentage points in the
proportion treated within 30 days, with limits of 1.63 to 5.61, and no change in the proportion
treated within 90 days. At minority-serving hospitals there was no change in any of the three
measures [@nguyen2021].

Coverage changed timing at the patient level. It did not change what treatment was received, and it
did not change anything at the facilities serving the most patients from minority groups. Both
findings are about the same policy.

## BS-6210 Health system accountability and public reporting

Public reporting assumes a causal chain, and each link in it can fail independently.

The chain runs: a measure is published, someone reads it, that reader changes behaviour, and the
change improves outcome. The intended readers are patients choosing a provider, referrers choosing
where to send, and institutions responding to reputational pressure.

Patient choice is the weakest link for breast cancer specifically. Choice requires an alternative
within reach, and the geography set out in [[BC-700]] means many patients have one accessible centre.
A published measure changes nothing for a patient with no second option.

Institutional response is the strongest link and also the most vulnerable to gaming, through the
denominator and threshold mechanisms described in [[BS-6190]].

Risk adjustment is where accountability schemes most often break, and the failure has a predictable
direction. A centre treating more socially disadvantaged patients will have worse crude outcomes. If
the adjustment model includes social factors, the centre is held to a lower standard and disparity
is normalised into the measurement. If it excludes them, the centre is penalised for its case mix
and the incentive is to avoid those patients. Both choices are defensible and they cannot both be
made.

The honest resolution is to report both. A crude figure describes what happened to the population.
An adjusted figure describes the institution's contribution. A scheme reporting only one of the two
is answering only one of the two questions.

## BS-6220 Equity-focused implementation research

An implementation framework asks why an effective intervention did not spread. An equity-focused one
asks why it spread unevenly, which is a different question requiring different variables.

The Health Equity Implementation Framework integrates determinants from implementation science with
determinants from health disparities research, and adds the clinical encounter itself as a level of
analysis [@woodward2019]. Its distinguishing feature is that it separates barriers that are general
from barriers that are specific to the disadvantaged group. A preliminary application to hepatitis C
treatment found both kinds in the same population. Poor care coordination affected everyone.
Accounts from peers of racial discrimination in the same health system affected one group
[@woodward2019].

The methodological consequence is the one to carry into practice. An intervention evaluated on its
average effect can widen a gap while improving the mean. It will do so whenever uptake is easier for
the already advantaged, which is the default for any intervention requiring time, travel, literacy or
connectivity.

This gives a concrete design requirement rather than a principle. Report the effect by subgroup as a
prespecified outcome, not as a secondary analysis. Patient navigation is the model. Its benefit was
greatest at the centres with the longest delays under usual care, which was reported as part of the
main result rather than discovered afterwards [@freund2014]. That single finding tells a service
where to deploy the intervention, and a whole-population mean would not have.

## BS-6230 Interventions that did not work, and why that matters

Null and partial results in implementation are informative in a way that null efficacy results are
not, because they identify which link in a chain was already intact.

Four from this part are worth holding together.

Patient navigation produced no benefit during the first 90 days of care, where most patients are
resolved. The benefit appeared between 91 and 365 days [@freund2014]. The intervention did not
improve routine care. It recovered patients who were being lost, which is a smaller and more
specific claim than the one usually made for it.

Medicaid expansion increased the proportion of patients from racial and ethnic minority groups
treated within 30 days by 3.62 percentage points. It changed nothing about receipt of definitive
treatment, nothing at 90 days, and nothing at minority-serving hospitals [@nguyen2021]. Removing a
payment barrier does not build capacity at the facilities where capacity is short.

Clinical breast examination screening downstaged disease substantially and did not produce a
significant mortality reduction in the prespecified analysis [@mittra2021]. Downstaging converts to
mortality benefit only where the treatment that follows is available and completed, which is the
sequential logic of the Global Breast Cancer Initiative pillars in [[BC-710]].

Protocol-assigned treatment in a randomised trial did not eliminate the outcome gap. In
{{trial:tailorx}} there was no substantial difference in 21-gene recurrence score by race, and
treatment was assigned by protocol. Black women still had a hazard ratio of 1.41 for overall
survival against White women in the whole population, with limits of 1.05 to 1.90 [@albain2021].
Standardising what is prescribed does not standardise what is experienced.

```caution
The tempting reading of these four is that implementation interventions do not work. The correct
reading is that each acted precisely where its mechanism allowed and nowhere else. Navigation acts on
loss to follow-up. Coverage acts on payment. Downstaging acts on stage. Protocol assignment acts on
prescribing. A programme that stacks all four and still finds a residual gap has not failed. It has
localised what remains, which is the only route to knowing what to build next.
```

```practice
Three habits follow from this chapter and each is available without new funding.

State the mechanism before deploying the intervention. If you cannot say which link in the pathway it
acts on, you cannot say what result would count as success.

Prespecify the subgroup analysis. An equity intervention evaluated only on its mean will report a
success that may have widened the gap it was built to close [@woodward2019].

Report the tail alongside the median for every timeliness measure. The patients who wait longest are
the ones the programme exists for, and a median conceals them [@lawson2022].
```
