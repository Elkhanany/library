## BS-5680 Communicating prognosis and uncertainty

Patients want prognostic information more often than clinicians offer it, and they want it in a
form clinicians rarely use. A median survival is a property of a distribution. A patient is a
single draw from it.

Three framings carry different information and are not interchangeable. A median says where the
middle is. A range says how wide the distribution is. A best-case and worst-case pair says what
the tails look like. Giving only the median hides exactly the part that matters to someone
deciding whether to take a trip this year.

Uncertainty should be attributed rather than merely admitted. Saying that the estimate is
uncertain is less useful than saying what would make it more certain, which is often a
characterisation that has not been done or a response that has not yet been observed.

## BS-5690 Absolute and relative benefit, and why the difference matters

This is the section the rest of the chapter depends on.

A hazard ratio is a ratio. It says nothing about how much disease there is to prevent. The same
hazard ratio produces a large absolute benefit in a high-risk population and a negligible one in a
low-risk population, and the trial that generated it was run in one of them.

Adjuvant abemaciclib in high-risk node-positive disease is the worked example. Comparing abemaciclib
with endocrine therapy against endocrine therapy alone, seven-year invasive disease-free survival was
77.4% against 70.9%, with a hazard ratio of 0.73. Seven-year overall survival was 86.8% against
85.0%, with a hazard ratio for death of 0.84 [@johnston2026os]. Both numbers come from the same
trial. The first is a 6.5 point difference in disease events. The second is a 1.8 point difference in
deaths, at seven years, in the highest-risk population enrolled.

A patient told that the drug reduces the risk of recurrence by about a quarter has been given a
true statement that will not support her decision. Now take the same evidence framed differently.
Out of a hundred women like her, roughly seven will avoid a recurrence and roughly two will avoid a
death by seven years. All one hundred will take a drug with diarrhoea and fatigue for two years. A
patient told that has been given the evidence in a form she can use.

```practice
Three habits make this reliable.

State absolute numbers with a denominator and a time horizon. Out of 100 women, over 7 years, is
a sentence a patient can hold. A percentage reduction is not.

Give the harm in the same units as the benefit. If the benefit is expressed per 100 women over
seven years, the toxicity should be too. Expressing benefit in absolute terms and toxicity in
adjectives is the commonest asymmetry in these conversations.

Say which population the trial enrolled, and whether the patient in front of you belongs to it.
Applying a high-risk trial's absolute benefit to a lower-risk patient overstates it, and the
hazard ratio hides that.
```

The same arithmetic governs extended endocrine therapy and genomic-assay-guided chemotherapy
decisions. The assays in [[BC-380]] exist precisely because relative benefit is roughly constant
while absolute benefit is not.

## BS-5700 Decision aids and preference-sensitive choices

A decision is preference-sensitive when the options differ in the kind of outcome they produce
rather than in how much of one outcome. Mastectomy against breast conservation, chemotherapy
against omission at intermediate genomic risk, and extended endocrine therapy are all of this kind.

Decision aids improve knowledge and reduce decisional conflict, and they do not reliably change
which option is chosen [@stacey2024]. That is the correct result. An aid that shifted choices in
one direction would be a persuasion tool.

The failure mode is deploying an aid where the decision is not actually preference-sensitive. Most
adjuvant trastuzumab decisions are not close calls, and presenting them as balanced misrepresents
the evidence.

## BS-5710 Explaining genomic results and variants of uncertain significance

A variant of uncertain significance is not a weak positive. It is an absence of information, and
patients and non-specialist clinicians both tend to read it as a small positive.

The two sentences that do most of the work are that the laboratory found a change it cannot
classify, and that the correct action is the one that would be taken if no test had been done.
Reclassification over time should be raised at disclosure rather than later, because it explains
why the result may change without the patient changing.

Somatic reports carry a different confusion. An alteration listed as actionable in one tumour type
may have no evidence in breast cancer, which is what tiering systems such as the ESMO scale exist
to encode [@condorelli2019]. A report that lists a drug is not a report that recommends it.

## BS-5720 Cultural humility, language access, and health literacy

Professional interpretation is a clinical intervention with an evidence base, and ad hoc
interpretation by family members produces omissions in both directions. Using a child as an
interpreter is a safety event.

Health literacy is not general literacy, and it is poorly predicted by education. Numeracy is
worse still, which is why the absolute-benefit framing in [[BS-5690]] should use natural
frequencies rather than percentages wherever possible.

Cultural humility differs from cultural competence in a way that matters practically. Competence
implies a body of knowledge about a group. Humility implies asking this patient what she wants,
which is more accurate and takes less time.

## BS-5730 Misinformation and how to respond to it

Correcting a false claim by repeating it tends to reinforce it. The more reliable approach is to
state the accurate claim, explain the mechanism, and address the underlying concern that made the
false claim attractive.

Most breast cancer misinformation attaches to real gaps. Claims about sugar, dairy or deodorant
persist partly because patients are given no account of why they developed the disease. An honest
answer, that most breast cancer has no identifiable cause in the individual, is more useful than a
correction alone.

The clinician's authority is limited here and should be spent carefully. Dismissing a patient's
source tends to end the conversation rather than the belief.
