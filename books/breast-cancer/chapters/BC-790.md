## BS-6740 Scope, and how to read the evidence tables in this chapter

This chapter is the evidence compendium for adjuvant endocrine therapy. It collects the randomised
trials that settled which agent, for how long, and with or without ovarian suppression. It ends on
adherence, because that is the largest modifiable determinant of how much of the benefit is
actually delivered.

Three neighbouring chapters carry what is deliberately absent from this one. The pharmacology of
each agent class is in [[BC-430]]. The decision architecture for early hormone receptor-positive
disease, including the genomic assays and the chemotherapy question, is in [[BC-490]]. Adjuvant
CDK4/6 inhibition is a separate decision and has its own chapter in [[BC-800]].

The table below is a filter on registry fields rather than on what a trial randomised. That
distinction does more work here than anywhere else in the book, because endocrine therapy was a
component of nearly every adjuvant trial run in this subtype for forty years. A trial enters the
table if endocrine therapy was anywhere in its design. It did not have to be the variable.

```evidence setting=early subtype=HR+/HER2-,HR+/HER2+ line=adjuvant modality=endocrine cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,status caption=Every_randomised_adjuvant_endocrine_trial_in_the_registry_for_this_subtype._Outcomes_appear_in_the_per-question_tables_that_follow.
```

Sorted by what was actually randomised, the thirty-two rows fall into six groups. Five of them are
the sections that follow: tamoxifen duration, aromatase inhibitor against tamoxifen, ovarian
suppression, extension beyond five years, and the oral degraders. The sixth group has no section
of its own, and its rows are worth naming so that none of them goes unexplained.

Four rows sit in the table because endocrine therapy was the constant rather than the variable.
{{trial:nsabp-b-20}} and {{trial:swog-8814-int-0100}} added chemotherapy to tamoxifen.
{{trial:wsg-planb}} randomised two chemotherapy regimens. Its assay-driven omission of
chemotherapy sat outside that randomisation. {{trial:wsg-adapt-hr-her2}} compared two assay-defined
groups who both received endocrine therapy alone, which is a selection rule tested for
non-inferiority rather than a randomised drug comparison. All four belong to [[BS-4300]], and none
of them randomised an endocrine decision.

They do carry one thing the endocrine sections cannot. The 21-gene recurrence score was built and
validated in cohorts whose endocrine therapy was tamoxifen alone [@paik2004; @albain2009a]. The
assay now used to place a patient on endocrine therapy without chemotherapy was calibrated against
a backbone most of those patients no longer receive. That is a measurement problem rather than a
biological one, and [[BS-4270]] is where it is argued.

{{trial:nsabp-b-14}} is the oldest row and the one the rest of the chapter stands on. It randomised
tamoxifen against placebo in node-negative receptor-positive disease [@fisher1989]. No trial since
has carried a no-treatment arm in this population. Every comparison below is therefore between two
active strategies, and the absolute benefit of endocrine therapy itself is measured nowhere in this
chapter.

Two rows randomised nothing that bears on a drug choice. {{trial:calgb9343}} randomised
radiotherapy in women aged seventy or older, with tamoxifen in both arms, and belongs to
[[BS-3760]] [@hughes2013]. {{trial:positive}} is a single-group study of a planned interruption to
attempt pregnancy, with its comparison drawn from an external cohort rather than from
randomisation [@partridge2023]. It is discussed in [[BS-5280]].

The last two rows are the within-class question, and this section rather than the next one carries
them because the filter below is built on the comparison against tamoxifen. {{trial:face}}
randomised letrozole against anastrozole in node-positive disease. {{trial:ma-27}} randomised
exemestane against anastrozole under a two-sided superiority design. Neither trial separated the
drugs on its primary endpoint. {{trial:fata-gim3}} randomised across the same three drugs inside a
larger design and also separated them on nothing [@deplacido2018]. Three randomised comparisons
agree that the choice
among the three aromatase inhibitors is not an efficacy choice. It is a tolerability and
interaction choice, and it should be made on that basis [@smith2017; @goss2013].

What none of the three can show is the manoeuvre a clinic makes after an arthralgia. Nobody
randomised a switch between aromatase inhibitors against continuing the first one. Moving a
symptomatic patient from letrozole to exemestane therefore rests on the absence of an efficacy
difference and on nothing else.

The overview table prints no result column at all, which is deliberate. Outcomes belong to the
per-question tables below, where the control arm is stated next to them. Read the arms column of
any generated table in this book before reading anything else in it.

Several of the sections below open with the question their trials were built to answer, taken from
the [Chapter Stories](stories.html) map, where the whole landscape is laid out as questions. The
division of labour is that tables enumerate and prose argues. A figure that sits in a table is not
repeated in the text unless the argument is about that figure.

## BS-6750 Tamoxifen and the trials that settled duration

```evidence setting=early line=adjuvant modality=endocrine topic=tamoxifen-duration
```

The duration question is not about how long the drug works. It is about how long its effect
persists after it stops.

The table holds two rows and one result. {{trial:atlas}} randomised continuation to ten years
against stopping at five, and reported. {{trial:attom}} sits in the registry as awaited, with no
primary duration result on its record and its primary endpoint not stated there. The ten-against-
five decision therefore rests on a single trial. The pooled reading of the two that circulates in
review articles is an arithmetic performed outside either trial's own publication.

The accounting matters more than the size of the result. ATLAS counts recurrence and breast cancer
mortality over years 5 to 14 [@davies2013atlas]. That window runs five years past the last tablet
in the continuation arm. The hazard of recurrence in this subtype does not fall away when treatment
stops [@pan2017]. A duration trial that closed its books at year ten would be reading the effect
while it was still arriving.

That is the whole case for extending anything. It is the mirror image of the late recurrence hazard
curve set out in [[BS-2880]]. A treatment whose effect arrives late is worth giving only to a woman
whose risk also arrives late.

Two features of the population constrain how the figures transfer. ATLAS randomised women already
free of disease at five years, so the estimate is conditional on surviving that far. Its breast
cancer outcomes are reported only in the women with ER-positive disease, and the remainder were
ER-negative or never assayed, which reflects practice between 1996 and 2005 [@davies2013atlas].

The price of the second five years is mostly endometrial and thrombotic. Continuing to ten years
raised endometrial cancer by a rate ratio of 1.74 and pulmonary embolus by 1.87, each against
stopping at five [@davies2013atlas]. Both are ratios of event rates. The absolute excess depends on
the baseline incidence in the woman being counselled, which a ratio does not carry.

What the table cannot show is the comparison a postmenopausal woman is actually offered. ATLAS set
continuation against stopping. Nobody randomised ten years of tamoxifen against five years of
tamoxifen followed by an aromatase inhibitor, which is the sequence the next two sections make the
default. The one analysis that does sit on the aTTom record asks instead whether an expression
assay identifies the women who gain [@bartlett2022], and [[BS-6810]] returns to it.

The patient-level overview of the first five years remains the anchor, and it established that a
carry-over effect exists at all [@ebctcg2011tam]. The earlier overviews had already shown that
tamoxifen reduces recurrence and mortality in ER-positive and ER-unknown disease, and that the
mortality reduction keeps growing to 15 years [@ebctcg1998tam; @ebctcg2005].

HER2 was once proposed as a marker of tamoxifen resistance [@pegram1998; @piccart2000; @mass2000].
In adjuvant trials the benefit of tamoxifen did not depend on HER2 or p53 status
[@berry2000; @knoop2001]. In metastatic disease, HER2-positive tumours responded less to every
endocrine treatment in a meta-analysis [@delaurentiis2005], and HER2 amplification with HER1
expression marked lower ER levels and a modestly smaller tamoxifen response [@arpino2004b].
Quantitative measurement of ErbB-2 carried prognostic and predictive information of its own
[@eppenbergercastori2001]. In TransATAC, low ER or PgR and high HER2 marked a higher recurrence risk
with either drug, and anastrozole was better than tamoxifen in every molecular subgroup
[@dowsett2008]. Tamoxifen is still the first choice in a
premenopausal woman who will not receive ovarian suppression, and in anyone for whom bone loss is
the dominant competing risk.

## BS-6760 Aromatase inhibitors against tamoxifen, and the menopausal status that decides

```story SQ-1020
```

```evidence setting=early line=adjuvant modality=endocrine topic=ai-vs-tamoxifen sort=year
```

Menopausal status is not a patient characteristic in this decision. It is a statement about where
oestrogen is being made, and therefore about which blockade will work.

In postmenopausal women an aromatase inhibitor outperforms tamoxifen. A patient-level
meta-analysis of 31,920 women found that recurrence rate ratios favoured aromatase inhibitors
while the treatments differed, at 0.70 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.64 to 0.77, and not
significantly thereafter. Ten-year breast cancer mortality across all comparisons was reduced,
with a rate ratio of 0.86 and an interval of 0.80 to 0.94 [@ebctcg2015ai]. The same analysis
priced the trade. Ten-year endometrial cancer incidence was 0.4% against 1.2% with tamoxifen, and
five-year bone fracture risk was 8.2% against 5.5%.

In premenopausal women the ovary is the dominant source, so an aromatase inhibitor alone does not
work. It requires ovarian suppression, and the comparison then changes character. A patient-level
meta-analysis of 7,030 women receiving ovarian suppression found that an aromatase inhibitor
reduced breast cancer recurrence against tamoxifen, with a rate ratio of 0.79 and an interval of
0.69 to 0.90. The absolute reduction in five-year recurrence risk was 3.2 percentage points, from
10.1% to 6.9%. Breast cancer mortality did not differ, with a rate ratio of 1.01 and an interval
of 0.82 to 1.24. Fractures were more common, at 6.4% against 5.1% [@ebctcg2022ofs]. The two
estimates do not have the same shape. Mortality fell alongside recurrence in the postmenopausal
comparison and did not under suppression, while a named toxicity rises in both. The agents
themselves are covered in [[BC-430]] and the suppression trials in [[BS-6770]].

The six randomised trials underneath the postmenopausal estimate agree on one thing, and it is
easy to lose in the detail. Wherever tamoxifen exposure is replaced by aromatase inhibition,
recurrence falls. Wherever both arms finish on an aromatase inhibitor and only the order differs,
nothing separates them. That is one finding about the class rather than six findings about six
protocols.

The six divide by control arm rather than by drug. {{trial:atac}} and {{trial:big1-98}} compared
five years of an aromatase inhibitor against five years of tamoxifen from the start. {{trial:ies}}
and {{trial:abcsg-8-arno-95}} compared a switch at two to three years against continuing tamoxifen
to the end of five. {{trial:team}} and {{trial:fata-gim3}} compared starting with an aromatase
inhibitor against starting with tamoxifen and switching later. Three different questions,
habitually read as one.

The component reports are the primary evidence under the meta-analysis. ATAC favoured anastrozole
for recurrence at its first analysis, at the completion of treatment and at 100 months
[@baum2002; @howell0000; @forbes2008]. BIG 1-98 first reported in 2005 [@thrlimann2005]. Its
sequencing comparison found that neither sequence improved disease-free survival over letrozole
alone [@mouridsen2009]. IES reported its first switch result in 2004 [@coombes2004]. ARNO 95 found
improved overall survival after switching to anastrozole [@kaufmann2007], and the Italian ITA trial
improved event-free and recurrence-free survival [@boccardo2005; @boccardo2006]. A meta-analysis of
the anastrozole switch trials found an overall survival benefit [@jonat2006]. A patient-level
meta-analysis of the aromatase inhibitor trials found lower recurrence whether the drug was given
from the start or after two to three years of tamoxifen [@dowsett2010].

The same trials measured what the benefit costs. In ATAC, patients with normal bone density at the
start did not appear to need monitoring beyond that recommended for healthy postmenopausal women
[@eastell2008]. Endometrial abnormalities were fewer with anastrozole [@duffy2006]. Overall quality of
life was similar, with different symptom profiles [@fallowfield2004]. Prior chemotherapy did not
change the relative benefit [@buzdar2006]. Tamoxifen lowered anastrozole blood levels without
affecting oestradiol suppression [@dowsett2001]. In BIG 1-98, letrozole increased bone fractures
[@rabaglio2009], and cardiovascular events were infrequent and differed between the arms
[@mouridsen2007].

TEAM constrains practice more than the others and is cited least. Its null result means that
starting with an aromatase inhibitor is not superior to starting with tamoxifen and switching
later [@vandevelde2011]. FATA-GIM3 reached the same place from a six-arm design [@deplacido2018].
The case for starting with an aromatase inhibitor therefore rests on tolerability and on the
gynaecological and thromboembolic profile. It does not rest on efficacy, because the two trials
built to test the order found none.

The decision rests on BIG 1-98. It was double-blind. It randomised the whole five years rather
than a switch part-way. It is also the only one of the six whose intention-to-treat overall
survival comparison favoured the aromatase inhibitor. ATAC, with ten years of follow-up, did not
reproduce that [@cuzick2010]. The divergence is not anastrozole against letrozole, because FACE
randomised those two head to head and separated them on nothing. It is blinding, crossover and
duration of follow-up.

BIG 1-98 also shows how much of its own survival claim is model-dependent. After the 2005 analysis,
patients still on tamoxifen were unblinded and offered letrozole. A censoring-weighted analysis
that removes those crossovers moves the overall survival estimate further in favour of letrozole
than the intention-to-treat estimate in the table [@regan2011]. Both are correct estimates of
different quantities. Only the intention-to-treat estimate describes the policy that was actually
studied.

The two switch trials differ from each other in a way the table hides. IES randomised inside a
single protocol with a fixed five-year total and reported overall survival [@coombes2007]. The
ABCSG-8 and ARNO 95 row is a combined analysis of two trials, reporting event-free survival with
no overall survival in the pooled paper [@jakesz0000]. Their agreement is real. It is agreement
between one trial and one pooling, not between two replications.

The measurement point is the one that causes harm in clinic. Menopausal status after chemotherapy
is inferred from amenorrhoea and from gonadotrophin and oestradiol assays. Amenorrhoea is not
menopause. An aromatase inhibitor given to a woman with recoverable ovarian function lowers
oestrogen, raises gonadotrophins and can restart the ovary. In an audit of 45 women with
chemotherapy-induced amenorrhoea who started an aromatase inhibitor, 27% showed a return of ovarian
function, and the authors advised caution with sensitive oestradiol monitoring [@smith2006]. Markers of ovarian reserve do not settle it either. Chemotherapy
lowers Mullerian inhibiting substance rapidly, and the fall did not predict later menstrual function
[@yu2010].

Three things the table cannot show. Every one of the six enrolled postmenopausal women, so none of
it transfers to a premenopausal woman who is not suppressed. No trial randomised the length of the
tamoxifen lead-in, so the two to three years the switch trials used is a protocol convention rather
than an optimum. And bone is not a column here, although the meta-analysis above prices recurrence,
mortality and fracture together.

## BS-6770 Ovarian function suppression and who benefits

```story SQ-0010
```

```evidence setting=early line=adjuvant modality=endocrine topic=ovarian-suppression sort=year cols=acronym,n,population,arms,result,os caption=The_randomised_trials_of_ovarian_suppression._Read_the_experimental-versus-control_column_first,_because_these_four_ask_three_different_questions.
```

Four rows, three questions, and only two of these trials randomised suppression at all.
{{trial:soft}} set tamoxifen plus suppression, and exemestane plus suppression, against tamoxifen
alone. {{trial:astrra}} set two years of goserelin added to tamoxifen against tamoxifen alone.
{{trial:soft-text}} and {{trial:abcsg12}} gave a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist to
everybody and randomised the partner drug instead. A reader who takes all four as tests of ovarian
suppression has read two of them backwards.

Where the four agree is narrower than it looks. Adding suppression to tamoxifen lowers recurrence in
premenopausal hormone receptor-positive disease, and in ASTRRA it also moved overall survival.
Upgrading the partner drug on top of suppression is a separate claim, and the two trials that tested
it disagree.

The decision rests on SOFT, because it is the only trial here that randomised the addition against
the thing a clinic would otherwise give, which is five years of tamoxifen. Its population was not
pre-selected on recovered ovarian function, which is what makes the row load-bearing. Its first
report did not find the addition significant [@francis2015soft]. The eight-year report reversed that
reading on disease-free and overall survival alike [@francis2018], and the twelve-year report held
it [@francis2022]. The final report, at a median of fifteen years, kept the reduction in breast
cancer recurrence and lost the survival difference [@francis2026]. No biology changed between the
first report and the second. The events needed time to accrue, which is the structural problem with
adjuvant endpoints set out in [[BS-6330]].

ASTRRA asks the same addition question in a narrower population. It enrolled women aged 45 or
younger who had already had chemotherapy and remained premenopausal or resumed ovarian function,
confirmed every six months by follicle-stimulating hormone and bleeding history [@kim2020astrra].
Two years of goserelin improved disease-free survival, and the separation persisted at eight years
[@baek2023]. That population is a prospective enrichment of precisely the SOFT stratum in which
suppression looked worthwhile, so its agreement with SOFT is close to circular. What it adds is
that the effect survives being looked for on purpose. Its overall survival comparison did reach
significance, and the interval around that estimate is wide enough that the point estimate should
not be quoted to a patient as a survival benefit.

On the partner question the two trials disagree, and the disagreement is not about the drugs.
SOFT/TEXT found exemestane better than tamoxifen on disease-free survival and not on overall
survival, at twelve years and again at fifteen [@pagani2023; @francis2026]. ABCSG-12 found
anastrozole no better than tamoxifen. Both held suppression constant in every arm, so that is not
what divides them. Four other things do. ABCSG-12 gave three years of treatment rather than five. It
used anastrozole rather than exemestane. It enrolled a largely chemotherapy-naive and lower-risk
population, and its primary report came before most late events in this subtype have occurred
[@gnant2009]. And its final analysis was powered around a second randomisation, to zoledronic acid,
which belongs to the adjuvant bisphosphonate argument rather than to this one [@gnant2014]. Its
endocrine comparison is a marginal one inside a bone-agent trial, and the patient-level
meta-analysis that pools the field averages that result into the total [@ebctcg2022ofs]. Reading the
two rows as a verdict on anastrozole against exemestane is the error the arms column exists to
prevent.

The subgroups are where the decision lives, and the table carries only the HER2-negative hazard
ratio. In HER2-negative disease, 86.0% of the joint population, the 15-year overall survival gain
from exemestane over tamoxifen, both with suppression, was larger in women who received
chemotherapy. It was 3.9 percentage points in SOFT and 2.5 in TEXT. Young age and high grade also
marked a larger gain [@francis2026]. The gain tracks baseline risk rather than the drug, so a
single average for this comparison is not usable in clinic.

One inversion inside SOFT is worth more attention than it gets. Exemestane with suppression gave the
best eight-year disease-free survival of the three arms and did not give the best eight-year overall
survival [@francis2018]. The arm that beat tamoxifen alone on survival was tamoxifen with
suppression. The trial was not powered to rank the two suppression arms against each other on
survival. Read the recurrence ordering as a survival ordering and you are reading something the
design did not test. The final report removed the inversion without settling the ranking, because no
arm differed significantly from tamoxifen alone on overall survival [@francis2026].

Three things nobody randomised are missing from every row, and they are the part of the decision
that is hardest in clinic. Duration is one, at two years in ASTRRA, three in ABCSG-12 and five in
SOFT and TEXT, never varied inside a trial. Method is the second. ABCSG-12 and ASTRRA specified a
gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist, and no trial here set one method against another, so
oophorectomy and radiation ablation are inferred rather than tested. The third is confirmation of
suppression. ASTRRA measured ovarian function repeatedly and the others did not, which means the
treatment being compared is biochemically the same drug and not reliably the same state.
Discontinuation is not a column either, although it is what decides how much of any of this is
delivered.

```caution
Every trial in this table enrolled premenopausal women. Where a benefit was found, it concentrated
in those who were young, node-positive, high grade or chemotherapy-treated. That gradient comes
from subgroup analysis and not from randomised allocation. Being premenopausal is the entry
criterion, not the indication.

A woman with a small grade 1 node-negative tumour belongs to the enrolled population and not to the
group that gained. Saying the absolute number out loud before prescribing is the discipline this
section asks for.
```

```practice
Suppression is a risk-stratified decision, not a menopausal-status decision. Receipt of
chemotherapy, young age and high grade mark the subgroups with the larger fifteen-year survival gain
[@francis2026].

In a woman with a small, grade 1, node-negative tumour the absolute gain sits at the bottom of
that range and the toxicity is not proportionally smaller. Quote the absolute difference before
prescribing rather than the hazard ratio.

Remaining premenopausal after chemotherapy is itself a selection marker, because it identifies the
women in whom ovarian function was never removed [@francis2018].

If suppression is used, the aromatase inhibitor is the better partner on recurrence and has not been
shown to be better on survival [@ebctcg2022ofs; @francis2026]. Fractures rise, so bone management
starts at the same visit.
```

## BS-6780 Extended adjuvant therapy and the shape of the late benefit

```story SQ-0020
```

```evidence setting=early line=adjuvant modality=endocrine topic=extended-endocrine sort=year
```

The case for extension is a statement about hazard. Distant recurrence in this subtype accrues at
a roughly constant rate from year 5 to year 20 [@pan2017]. The size of that residual risk by
tumour size and nodal status is tabulated in [[BS-2880]] and [[BS-4350]]. Extension is therefore a
dormancy intervention rather than consolidation of a falling risk, and [[BC-330]] is where that
argument is made properly.

One thing is worth settling before the rows are read. Four of the eight report overall survival, and
none of those four shows an advantage for the longer arm. The other four do not report it at all.
An extension decision is a recurrence decision, and it should be described to a patient as one.

The eight look inconsistent until they are sorted by control arm, which is the axis that decides
what each can answer. {{trial:ma17}}, {{trial:ma17r}} and {{trial:nsabp-b42}} randomised further
treatment against placebo, and they are the only three that bear on whether to extend at all.
{{trial:abcsg16}}, {{trial:data}}, {{trial:gim4}} and {{trial:ideal}} randomised a longer extension
against a shorter one, so they answer how long rather than whether. {{trial:sole}} answers neither,
because both of its arms were extended.

The three placebo-controlled trials answer whether to extend at all, and they answer it in both
sequences. MA.17 extended letrozole after tamoxifen. MA.17R extended it after an aromatase
inhibitor, and both separated from placebo on disease-free survival [@goss2003; @goss2016]. NSABP
B-42 asked the same question in the same place as MA.17R and did not clear its own significance
threshold [@mamounas2019]. Extending after an aromatase inhibitor is therefore not the weaker
version of the question, which is how it is usually read.

The four duration-against-duration trials are where the field reads a disagreement that may not be
there. DATA and GIM4 asked the same thing, a longer aromatase inhibitor course after two to three
years of tamoxifen, and returned disease-free survival hazard ratios of 0.79 and 0.78. Only GIM4
crossed significance, and it read out at a median follow-up of 11.7 years against DATA's five-year
adapted endpoint [@tjanheijnen2017; @delmastro2021]. What separates a positive extension trial from
a null one here is events and follow-up rather than drug or sequence. IDEAL admitted women after
any five years of endocrine therapy, so each of its arms mixes the two sequences [@blok2018].

NSABP B-42 is where a convention rather than a biology decided the reported verdict. Its
disease-free survival p value fell below 0.05 and above the threshold its own design had
prespecified, so the trial is recorded as negative [@mamounas2019]. The ten-year update reached
significance on the same endpoint [@mamounas2023]. Nothing about letrozole changed between those
reports. An interim-analysis alpha changed what the same comparison was permitted to be called,
which is worth knowing before quoting either one.

ABCSG-16 is the only row that priced the longer course in fractures as a named secondary endpoint
rather than as an adverse event count [@gnant2021]. Its two arms differed only in how many further
years of anastrozole were given. The fracture excess is therefore attributable to duration and not
to starting the drug, which is the one place in this table where the cost of extension is reported
as an outcome.

SOLE answers a question the others do not ask. It gave letrozole intermittently against
continuously, on the hypothesis that a break restores sensitivity, and found no difference at five
years or at seven [@colleoni2017; @jerusalem2021]. How to give an extension is separate from
whether to give one. A null schedule trial is not evidence about duration.

The reading that fits is about what the control arm was doing, not about the molecule or the
sequence. Against no further treatment, the longer arm lowered recurrence in MA.17 and MA.17R, in
neither case with a survival difference. Against a shorter further course, three of the four
trials did not separate. MA.17R is the awkward row for any tidy account of diminishing returns.
Its patients had already had about five years of an aromatase inhibitor, and most had taken
tamoxifen before that.

```caution
MA.17 is quoted as the trial that established extension, and its estimate is more fragile than
that reputation suggests. It was stopped at its first interim analysis on short
follow-up, and the placebo group was then unblinded and offered letrozole [@goss2003; @ingle2008].
Its long-term estimate compares five further years against a shorter delayed course, not against
none.

Collapsing this table into one message is the commonest error in this literature. The general form
of that error, and how to spot it in a report whose comparator changed mid-trial, is in
[[BS-6370]].
```

Its later reports show the same benefit through the crossover. The updated analysis at a median of 30
months confirmed better disease-free and distant disease-free survival with letrozole
[@goss2005]. Analyses adjusted for the crossover favoured letrozole for disease-free, distant
disease-free and overall survival, with an overall survival hazard ratio of 0.61 by one method and
0.76 by another [@jin2012]. In a non-randomised cohort analysis, women who started letrozole after a
gap since tamoxifen also benefited [@goss2008]. The price was a modest fall in bone density
[@perez2006] and more vasomotor symptoms, without an adverse effect on overall quality of life
[@whelan2005]. ABCSG-6a gave three years of anastrozole after five of tamoxifen and reduced
recurrence against no further treatment [@jakesz2007].

The patient-level meta-analysis of twelve aromatase inhibitor extension trials in 22,031
postmenopausal women who had already completed at least five years of endocrine therapy now prices
both sides [@ebctcg2025]. Allocation to further aromatase inhibition reduced recurrence, with a
rate ratio of 0.73 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.67 to 0.80. What a clinic needs from it is
the absolute framing rather than the proportional one, and that arithmetic is in [[BS-5690]].

The subset that matters most in current practice is the one already treated with an aromatase
inhibitor. There, five further years reduced recurrence with a rate ratio of 0.71, taking the risk
from year 5 to year 15 from 15.2% to 11.6%. Distant recurrence fell with a rate ratio of 0.73,
from 8.6% to 6.6%. Breast cancer mortality fell non-significantly, with a rate ratio of 0.90, an
interval of 0.70 to 1.15 and a p value of 0.40, from 5.0% to 4.4% [@ebctcg2025].

Because the proportional reduction was similar across tumour characteristics, the absolute benefit
scales with baseline risk. Ten years against five years took node-positive risk from 20.1% to
16.3% and node-negative risk from 11.8% to 9.1% [@ebctcg2025]. Five-year fracture risk rose with a
rate ratio of 1.35, from 3.4% to 4.6%.

One component of the extension benefit is not recurrence at all. Contralateral and second primary
breast cancers fall with continued aromatase inhibition, and IDEAL isolates that effect because its
two arms differed only in duration [@blok2018]. MA.17R reports the same direction on annual
contralateral incidence [@goss2016]. Preventing a new primary is a real benefit. It is not the
same achievement as preventing relapse, and [[BS-3840]] sets out why the distinction matters when
quoting a disease-free survival difference.

Two populations are missing from every row. No extension trial enrolled premenopausal women, so
duration in a woman treated with tamoxifen and suppression is unstudied. And every placebo-
controlled trial enrolled women who had already tolerated five years, which selects for adherence
and against the patients most likely to stop. No trial in the table allocated extension on a
molecular signal, which is the gap [[BS-6810]] returns to.

```practice
Extended therapy is a recurrence decision with an unproven mortality effect. Present it that way
rather than as a survival intervention [@ebctcg2025].

Anchor the offer to nodal status, because the proportional benefit is flat and the absolute
benefit is not. Quote the node-positive and node-negative recurrence risks between years 5 and 15
rather than the rate ratio [@ebctcg2025].

Put the fracture risk in the same sentence as the recurrence risk. The same analysis prices both,
and the five-year fracture rate is what a patient will weigh against the recurrence she cannot
see [@ebctcg2025].

A patient who has tolerated five years badly is unlikely to tolerate ten. Non-adherence in these
placebo-controlled trials ran at 39.0% on further treatment and 37.6% on placebo [@ebctcg2025],
which is the honest baseline for what extension means in practice.
```

## BS-4350 Late recurrence risk and surveillance implications

This subtype recurs on a timescale that outlasts most follow-up schedules and most trials. The
annual hazard of recurrence does not fall to zero after five years, and the pattern across 24
years of follow-up is set out in [@colleoni2016] and [@saphner1996].

The size of that residual risk is now precisely quantified. Among 62,923 women with oestrogen
receptor-positive disease who were disease-free after five years of scheduled endocrine therapy,
recurrences occurred at a steady rate from year 5 to year 20. Distant recurrence risk over that
window was 13% for T1N0 disease, 20% for T1N1-3, 34% for T1N4-9, 19% for T2N0, 26% for T2N1-3 and
41% for T2N4-9 [@pan2017].

Grade added moderately within a stage stratum. For T1N0 disease the risk over years 5 to 20 was
10% for low-grade, 13% for moderate-grade and 17% for high-grade tumours [@pan2017]. Progesterone
receptor and HER2 status were not predictive of late distant recurrence in that dataset.

Two implications follow, and only one of them is comfortable.

The comfortable one is that the original pathology report retains predictive power for two
decades. Size and nodal status are not superseded by anything currently in routine use.

The uncomfortable one is that no surveillance strategy has been shown to convert that knowledge
into benefit. Detecting asymptomatic distant recurrence earlier has not improved survival, and the
evidence against surveillance intensity is in [[BC-650]]. Circulating tumour DNA detects molecular
relapse before imaging does, and whether acting on that lead time helps is unresolved and is the
subject of [[BC-390]]. Late recurrence is a dormancy problem, and its biology is in [[BC-330]].

```interplay target=BC-350
Heterogeneity in this subtype is mostly temporal rather than spatial, which is why the chapter
reads as a series of duration decisions. The adjuvant setting applies years of selective pressure
to a population that cannot be sampled. What emerges at year eight has been shaped by that
pressure exactly as the metastatic sequences in [[BS-2490]] are. Extended endocrine therapy and
adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition are not simply longer treatment. They are longer selection, and the
ESR1 and RB1 escape routes that define the metastatic setting [[BS-2500]] are being selected for
during the years in which the patient is called disease-free.
```

## BS-6790 Oral selective estrogen receptor degraders in the adjuvant setting

```story SQ-1030
```

```evidence setting=early line=adjuvant modality=endocrine topic=adjuvant-serd
```

No adjuvant oral selective estrogen receptor degrader has reported. The table carries three rows
and no outcome behind any of them. Everything in this section is a hypothesis with a trial design
attached to it.

The rationale was transposed from advanced disease, where the target is an ESR1 ligand-binding
domain mutation conferring ligand-independent receptor activity. The biology is in [[BC-140]] and
the agents are in [[BS-3830]]. The transposition has a problem that is rarely stated. ESR1
mutations are selected by aromatase inhibitor exposure and are uncommon at diagnosis [[BS-2490]].
In the adjuvant setting there is no measurable tumour, and usually no mutation to target.

The adjuvant hypothesis therefore cannot be that a degrader treats a mutant receptor. It has to be
that more complete receptor blockade stops resistance from forming. That is a different claim and
it has never been tested in any setting.

The CAMBRIA pair tests two versions of it, split by when the degrader is given rather than by which
degrader it is. {{trial:cambria1}} switches patients to camizestrant after two to five years of
standard endocrine therapy, which is the late-selection version. {{trial:cambria2}} replaces
standard endocrine therapy from the start, which is the complete-blockade version.
{{trial:lidera}} puts a second molecule into the same question, giredestrant against standard
adjuvant endocrine therapy. Its registry record does not yet fix a primary endpoint, so what it
will be judged on is not settled here. Two agents behaving the same way would say something about
the strategy. One of each would say something about the molecules.

Two design features deserve attention now rather than at readout. Invasive breast cancer-free
survival counts second primaries, so part of any positive result will be prevention rather than
recurrence control, exactly as in the extension trials above. And CAMBRIA-1 admits patients who
received a CDK4/6 inhibitor, while CAMBRIA-2 permits abemaciclib in both arms for its first two
years [@hamilton2025]. The comparator in each is current practice rather than endocrine
monotherapy.

What no trial in the class does is select. None of the three randomises on circulating tumour DNA,
on ESR1 status or on any measure of residual disease. A positive trial would therefore license a
drug for a population in which most patients were never going to relapse. The interception
argument is in [[BC-390]] and [[BS-2890]].

```caution
The prior from advanced disease is weaker than the enthusiasm suggests. In ESR1 wild-type
populations the oral degraders have produced modest effects against standard endocrine therapy. The
progression-free survival figures that support that statement are in [[BS-3830]].

Reading a positive metastatic result as support for adjuvant use is the error that four adjuvant
CDK4/6 trials made visible. Two were positive and two were negative within one class, and
[[BC-800]] works through why.
```

## BS-6800 Adherence, toxicity, and the gap between prescribed and taken

Every hazard ratio in this chapter is an intention-to-treat estimate over a population in which a
large minority stopped taking the drug. That is not a footnote to the effect size. It is part of
what the effect size means, and the arithmetic follows from it.

In a cohort of 8,769 women with stage I to III hormone-sensitive breast cancer who filled at least
one prescription, 32% had discontinued endocrine therapy by 4.5 years. Among those who continued,
72% were fully adherent by a medication possession ratio of 80% or more. Combining the two, 49%
took adjuvant endocrine therapy for the full duration on the intended schedule. Women under 40 had
the highest discontinuation risk, with a hazard ratio of 1.51 and a 95% confidence interval of
1.23 to 1.85 [@hershman2010].

Compare that against the size of the other lever a clinician controls. Choosing an aromatase
inhibitor over tamoxifen in a postmenopausal woman changes recurrence by a few percentage points
over a decade. Not taking the tablet removes the entire effect of the drug. Clinic time is spent
almost entirely on the smaller lever.

The randomised evidence says most of that loss is not pharmacological. In the placebo-controlled
extended-therapy trials pooled by the EBCTCG, non-adherence was 39.0% on further aromatase
inhibition and 37.6% on placebo [@ebctcg2025]. Almost as many women stopped an inert tablet as
stopped an active one, which says that the burden is the regimen as much as the pharmacology.
Symptom management therefore has a ceiling, and the rest of the problem is structural.

The toxicities that drive this are quantified elsewhere in the chapter. Fractures rise with
aromatase inhibition in postmenopausal women [@ebctcg2015ai] and with ovarian suppression in
premenopausal women [@ebctcg2022ofs]. Endometrial cancer and thrombosis rise with tamoxifen
[@ebctcg2015ai]. Arthralgia and vasomotor symptoms are the commonest reasons given for stopping,
and the symptom burden is developed in [[BC-430]] and [[BC-650]].

Measurement matters here more than usual, because the three common methods disagree by design.
Self-report overestimates, and most of all when the prescriber is the one asking the question.
Pharmacy refill measures collection from a dispensary rather than ingestion. Serum tamoxifen or
endoxifen, and serum oestradiol during aromatase inhibition, measure exposure directly and are
almost never used. A trial reporting adherence by refill and a clinic reporting it by self-report
are not describing the same quantity, and neither is wrong.

Dose is the one pharmacological lever with randomised evidence behind it. {{trial:tam01}} tested 5
mg of tamoxifen daily against placebo and reported durable benefit at ten years [@lazzeroni2023].
That trial enrolled breast intraepithelial neoplasia rather than invasive disease. Using low-dose
tamoxifen to rescue an intolerant patient with invasive cancer is an extrapolation, and it should
be described to her as one.

```practice
Ask about adherence at every visit, and ask in a form that permits a true answer. Roughly half of
patients do not complete the prescribed course [@hershman2010].

Flag women under 40 as the highest-risk group for discontinuation and follow them accordingly
[@hershman2010].

Set the expectation at the first visit rather than at the first symptom. Say that most women get
vasomotor or musculoskeletal symptoms, that these usually settle, and that stopping is the outcome
you are trying to prevent.

Treat the symptom before shortening the course. Switching between aromatase inhibitors, adding a
vasomotor agent, or moving the dose to bedtime all leave the patient on treatment. Stopping does
not.

Quantify the loss out loud when a patient asks to stop early. A woman stopping at year two has
taken about two fifths of the intended course, and she is entitled to know roughly what that costs
in the absolute framing of [[BS-5690]].

Assess bone density before and during aromatase inhibition or ovarian suppression, because
fractures rise with both [@ebctcg2015ai; @ebctcg2022ofs]. The management belongs in the same visit
and is in [[BS-5570]].

Record which adherence measure you used. A refill ratio and a patient's answer are different
numbers, and they will disagree.

When quoting a benefit from any trial in this chapter, remember it was achieved under the same
adherence that the patient in front of you will have. The number is not an estimate of perfect
compliance and should not be presented as one.
```

## BS-6810 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

```story SQ-1060
```

```evidence setting=early modality=endocrine,cdk4-6 status=ongoing
```

The filter above asks for unreported trials in early disease with an endocrine or cell-cycle
component. It returns three adjuvant phase 3 trials and one neoadjuvant phase 2 with no control
arm. Read the line and phase columns before treating the four as one programme.

Five questions are open, and only two of them have a trial that will answer them.

Whether an oral degrader beats standard endocrine therapy in the adjuvant setting is being tested
in two positions at once. {{trial:cambria1}} and {{trial:cambria2}} could disagree. A split result
would be more informative than two concordant ones. A positive switch trial with a negative upfront
trial would say the benefit lies in late blockade, after resistance has begun to form.

Whether extension can be targeted rather than offered broadly has been tested only
retrospectively. The Breast Cancer Index began as a two-gene expression ratio, HOXB13 to IL17BR,
associated with outcome under tamoxifen [@ma2004]. In TransATAC its linear model was the only one of three
tests prognostic for both early and late distant recurrence [@sgroi2013]. In tumours from {{trial:ideal}},
a high ratio predicted benefit from five rather than two and a half further years of letrozole,
with a hazard ratio of 0.42, and a low ratio did not, at 0.95 [@noordhoek2021]. The interaction p
value was 0.045. The same kind of analysis was run in aTTom. In its first report, from 583
node-positive patients, a high ratio predicted benefit from ten rather than five years of tamoxifen,
with a hazard ratio of 0.35 [@bartlett2019]. A low ratio did not, at 1.07. The final analysis on
the aTTom record found the same split in its node-positive patients [@bartlett2022]. These are
predictive claims made inside trials randomised for something else, which is where the 21-gene score
stood before {{trial:tailorx}} [[BS-4300]]. Nobody has randomised extended endocrine therapy on a
molecular signal, and circulating tumour DNA is the other obvious candidate. The assay performance
and the interception problem are in [[BC-390]] and [[BS-2890]].

Duration in premenopausal women is close to unstudied. Every aromatase inhibitor extension trial in
this chapter required a postmenopausal population. {{trial:atlas}} carried no such requirement, and
it tested tamoxifen rather than the suppression-based regimens these women now receive.

The duration of ovarian suppression has never been compared head to head in a randomised trial.
{{trial:astrra}} used two years and {{trial:soft}} used five. The question is not academic,
because suppression is the least tolerated component of the regimen in young women [[BC-610]].

Whether cell cycle inhibition changes the endocrine question is being approached from two
directions here. {{trial:adaptcycle}} asks whether ribociclib with an aromatase inhibitor can
replace chemotherapy in intermediate-risk disease, with entry decided by a recurrence score and by
Ki-67 measured after short preoperative endocrine therapy [@hamann2025]. A positive result would
move part of the chemotherapy decision in [[BS-4300]] into the endocrine column. That is a larger
change than any degrader result would produce.

Its entry rule is also the clearest example in this chapter of a measurement standing in for a
biology. Endocrine response is defined by a Ki-67 threshold, scored locally in some centres and
centrally in others, and the two readings do not agree in every patient [@hamann2025]. Allocation
therefore depends partly on which laboratory read the slide. The scoring conventions are set out
in [[BS-6930]].

{{trial:neoletrib}} is the odd row and the one worth keeping. It is a single-group neoadjuvant
study of letrozole with ribociclib, with serial biopsy and single-cell sequencing during treatment
[@fjermeros2024neoletrib]. It will not settle a treatment question and is not built to. It is the
only study in this chapter that samples a tumour while endocrine pressure is being applied, which
is the measurement the adjuvant setting can never make. The window designs it belongs among are in
[[BS-6940]].

```interplay target=BC-350
Adjuvant endocrine therapy applies selective pressure for five to ten years to a population that
cannot be sampled. That is the heterogeneity argument of Part VI running in slow motion. What
emerges at year eight has been shaped by the agent chosen at year zero. The ESR1 mutations that
define endocrine resistance in metastatic disease [[BS-2500]] are selected by exactly the aromatase
inhibitor exposure this chapter recommends. The oral degrader trials in [[BS-6790]] are the first
attempt to act on that selection before it has an observable output. They will be reported as
duration trials. They are better understood as experiments on which escape route the adjuvant years
build.
```
