## BS-0120 Global incidence, mortality, and prevalence

In the 2022 estimates, cancer of the female breast accounted for 11.6% of all new cancer cases and
6.9% of all cancer deaths worldwide [@bray2024]. The gap between those two proportions is the
chapter in one line. Incidence is concentrated where survival is best.

Estimates from the GLOBOCAN database put the burden at over 2.3 million new cases and 685000 deaths
in women in 2020 [@arnold2022]. Demographic projection alone, with no change in age-specific rates,
takes that to over 3 million cases and 1 million deaths a year by 2040.

Prevalence follows from the same arithmetic and is often left out. High incidence combined with good
survival produces a large population living with a diagnosis, sometimes for decades. Those patients
consume follow-up, survivorship care and surveillance imaging, and they are invisible in any figure
that reports only cases and deaths.

The single most consequential number in this chapter is the spread in survival. World Health
Organization estimates of five-year net survival for women diagnosed between 2017 and 2021 give a
regional median of 39.1% in the African Region against 88.5% in the Region of the Americas
[@girardi2026]. That gap is not a gap in tumour biology. It is the argument that [[BC-710]] exists
to answer.

## BS-0130 Age, birth cohort, and geographic patterns

Incidence rises steeply with age and then flattens, and the flattening is informative. A single
exponential age curve would suggest one accumulating process. The observed shape is more consistent
with two overlapping disease groups separated around the menopause, which is developed in
[[BC-170]] and [[BC-610]].

Geography separates incidence from mortality cleanly. Age-standardized incidence ranges from under
40 per 100000 women in some Asian and African countries to over 80 per 100000 in Australia and New
Zealand, North America and parts of Europe [@arnold2022]. Variation in mortality is much narrower.
Countries in epidemiologic transition therefore carry a share of deaths out of proportion to their
share of cases.

Cohort and period effects are different things and are routinely conflated. A period effect shifts
rates in every age group at the same calendar time, which is what the introduction of screening
looks like. A cohort effect follows a birth cohort through life, which is what a change in
reproductive pattern or childhood exposure looks like. Formal age-period-cohort analysis of United
States incidence in women aged 20 to 49 years found evidence of both [@xu2024].

Reading a rising national curve as a change in exposure requires ruling out the period effect first.
That is the subject of the next section.

## BS-0140 Reading incidence and mortality trends without confusing screening for biology

Four artefacts sit between a screening programme and an incidence curve, and all four push in the
same direction. Lead time moves the diagnosis earlier without moving the death. Length bias enriches
the screen-detected group for slow-growing tumours. Stage migration reassigns patients to better
stages without changing anyone's outcome. Overdiagnosis adds cancers that would never have surfaced.

The effect on incidence is unambiguous. In the United States, incidence rose by 1% a year between
2012 and 2021, and the rise was largely confined to localized-stage and hormone receptor positive
disease [@giaquinto2024]. The national cancer statistics for 2024 place breast among the cancers
whose incidence is still rising, by 0.6% to 1% a year during 2015 to 2019 [@siegel2024]. An increase
concentrated in the stage and subtype that screening finds most easily is a detection signal until
proven otherwise.

Mortality is the harder question, because both screening and treatment improved together. Modelling
work has tried to separate them twice, a generation apart. Seven independent models covering 1975 to
2000 attributed between 28% and 65% of the mortality reduction to screening, with a median of 46%,
and the remainder to adjuvant treatment [@berry2005]. Four models covering 1975 to 2019 estimated a
58% reduction in breast cancer mortality overall [@caswelljin2024]. Of that reduction, 25% was
associated with screening, 47% with treatment of stage I to III disease and 29% with treatment of
metastatic disease.

```caution
The wide spread across models is the finding, not a nuisance to be averaged away. These are not
measurements of the contribution of screening. They are outputs of models that differ in their
assumptions about the natural history of untreated disease. Quoting a single percentage without the
range misrepresents how well this is known.
```

A tumour-size analysis makes the same point from observed data. After screening was introduced, the
rate of large tumours fell by 30 cases per 100000 women while the rate of small tumours rose by 162
per 100000 [@welch2016]. If the underlying disease burden was stable, most of those additional small
tumours were never going to become large ones.

## BS-0150 Reproductive, hormonal, metabolic, and environmental determinants

The established determinants act through cumulative exposure of breast epithelium to oestrogen, and
the pattern of associations fits that mechanism closely. Circulating sex hormone concentrations in
postmenopausal women are associated with subsequent breast cancer risk [@key2002].

Childbirth is the instructive case, because its effect reverses with time. In a pooled analysis of
15 prospective cohorts, risk in parous women peaked about five years after delivery, with a hazard
ratio of 1.80 against nulliparous women [@nichols2019]. It then declined, and crossed below 1.0 at
about 24 years after birth. The pattern was driven by oestrogen receptor positive disease. Parity is protective
in the long run and transiently the opposite, and a study with short follow-up will find whichever
half it happened to observe.

Exogenous hormones act in the same direction. In an individual-participant meta-analysis, current
users in years 5 to 14 of oestrogen with progestagen had a relative risk of 2.08 against never users
[@cghfbc2019]. For current users of oestrogen alone over the same interval the relative risk was
1.33. Some excess risk persisted for more than ten years after stopping.

Metabolic and behavioural exposures are weaker per person and large in aggregate. Body mass index is
associated with the incidence of several cancers including breast cancer [@bhaskaran2014]. A
randomized dietary modification intervention was followed by reduced breast cancer mortality on
long-term follow-up [@chlebowski2020].

Not every hypothesis survives. Three prospective studies with a meta-analysis of published work
found no substantial association between night shift work and breast cancer incidence
[@travis2016]. That null result is worth as much space as a positive one and usually gets less.

## BS-0160 The rise of young-onset disease

Incidence below age 50 is rising, and the rise is not obviously a detection artefact. In the United
States, incidence increased by 1.4% a year in women under 50 and by 0.7% a year in women aged 50 and
over between 2012 and 2021 [@giaquinto2024]. That steeper rise in the younger group was significant
only among White women, which limits how far the argument below can be carried. Most organized
screening does not begin before 40, so a faster rise in the unscreened age group cannot be
attributed to screening in the ordinary way.

A registry analysis of 217815 women aged 20 to 49 years diagnosed between 2000 and 2019 gives the
detail [@xu2024]. Overall incidence rose with an average annual percent change of 0.79. The increase
was confined to receptor positive disease, with an average annual percent change of 2.72 for
tumours positive for both oestrogen and progesterone receptors. Rates of tumours negative for both
receptors did not increase.

Three explanations compete and they are not mutually exclusive. Exposure may have changed, through
later first birth, lower parity, rising body mass index and other cohort-linked shifts. Detection
may have changed, through more imaging in symptomatic young women. Biology may have changed in ways
nobody has identified.

The receptor-specific pattern favours the exposure account, because the hormonally driven subtype is
the one that is rising. It does not settle it. Management questions specific to this group are taken
up in [[BC-610]].

## BS-0170 Subtype-specific epidemiology across populations

Subtype distribution differs between populations, and that difference is frequently offered as the
explanation for an outcome gap. It explains part of one and not most of it, and the distinction is
worth being precise about.

The United States data are the clearest available. Black women have 38% higher breast cancer
mortality than White women despite 5% lower incidence [@giaquinto2024]. Triple-negative disease is
more common in Black women, which contributes. It does not account for the gap, because Black women
have the lowest survival within every subtype and every stage except localized disease. They are
also less likely to be diagnosed at localized stage, 58% against 68%.

Age adds a further pattern. In women aged 20 to 29 years and 30 to 39 years, age-standardized
incidence was higher in non-Hispanic Black women than in non-Hispanic White women, with incidence
rate ratios of 1.53 and 1.15 [@xu2024]. In women aged 40 to 49 years the relationship reversed, with
a rate ratio of 0.96.

A crossover of that kind is not a property of tumours. It is what a difference in age-specific
exposure or in detection looks like. Race, ethnicity and genetic ancestry are three different
variables, and they are separated in [[BC-690]].

## BS-0180 Overdiagnosis as an epidemiologic concept

Overdiagnosis is the detection at screening of a cancer that would not have become clinically
apparent during that person's remaining lifetime. It is not misdiagnosis, and it is not a false
positive. The pathology is correct in every overdiagnosed case.

Two distinct mechanisms produce it, and they call for different responses. The first is
non-progressive or extremely indolent disease. The second is progressive disease in a person who
dies of something else first. The second mechanism depends on age and comorbidity rather than on
tumour biology.

The magnitude depends entirely on the denominator chosen, which is why published estimates appear
irreconcilable. An independent United Kingdom review estimated excess incidence at 11% of cancers
diagnosed in the invited group over the long term, and at 19% of cancers diagnosed during the active
screening period [@ukpanel2012]. Expressed as absolute numbers, for every 10000 UK women aged 50
invited to screening over the following 20 years, the panel estimated 43 breast cancer deaths
prevented and 129 cancers overdiagnosed.

A Bayesian natural history analysis of a United States screening cohort separated the two mechanisms
directly [@ryser2022]. Among 35986 women with 82677 mammograms and 718 diagnoses, 4.5% of preclinical
cancers were estimated to be non-progressive. Under biennial screening from age 50 to 74, 15.4% of
screen-detected cancers were estimated to be overdiagnosed, 6.1% through indolent disease and 9.3%
through competing mortality.

```caution
Overdiagnosis cannot be identified in an individual patient. It is a property of a population under
a screening policy, estimated by comparing observed incidence with modelled counterfactual
incidence. No test, no biopsy and no gene panel can tell a given woman that her cancer was
overdiagnosed. Communication of that fact is treated in [[BS-3230]].
```

## BS-0190 Data quality, registry coverage, and what is invisible in global statistics

Every global figure in this chapter rests on registries, and registries are unevenly distributed.
The third cycle of the CONCORD programme analysed individual records for 37.5 million patients from
322 population-based registries in 71 countries, of which 47 provided data with complete population
coverage [@allemani2018]. Countries without a functioning registry do not appear as low values. They
appear as estimates derived from neighbours.

That distinction matters when reading any global comparison. The WHO survival estimates covering all
194 Member States were produced precisely because measured survival data remain limited in low-income
and middle-income countries [@girardi2026]. A survival figure for a country with no registry is a
model output. A survival figure for a country with complete registration is a measurement. The two
are printed in the same typeface.

Even complete registration leaves things out. Stage at diagnosis is frequently missing or
inconsistently coded. Receptor and HER2 status are often absent, which makes subtype-specific
international comparison largely impossible. Treatment actually received is rarely captured.
Cause-of-death coding varies by jurisdiction, which is why net survival, estimated relative to
background mortality, is preferred to cause-specific survival in cross-national work.

The practical consequence is a reading rule. Where the survival gap in [[BS-0120]] is widest, the
data supporting it are weakest, and the direction of that bias is not known. The infrastructure
question is taken up in [[BC-710]], and the equity question in [[BC-690]].
