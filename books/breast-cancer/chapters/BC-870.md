## BS-7350 Scope, and the sequence this chapter assumes

This chapter covers endocrine therapy for metastatic hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative
disease. It covers the two backbones, the acquired alteration that separates them, the oral
receptor degraders, the first proteolysis-targeting chimera result, and the pathway inhibitors
that are added to an endocrine partner.

Three things are deliberately left to neighbouring chapters. CDK4/6 inhibition is in [[BC-880]],
including which inhibitor to choose and whether to continue it. Conjugates and chemotherapy are in
[[BC-890]]. The clinical framing of the four biomarker branches is in [[BC-560]], which this
chapter expands rather than repeats. Receptor biology is in [[BC-140]] and agent pharmacology in
[[BC-430]].

The sequence assumed here is the common one. Most patients arrive having had an adjuvant
aromatase inhibitor, then a first-line aromatase inhibitor with a CDK4/6 inhibitor. Almost every
endocrine decision in this chapter is therefore made in a tumour already selected by oestrogen
deprivation. That is not a detail of ordering. It determines which alterations are present and
which drugs still have a target.

The tables are generated from the trial registry when the book is built. Five slices appear below:
the whole field of endocrine therapy after progression, the first-line regimens, the oral
degraders, the pathway combinations, and the trials still running. A filter is mechanical, so a
wide one catches every trial whose fields match and not only the trials the section is about.
Where a section makes a specific argument, its table is filtered on the question rather than on
the drug class. A new readout enters the registry once and appears in every table whose filter
matches it. The prose does not repeat a figure the table carries, except where the argument is
about the figure itself.

The backbones were chosen by a generation of randomised trials that no longer appear in any table.
Anastrozole matched or beat tamoxifen as first-line therapy in postmenopausal women
[@bonneterre2000; @nabholtz2000; @vergote2000], and exemestane gave an early advantage in time to
progression without a survival difference [@paridaens2008]. Tamoxifen still worked after
first-line anastrozole [@thurlimann2003]. Against megestrol acetate after tamoxifen, anastrozole and
letrozole were at least as effective and avoided its weight gain [@buzdar1996; @buzdar2001]. Higher
doses of megestrol added nothing [@abrams1999], and the progestins compared with each other differed
mainly in side effects [@willemse1990]. A meta-analysis found improved survival with third-generation
aromatase inhibitors against standard hormonal therapy [@mauri2006]. Among the inhibitors,
letrozole was more active than anastrozole second line in one trial [@rose2003], exemestane and
anastrozole were similar in visceral disease [@campos2009], and anastrozole and letrozole were
equally tolerated [@dixon2011]. Fulvestrant was as effective as anastrozole after tamoxifen in the
two registration trials [@howell2002; @osborne2002]. Oral estradiol is an option after aromatase
inhibitor resistance, and 6 mg daily gave a clinical benefit rate similar to 30 mg with fewer
serious adverse events [@ellis2009].

Two backbones exist and they fail differently. Oestrogen deprivation removes the ligand, using an
aromatase inhibitor with ovarian suppression where ovaries are functioning. Receptor-directed
therapy leaves the ligand alone and acts on the protein. That distinction is the argument of this
whole chapter, so it is worth stating before any trial.

Two tables follow and neither is confined to this section's question. The first is the wide one.
It holds every registry trial with an endocrine component that enrolled after progression,
whatever was added to the backbone or substituted into it, with the single-arm and exploratory
studies left in. The second holds the first-line regimens, where an endocrine backbone stands in
both arms and the comparison is about what joins it or replaces it. Read the arms column before
the result column in both. The sections below take narrow slices of each.

Each section opens with the question its trials were built to answer, taken from the
[Chapter Stories](stories.html) map. The story says what was being looked for and what the design
cannot settle. The table says what else is in the cell. The prose argues the decision.

Five reported trials address the backbone question itself, and none of them found a large effect.
Substituting fulvestrant for an aromatase inhibitor, or adding it, buys months of progression-free
time where it works at all. {{trial:falcon}} is the trial the decision rests on, because it is the
double-blind randomised comparison against the drug a clinic would otherwise start
[@robertson2016]. Its final analysis reported no difference in overall survival between the two
backbones [@robertson2025].

{{trial:first}} asked the same drug question and is often quoted as though it agreed. It was a
phase II trial whose primary endpoint was clinical benefit rate, and it did not meet it
[@robertson2009]. A follow-up analysis reported median time to progression of 23.4 months with
fulvestrant against 13.1 months with anastrozole [@robertson2012]. Its survival
comparison was added by protocol amendment after the progression results were known
[@ellis2015]. What separates FIRST from FALCON is phase, endpoint and analysis
plan rather than the drug, which was the same drug in both.

The two addition trials disagree with each other, and the explanation is the population.
{{trial:swog-s0226}} added fulvestrant to anastrozole in previously untreated metastatic disease
and lengthened progression-free survival [@mehta2012]. Its gain reached overall survival in a
prespecified secondary comparison, and it held although crossover to fulvestrant at progression
was encouraged [@mehta2019]. {{trial:fact}} added the same drug at first relapse after treatment of
localised disease, in a population most of whom had already had adjuvant antioestrogen therapy,
and gained nothing on either endpoint [@bergh2012]. The drug and the schedule matched. What
differed was how much endocrine therapy the tumours had already been through. Both used the
loading regimen followed by 250 mg monthly, which {{trial:confirm}} then showed was underdosing
[@dileo2010; @dileo2013]. The addition question has never been asked at the dose now prescribed.

The dose question is pharmacology rather than biology. The drug is given intramuscularly, reaches
steady state slowly, and needs a day-14 loading dose in the first cycle. A patient who received
the lower dose historically has not had an adequate trial of the drug.

The {{trial:falcon}} population has largely disappeared. Endocrine therapy-naive advanced disease
now means de novo metastatic presentation without prior treatment, or relapse many years after
tamoxifen alone. Most patients presenting today carry adjuvant aromatase inhibitor exposure. The
trial therefore settles a question asked at presentation. Almost every decision in this chapter is
made at progression instead.

Everything else in the wide table is downstream of one fact about the second line. After a
non-steroidal aromatase inhibitor, endocrine monotherapy of any kind performs poorly.
{{trial:efect}} could not separate fulvestrant from exemestane [@chia2008]. {{trial:sofea}} could
separate neither fulvestrant with anastrozole from fulvestrant alone, nor fulvestrant from
exemestane [@johnston2013]. Both gave fulvestrant at 250 mg monthly after a loading regimen. That
weak performance is not a historical curiosity, because it is the control arm almost every later
trial in the table was measured against.

What has been added to that control arm falls into two groups. Where the partner carries a target
of its own, the addition works. A CDK4/6 inhibitor did so in {{trial:paloma3}} and
{{trial:dawna-1}} [@cristofanilli2016; @xu2021a], and pathway inhibition did so in the trials
argued in [[BS-7400]]. Where the partner was chosen on a mechanism with no companion marker, the
record is consistently negative. Entinostat in {{trial:e2112}}, venetoclax in {{trial:veronica}}
and fulvestrant added to an Aurora A kinase inhibitor in {{trial:tbcrc-041}} each returned their
control arm's result [@connolly2021; @lindeman2022; @haddad2023]. {{trial:enobosarm}} compared two
doses of an androgen receptor agonist against each other, with no standard-therapy arm to be
measured against at all [@palmieri2024].

Six rows are in that table for their backbone rather than for the question the trial asked.
{{trial:pearl}} and {{trial:young-pearl-kcsg-br15-10}} set an endocrine-based combination against
capecitabine, which is the decision argued in [[BC-890]] [@martin2020; @park2019a].
{{trial:summit}} and {{trial:sgntuc-019}} gave fulvestrant inside a HER2-directed regimen to
disease carrying an activating HER2 mutation [@jhaveri2023summit; @okines2025]. Each reports an
uncontrolled response rate from a basket cohort. Fulvestrant is the constant in both and not the
variable. {{trial:bioper}} and {{trial:triniti-1}} are single-arm studies of what to do after a
CDK4/6 inhibitor fails, and they appear because this table was asked for the exploratory trials as
well [@albanell2023; @bardia2021]. None of the six speaks to which backbone to start.

The first-line table has the same property and should be read the same way.
{{trial:paloma-1-trio-18}} and {{trial:dawna-2}} added a cell-cycle inhibitor to an aromatase
inhibitor [@finn2014; @zhang2023]. {{trial:sonia}} asked when to give that inhibitor rather than
whether [@sonke2024]. {{trial:right-choice}} set endocrine therapy with ribociclib against
combination chemotherapy in premenopausal disease selected for being aggressive [@lu2024]. Those
four belong to [[BC-880]] and [[BC-890]]. {{trial:egf30008}} enrolled first-line patients
regardless of HER2 status and found no progression-free survival gain in the centrally confirmed
HER2-negative majority, which is the population of this chapter [@johnston2009]. {{trial:pada1}}
and {{trial:serena6}} changed the endocrine partner during first-line treatment on a plasma
result, and are argued in [[BS-7380]]. {{trial:inavo120}} is argued in [[BS-7400]].

What neither table can show is the comparison the question now needs. Every reported backbone
trial ran without a CDK4/6 inhibitor in either arm. They answer a question about endocrine
monotherapy, in a position where monotherapy is no longer what is given. {{trial:serena4}},
{{trial:persevera}} and {{trial:veritac3}} put an oral degrader against an aromatase inhibitor
with palbociclib in both arms, which is the substitution question asked in the backbone actually
used. None of the three has reported. Nor has any trial chosen the first-line backbone by ESR1
status, which is reasonable, because the alteration is rare before treatment.

The practical position is that the backbone is now chosen by two things. The first is what the
tumour has already seen. The second is whether an ESR1 mutation is detectable.

## BS-7370 ESR1 mutation and what it changes

ESR1 mutation is acquired under aromatase inhibitor exposure and is rare in patients who have
never received one. The evidence for that, including how prevalence differs between the adjuvant
and metastatic settings, is set out in [[BS-2490]] and is not repeated here.

Three consequences follow and they should be held separately.

The mechanism is ligand independence. A mutant receptor adopts an active conformation without
oestrogen, so lowering oestrogen removes nothing that the tumour still needs. The receptor protein
remains present and remains transcriptionally active. A drug that acts on the protein therefore
still has a target.

The failure is specific. ESR1 mutation predicts failure of oestrogen deprivation, not failure of
endocrine therapy as a class. This is the organising fact of the chapter and it is frequently
misread. Patients whose disease carries the alteration are often described as endocrine-resistant
and moved to chemotherapy, which discards the one branch where the mechanism and the available
drugs line up.

The mutation describes a population rather than a tumour. It is subclonal and frequently
polyclonal within a single patient. Its prevalence rises across successive endocrine lines
[[BS-2560]].

The measurement side is separable from all of that. Detection is a plasma assay, not a tissue
assay. Archival primary tissue is usually negative because the selection had not yet happened, so a
negative result on an old block carries no information. In plasma, sensitivity at low variant
allele fractions is the limiting factor. Disease that sheds little DNA can give a false negative.
The respective blind spots of tissue and plasma are developed in [[BC-390]].

```caution
A detectable ESR1 mutation is not a marker of general endocrine resistance, and it is not a
prognostic curiosity either. Its only established use is narrow and specific. It says that
continued oestrogen deprivation has lost its target, and that a receptor-directed agent has not.
Reading it as a reason to abandon endocrine therapy inverts the result.
```

```interplay target=BC-350
This is the one place in the book where the evolutionary argument of Part VI converts cleanly into
a prescription. Treatment applies a selection, the selection is convergent at the level of a single
gene, the gene is detectable in blood, and a drug class exists whose mechanism survives that
particular escape. Every step in that chain is required. Where any one is missing, as with cyclin
E1 in [[BC-880]] or with antigen loss in [[BC-460]], the same evolutionary reasoning produces
knowledge and no action.
```

## BS-7380 Oral selective estrogen receptor degraders and their biomarker-defined benefit

```story SQ-0530
```

Five randomised trials have tested an oral receptor degrader against endocrine monotherapy after
progression. Two are phase III and three are phase II. Their headline results divide sharply, and
reading that division as a ranking of the drugs is the mistake this section exists to prevent.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- line=2L status=reported topic=SERD cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,result caption=The_five_randomised_trials_of_an_oral_receptor_degrader_against_endocrine_monotherapy_after_progression._The_wider_field_of_endocrine_therapy_after_progression_is_tabulated_in_section_2,_and_the_PROTAC_and_pathway_trials_have_their_own_sections_below.
```

The divergence in that result column is design rather than chemistry. {{trial:emerald}} and
{{trial:ember3}} prespecified the ESR1-mutant population as a primary analysis, which is why each
can report a mutant result as a finding [@bidard2022emerald; @jhaveri2025ember3].
{{trial:acelera}} and {{trial:ameera3}} had no mutation-defined population in their designs at
all, and read circulating tumour DNA out afterwards. {{trial:serena2}} was a multi-dose study
against fulvestrant, reported at a 90 percent confidence interval rather than the 95 percent
convention of the phase III trials beside it, in postmenopausal women only [@oliveira2024]. It beat
fulvestrant at both of the doses it carried forward, in a population not defined by mutation
status.
The populations differ as much as the designs. Prior CDK4/6 inhibition was required to enter
EMERALD and was a stratification factor or nothing elsewhere.

{{trial:emerald}} is the trial the decision rests on. Its comparator was the endocrine monotherapy
that would otherwise have been given. Every patient in it had already had a CDK4/6 inhibitor. In
patients with an ESR1 mutation whose prior endocrine therapy with a CDK4/6 inhibitor had lasted at
least 12 months, the progression-free survival benefit was clinically meaningful and consistent
across subgroups [@bardia2024b]. The
mutant analysis was written into the protocol rather than found in the data.

The two trials that missed carry more weight than their headline suggests, because the same split
appears in them without having been designed for. In {{trial:acelera}}, of 232 patients evaluable
for circulating tumour DNA, the 90 with a detectable ESR1 mutation had a hazard ratio for
progression of 0.60, against 0.88 in the 142 without one [@martin2024]. In {{trial:ameera3}} the
120 of 280 patients carrying a baseline mutation had a hazard ratio of 0.9, in a trial whose
overall hazard ratio sat slightly above one [@tolaney2023]. Two failed trials reproduced the
pattern of the two that succeeded, which is what makes it hard to explain as selective reporting.

The same dependence appears once more outside this table. {{trial:veritac2}} set a receptor-
degrading PROTAC against fulvestrant and concentrated its effect in the mutant patients
[@campone2025], and that trial is read properly in [[BS-7390]]. Three mechanisms of receptor
degradation, one dependence on mutation status. Together they establish that the mutation marks a
treatable state rather than a general endocrine resistance.

The mutant and wild-type groups are set by an assay threshold and not by a boundary in the
biology. Each trial called ESR1 status from plasma at its own limit of detection, in its own
evaluable subset. A tumour called wild-type may carry a subclone below that limit, and a patient
whose disease sheds little DNA may be called wild-type on volume rather than on genotype. Nothing
in the table separates a true wild-type tumour from an undersampled one.

```caution
The control arm in every one of these trials is endocrine monotherapy, usually fulvestrant, given
to patients who have already progressed on endocrine therapy. Median progression-free survival in
those control arms is measured in a few months, which the result column shows. A hazard ratio
measured against a nearly inert comparator establishes that a drug is active. It does not establish
where the drug belongs relative to a CDK4/6 inhibitor combination, a pathway inhibitor or a
conjugate. No oral degrader
has yet shown an overall survival benefit in any of these trials, and no two of them have been
compared with each other.
```

{{trial:ember3}} carried a third arm, in which imlunestrant with abemaciclib beat imlunestrant
alone [@jhaveri2025ember3]. That comparison admitted patients who had never received a CDK4/6
inhibitor. It is evidence that the combination is better than the single agent, and not evidence
about continuing a CDK4/6 inhibitor beyond progression.

```

Every trial above randomised after the scan had changed. The harder question is whether to act on
a rising mutation in a patient whose imaging is still reassuring. Two trials randomised at exactly
that moment and they agree. {{trial:pada1}} monitored patients on a first-line aromatase inhibitor
with palbociclib and, on a rising ESR1 mutation without progression, switched the endocrine partner
to fulvestrant [@bidard2022pada]. {{trial:serena6}} used the same trigger and switched to
camizestrant while continuing the same CDK4/6 inhibitor [@bidard2025; @turner2026serena6]. One
degrader is parenteral and one is oral, one trial was open-label in a single country and the other
was double-blind across twenty-three. The agreement therefore looks like a finding about the
timing rather than about the molecule. The design argument, and its limits, are developed in
[[BS-2600]].

What neither randomised is the comparison a clinic actually weighs. Both control arms continued an
aromatase inhibitor already known at the molecular level to be failing, so the contrast is against
something close to inert. Nobody has compared switching now with switching at radiological
progression, which is the choice in front of the oncologist. The clock is the second problem.
Progression-free survival here is measured from a randomisation triggered by an assay, so the
starting point is set by a detection threshold on a ctDNA panel rather than by anything the
patient experienced. Molecular progression is a laboratory convention as much as a biological
event, and patients whose resistance takes a route that panel does not report never enter.

One figure speaks past the first line. In {{trial:serena6}} median second progression-free
survival was 25.7 months with the switch against 19.1 months without it [@bidard2025]. The
advantage was still there after the next line at that analysis, which is what a strategy trial has
to show. Overall survival is not yet reported in either trial.

The arithmetic of that strategy is the part no table shows. PADA-1 monitored 1017 patients to
randomise 172. SERENA-6 tested 3256 patients at least once, found 548 positive, and randomised 315
[@turner2026serena6]. The rest were screened, tested every two to three months while well and
responding, and never entered a trial result.

The precedent that should restrain enthusiasm is {{trial:swog-s0500}}. It changed treatment on a
persistent circulating tumour cell count rather than on imaging, and survival did not improve
[@smerage2014]. A blood marker can be prognostic without acting on it being therapeutic. That is
why a progression-free survival result measured from a molecular randomisation needs a survival
result behind it before it becomes a standard.

The strategy also has a service cost the progression curves do not show. It requires plasma testing
every two to three months in patients who are well and responding. It then commits the service to
changing treatment on a laboratory result while the imaging is unchanged. A clinic that cannot
deliver the testing reliably should not adopt the strategy, because an irregularly tested patient
gets the control arm with extra appointments.

```caution
{{trial:serena6}} is the strongest available case for acting on a molecular result before the scan
changes. It establishes the strategy in one mutation, with one drug, in patients who stayed on
first-line therapy long enough to be tested repeatedly. Whether pre-emptive switching generalises
to other resistance alterations is an open question, not an extension of this result.
```

## BS-7390 Targeted degradation and the first PROTAC result

A proteolysis-targeting chimera is a bifunctional molecule. One end binds the oestrogen receptor.
The other recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase. Bringing the two together marks the receptor for
ubiquitination and proteasomal destruction. The molecule is then released and can act again, so
the mechanism is catalytic rather than stoichiometric. A conventional degrader binds the receptor
and destabilises the molecule it is bound to.

{{trial:veritac2}} tested that chemistry against fulvestrant in a randomised phase 3 trial. Its
split is the teaching case of the chapter. Vepdegestrant significantly prolonged progression-free
survival in the ESR1-mutant population. In the full randomised population it did not
[@campone2025].

The comparator is what makes this informative. Fulvestrant is itself a receptor degrader. The
result is therefore degrader against degrader, so it cannot be explained by saying that degradation
beats antagonism. Two readings remain and the trial does not separate them. Mutant disease may be
more dependent on the amount of receptor protein present, so more complete degradation matters
more. Or fulvestrant may simply perform worse against a mutant receptor, if its receptor engagement
at the licensed intramuscular dose is incomplete. Both predict the observed split.

The wider point is the one to carry away. Two chemically distinct oral approaches to the receptor,
the degraders of [[BS-7380]] and now the chimera, produce the same biomarker-defined split against
the same comparator. What selects the responding patient is the resistance mechanism, not the
chemistry used against it. A new molecule in this class should be expected to reproduce the split
until a trial shows otherwise.

Two limits apply as they do to the whole class. No survival data exist. No trial has compared this
agent with a CDK4/6 inhibitor combination at the same point in the sequence.

## BS-7400 Endocrine therapy with PI3K, AKT and mTOR pathway inhibition

```story SQ-0510
```

```story SQ-0540
```

One pathway, four levels of it, and seven trials that disagree about who should receive the drug
more than they disagree about whether it works. Five of the seven are phase III.
{{trial:faktion}} is phase II and {{trial:bylieve}} is a single-arm cohort. Both are read as
supporting evidence rather than as licensing trials.

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=pi3k-akt status=reported cols=acronym,n,population,arms,result,os caption=Pathway_inhibition_added_to_an_endocrine_partner._INAVO120_is_a_first-line_trial_and_so_does_not_appear_in_the_table_above.
```

The agreement in that table is broad and it is about direction. At every level of the pathway,
adding the inhibitor to an endocrine partner lengthens progression-free survival against that
partner alone. mTOR inhibition did it in 2012, alpha-selective PI3K inhibition and AKT inhibition
did it next, and dual PI3K and mTOR inhibition did it most recently. Survival results are scarce.
Two of the seven report a significant gain in overall survival, and one of those is a phase II.

The differences are about how the genotype entered the design, and that decides how much of each
estimate is selection. {{trial:solar1}} enrolled two cohorts by tumour-tissue PIK3CA status.
It powered the mutated cohort and reported the non-mutated one beside it, which is what lets the
marker be called predictive here rather than prognostic [@andre2019; @andre2021]. The methodology
is worked through in [[BS-6340]]. {{trial:capitello291}} enrolled without selecting and made the
pathway-altered patients a co-primary population [@turner2023capivasertib]. That is a weaker basis
for a predictive claim, for a mechanical reason. The overall population contains the altered
patients, so an overall effect is expected whenever the altered subgroup benefits. Separating the
marker's predictive value needs the marker-negative analysis, which in that trial was not a primary
endpoint.
{{trial:viktoria1}} went further in the other direction and randomised the PIK3CA wild-type cohort
separately, so its reported result belongs to wild-type disease alone and its mutant cohort has
not yet reported [@hurvitz2026]. {{trial:bylieve}} required the mutation for entry and had no
control arm at all [@rugo2021b]. Its report also carries a published expression of concern
[@theeditorsofthelancetoncology2023].

The control arms differ as much as the designs, and that is the sentence the result column hides.
Fulvestrant alone ran to a median of 2.0 months in {{trial:viktoria1}}, against 3.6 months in
{{trial:capitello291}} and 5.7 months in the mutated cohort of {{trial:solar1}}. The spread across
those three control arms is as large as some of the treatment effects in the table, and it tracks
how much prior therapy each population had been given. VIKTORIA-1 required progression on a CDK4/6
inhibitor with an aromatase inhibitor. Most of CAPItello-291 had had a CDK4/6 inhibitor. SOLAR-1
ran before that class was routine. A hazard ratio is a ratio to whatever the control arm did, so
these numbers cannot be ranked against one another.

{{trial:faktion}} and {{trial:capitello291}} make the same point inside one drug. The phase II
enrolled nobody who had received a CDK4/6 inhibitor. It reported a significant overall survival
gain [@jones2020; @howell2022]. The phase III enrolled a population most of whom had, and it has
not reported overall survival at all. Same drug, same comparator, different population. The smaller
and earlier trial is the one that looks better, which is the order to be suspicious of.

Measurement separates these trials as well. SOLAR-1 and BYLieve called the alteration on tumour
tissue. Other trials accepted circulating tumour DNA, and the two do not identify the same
patients. PTEN enters the AKT-pathway-altered group as loss of immunohistochemical staining rather
than as a sequenced mutation. That one label therefore covers three assays and three thresholds.

{{trial:inavo120}} is the trial to reason from in the first line. It selected twice, requiring a
PIK3CA mutation and relapse during or within 12 months of adjuvant endocrine therapy, and it added
the inhibitor to palbociclib with fulvestrant [@turner2024inavo120]. It is the only phase III
trial in this section with a significant survival benefit [@jhaveri2025inavo]. Whether that
belongs to the population, to the triplet or to the drug cannot be separated from a single trial.
No arm isolates the pathway inhibitor from the cell-cycle drug.

{{trial:bolero2}} predates biomarker selection entirely [@baselga2012]. Everolimus with exemestane
delayed progression in unselected patients and did not extend life [@piccart2014]. It remains
useful where sequencing is unavailable and the mechanism is otherwise unaddressed.

Three things the table has no column for decide how this is used. Nothing here randomises pathway
inhibition against a conjugate, or against continuing CDK4/6 blockade, at the same point in the
sequence. Nothing compares two drugs of the same class, and {{trial:inavo121}} is the first trial
that will. And no column records delivered dose intensity, which is what hyperglycaemia, rash,
stomatitis and diarrhoea actually determine.

Tolerability is what decides how much of that benefit a patient actually receives, and it differs
in kind between the three approved options. {{trial:solar1}} established PIK3CA mutation as a
predictive marker and also established why alpelisib is hard to give. Grade 3 or 4 hyperglycaemia
occurred in 36.6% of patients against 0.7% on placebo [@andre2019]. That rate is why the drug
needs a glucose plan agreed before the first prescription rather than after the first abnormal
reading.

Capivasertib is better tolerated and its toxicity is differently shaped. In {{trial:capitello291}}
grade 3 or higher rash occurred in 12.1% of patients against 0.3% on placebo, and diarrhoea in
9.3% against 0.3% [@turner2023capivasertib]. Rash is early and usually manageable. That is a
different burden from a metabolic one carried for the length of a response.

Everolimus is limited by stomatitis, pneumonitis and metabolic toxicity rather than by any of
those. Pathway biology is in [[BC-170]] and agent detail in [[BC-450]].

```practice
Test for PIK3CA, AKT1 and PTEN alterations at progression on first-line therapy, not later. The
result determines most of the options in this section.

Use plasma first and tissue second. A plasma-negative PIK3CA result in low-burden or low-shedding
disease should be followed by tissue before the option is discarded [[BC-390]].

Read PTEN carefully. It is usually reported as loss of immunohistochemical staining, which is a
different kind of claim from a sequenced mutation and is less reproducible between laboratories.

Plan the metabolic monitoring before the first prescription, not after the first fasting glucose.
Hyperglycaemia with a PI3K-alpha inhibitor is an on-target effect and is predictable. Grade 3 or 4
hyperglycaemia reached 36.6% in {{trial:solar1}} against 0.7% on placebo [@andre2019], and that is
the number to hold when consenting a patient.

Ask who else is managing the patient. These agents need a plan for glucose, rash, diarrhoea and
stomatitis that survives a weekend, and that plan belongs in the notes rather than in the
consultation.
```

```interplay target=BC-350
PIK3CA mutation frequency is not evenly distributed. In The Cancer Genome Atlas, patients of
African ancestry carried more TP53 mutations and fewer PIK3CA mutations than patients of European
ancestry. Most of those molecular differences disappeared after adjusting for intrinsic subtype,
which means the mutation-frequency difference is entangled with the subtype distribution rather
than independent of it [@huo2017]. Two consequences follow and they belong together. Biologically,
an alteration frequency is a property of a population's subtype mix, not a fixed attribute of
ancestry. Operationally, a drug whose eligibility is defined by that alteration will be prescribed
at different rates across populations for reasons that are partly biological and partly about who
gets sequenced at all. Access and representation are developed in [[BC-690]].
```

## BS-7410 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

```evidence setting=metastatic subtype=HR+/HER2- modality=endocrine status=ongoing,awaited cols=acronym,phase,n,population,arms,endpoint,status caption=Endocrine_and_pathway_trials_that_have_not_reported.
```

Three trials ask the same question and it is the question that would rewrite this chapter.
{{trial:serena4}}, {{trial:veritac3}} and {{trial:persevera}} each test an oral receptor degrader
against an aromatase inhibitor as the first-line backbone, with palbociclib in both arms. All
three enrol without selecting for ESR1 mutation, which is the correct design, because the
alteration is rare before treatment. A positive result would move the degraders from salvage to
backbone. It would also make the pre-emptive switching strategy redundant, since there would be
nothing to switch from.

{{trial:capitello292}} asks whether AKT inhibition belongs alongside CDK4/6 blockade rather than
after it, in a population unselected for the pathway alteration. {{trial:evera}} holds
everolimus constant in both arms and varies the endocrine partner, so it asks a degrader question
inside a pathway regimen rather than a pathway question. {{trial:inavo121}} compares two
PI3K-alpha inhibitors directly, and is the only trial in this area that will produce a within-class
comparison. Within-class comparisons are what the field consistently lacks.

{{trial:evopar-breast01}} removes first-line CDK4/6 inhibition altogether in germline or somatic
BRCA1, BRCA2 or PALB2 disease, replacing it with a PARP1-selective inhibitor and an oral degrader.
It is the only trial listed here that takes the CDK4/6 inhibitor out, rather than changing the
endocrine partner standing beside it.

Four questions have no trial attached to them and should be named. Nothing compares an oral
degrader with a conjugate at the same decision point. Nothing randomises the order in which
endocrine-based and conjugate therapy are given, which is the decision made in [[BC-890]]. No
oral degrader has been compared with another, so the class will be ranked on cross-trial
inspection unless that changes. And no oral degrader trial is powered for overall survival, so the
class may reach routine use without a survival result at all.
