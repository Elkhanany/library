## BS-5370 Male breast cancer and its evidence deficit

Male breast cancer is managed by extrapolation from postmenopausal hormone receptor positive
female disease. That is a defensible starting position and it is not a neutral one, because the
points at which it fails are not marked.

The largest characterisation is a retrospective joint analysis of cases diagnosed over 20 years.
Of 1,822 patients enrolled, 1,483 were analysed. Median age at diagnosis was 68.4 years. Among the
non-metastatic cases, 99.3% were oestrogen receptor positive, 81.9% progesterone receptor
positive, and 96.9% androgen receptor positive. Adjuvant endocrine therapy was given to 76.8%, and
it was tamoxifen in 88.4% of those. Breast-conserving surgery was performed in 4% and sentinel
node biopsy in 18% [@cardoso2018].

Two facts in that paragraph deserve separating. The receptor distribution is a biological finding.
The surgical figures are a practice finding, and they describe a population being treated with an
older algorithm than the one applied to women with the same stage.

The endocrine standard is tamoxifen rather than aromatase inhibitor monotherapy, and the reason is
mechanistic rather than conventional. In a postmenopausal woman the ovary has stopped producing
oestrogen, so almost all circulating oestradiol arises from peripheral aromatisation. Inhibiting
aromatase removes nearly the entire supply. In a man the testis is still an active endocrine
organ. It supplies testosterone as substrate and contributes directly to circulating oestradiol.
Lowering oestradiol relieves negative feedback at the hypothalamus and pituitary. Luteinising
hormone rises, testicular androgen output rises, and the additional substrate partly restores what
the inhibitor removed. An aromatase inhibitor given to a man is therefore not the same
intervention as the same drug given to a postmenopausal woman. It is that drug working against an
intact gonadal axis. This is why guidelines recommend tamoxifen as the preferred adjuvant
endocrine therapy, and reserve aromatase inhibition for use with a gonadotropin-releasing hormone
analogue or after orchiectomy [@hassett2020].

The clinical data are consistent with the mechanism and are thin. In a registry analysis matching
male and female patients on six prognostic variables, 5-year overall survival among
tamoxifen-treated patients was 89.2% in men and 85.1% in women, which did not differ. Among
aromatase inhibitor treated patients it was 73.3% in 30 men and 85.0% in 60 women, a difference
that reached significance [@eggemann2018]. Thirty men is the entire comparison. A prospective
cohort of 448 men found reduced disease-free survival in those not receiving tamoxifen
[@eggemann2020].

```caution
The evidence deficit here is not accidental and it is not only a matter of rarity. No randomised
trial has compared endocrine strategies in men. Most breast cancer trials specified female sex in
their eligibility criteria without a stated rationale, so the population was excluded rather than
unrecruited. Rarity explains why a dedicated trial is difficult. It does not explain why men were
kept out of trials that had nothing sex-specific in their design. The ethics and the design
remedies belong to [[BC-740]] and [[BS-6400]]. {{trial:male}} is the exception that shows the
shape of the deficit: it measured what standard endocrine therapy does to estradiol and to quality
of life in men, which is a question no trial in women ever had to ask separately.
```

Germline BRCA2 is enriched in this population, and testing is indicated at diagnosis rather than
by family history threshold [[BC-070]]. Presentation is later and nodal involvement more frequent,
which is partly biology and partly the absence of any screening pathway.

## BS-5380 Breast cancer in transgender and gender-diverse people

The tissue at risk is breast tissue, not gender. That single sentence resolves most of the
clinical ambiguity, and the remaining difficulty is that health systems record gender and not
anatomy.

The best incidence estimate comes from a nationwide cohort at a single gender clinic. Among 2,260
trans women followed for 33,991 person-years, 15 invasive breast cancers occurred after a median
of 18 years of hormone treatment. The standardised incidence ratio was 46.7 against cisgender men,
with a 95% confidence interval of 27.2 to 75.4, and 0.3 against cisgender women, with an interval
of 0.2 to 0.4. Among 1,229 trans men followed for 14,883 person-years, four invasive cancers
occurred. Most tumours were ductal and hormone receptor positive [@deblok2019].

Say what those numbers are numbers of. Forty-six-fold sounds alarming and it is a ratio against a
baseline that is very close to zero. Fifteen cancers in 33,991 person-years is a low absolute
rate, lower than in cisgender women. Both statements come from the same dataset and only the
second is useful for counselling.

Four practical points follow, and none of them requires new biology.

Screening should follow the tissue. A trans woman on long-term oestrogen has breast tissue. A
trans man after chest masculinisation surgery retains residual breast tissue, because the
procedure is not a total mastectomy. There is no trial evidence on screening in either group, so
recommendations are extrapolated and should be described as such.

Record an organ inventory rather than inferring anatomy from a gender marker. The marker in a
record is administrative and may have changed. The tissue has not.

Laboratory and imaging reference ranges are sex-indexed in most systems, which generates spurious
flags and occasionally missed abnormalities.

Gender-affirming hormone therapy interacts with breast cancer treatment directly. Endocrine
therapy for a hormone receptor positive tumour and exogenous oestrogen are working against each
other. That is a real conflict and the decision belongs to the patient with the mechanism
explained. Stopping a treatment central to a person's identity is not a neutral supportive care
measure.

The data deficit is worth naming because it will not fix itself. Registries do not reliably record
gender identity, so incidence and outcome estimates in this population rest on a small number of
clinic cohorts. The access and representation arguments are developed in [[BC-690]].

## BS-5390 Exogenous hormonal exposure

Three exposures recur and they behave differently. Conflating them produces most of the confusion
patients arrive with.

Menopausal hormone therapy has randomised evidence, and the randomised evidence splits by
formulation. In long-term follow-up of two placebo-controlled trials enrolling 27,347
postmenopausal women, conjugated equine oestrogen with medroxyprogesterone acetate, given to
16,608 women with a uterus, was associated with higher breast cancer incidence. There were 584
cases at an annualised rate of 0.45% against 447 cases at 0.36%, with a hazard ratio of 1.28 and a
95% confidence interval of 1.13 to 1.45. Breast cancer mortality did not differ significantly.
Conjugated equine oestrogen alone, given to women with prior hysterectomy, was associated with
lower breast cancer incidence and lower breast cancer mortality [@chlebowski2020mht]. An
individual participant meta-analysis of the observational evidence found that risk varied with the
type of preparation and with the duration and timing of use [@cghfbc2019].

The divergence between the two arms is the substantive finding. Oestrogen alone and oestrogen with
a progestin are not two doses of one exposure. In this trial they moved breast cancer incidence in
opposite directions.

Hormonal contraception carries a small relative increase. In a nationwide prospective cohort of
1.8 million women followed on average for 10.9 years, 11,517 breast cancers occurred. Compared
with never users, current and recent users had a relative risk of 1.20, with a 95% confidence
interval of 1.14 to 1.26. The risk rose with duration, from 1.09 with less than a year of use to
1.38 with more than 10 years [@morch2017]. The baseline risk in this age range is low, so a
relative risk of 1.20 corresponds to a small absolute excess. The population-level determinants
that this sits inside are in [[BS-0150]].

Gender-affirming hormone therapy is the third exposure and is covered in [[BS-5380]].

After a breast cancer diagnosis the question changes shape. Systemic menopausal hormone therapy is
not offered. Low-dose vaginal oestrogen for genitourinary symptoms is the genuinely contested
case, and it is taken up in [[BS-5560]].

## BS-5400 Occult primary and unusual presentations

Occult primary breast cancer is axillary nodal adenocarcinoma with a compatible immunophenotype
and no primary identified on examination, mammography, or ultrasound. It is a radiological
category rather than a biological one, which is why its incidence fell when magnetic resonance
imaging became available.

The diagnostic sequence is fixed. Core biopsy of the node with receptor testing comes first.
Breast magnetic resonance imaging follows, and it identifies a primary in a substantial proportion
of cases. Systemic staging excludes an extramammary source, and the immunoprofile should be
reviewed against alternatives before the breast is accepted as the origin.

Treatment follows the principle that this is node-positive breast cancer of the corresponding
stage. Systemic therapy is selected by receptor status exactly as it would be with a visible
primary. Local management of the breast is either mastectomy or whole-breast irradiation, and
irradiation is the option that preserves the breast without leaving the presumed primary
untreated. Axillary management follows nodal burden.

Other unusual presentations share one feature, which is that they disturb the staging workflow
rather than the treatment logic. Paget disease of the nipple requires a search for an underlying
carcinoma. Metastasis-first presentation, where disease is found at a distant site before the
breast, requires receptor testing on the accessible lesion and a decision about whether the
primary needs local treatment at all [[BS-5230]]. Inflammatory presentation is a clinical
diagnosis and belongs to [[BC-520]].

```practice
Test receptors on the nodal core before anything else. The whole systemic plan follows from that
result, and the node is the only tissue you have.

Order breast magnetic resonance imaging before committing to a local plan. Finding the primary
converts an unusual problem into an ordinary one.

Review the immunoprofile against non-breast origins explicitly. An axillary node is a plausible
destination for several primaries, and accepting the breast by default is the error here.

Treat by stage, not by the fact that the primary is invisible. This is node-positive breast
cancer, and the systemic algorithms in Part IX apply unchanged.
```

## BS-5410 Bilateral and multiple primary cancers

Bilateral disease is synchronous or metachronous by an arbitrary interval, usually three to six
months. The interval is a reporting convention. Its clinical value is that it separates two
cancers being staged together from a second cancer arising in a treated patient.

The long-term risk is quantified in a population cohort of 476,373 women with breast cancer as
their first invasive cancer. By 20 years, 5.6% had developed a contralateral breast cancer, which
was 3.1 percentage points more than expected in the general population. The absolute excess was
greater in younger women. Endocrine therapy was associated with reduced contralateral risk and
radiotherapy with increased risk [@mcgale2025].

The harder problem is distinguishing a second primary from a metastasis to the contralateral
breast, because the treatment intent differs completely. Features supporting an independent
primary are an in situ component, a different histological type, a different grade, and a location
in a separate quadrant. Features supporting metastasis are absence of an in situ component and
involvement of skin or lymphatics.

```caution
Receptor discordance between the two tumours is frequently used as evidence of independence. It is
weak evidence. Receptor status changes between a primary and its own metastases often enough that
discordance alone cannot establish clonal independence, which is the argument in [[BC-290]].
Sequencing can address the question directly and is rarely performed for this indication.
```

Bilateral disease at presentation, and any second breast primary, raises the germline testing
threshold [[BC-070]]. A confirmed pathogenic variant then changes the contralateral risk estimate
and with it the risk-reducing surgery discussion in [[BS-0680]].

## BS-5420 Rare breast malignancies and the boundaries of this book

Everything in Parts VIII to X assumes an invasive carcinoma of the breast. The lesions in this
section are not that, and applying those algorithms to them is the characteristic error.

Phyllodes tumours are fibroepithelial neoplasms graded benign, borderline, or malignant. They are
a surgical disease. Wide local excision with clear margins is the treatment, axillary staging is
not indicated because spread is haematogenous, and neither endocrine therapy nor conventional
breast cancer chemotherapy has a role. Malignant phyllodes behaves as a sarcoma.

Angiosarcoma occurs as a primary tumour and as a late complication of breast irradiation. In a
multicentre series of radiation-associated angiosarcoma after breast cancer, recurrence was
frequent and survival poor despite complete surgical resection [@seinen2012]. It presents as
cutaneous discolouration that is easily mistaken for radiation change or infection, and the
diagnosis is made by biopsying skin that a clinician was inclined to observe.

Primary breast lymphoma is managed as lymphoma of its histological subtype. Breast implant
associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma arises in the capsule around a textured implant rather
than in breast parenchyma, and presents most often as a late seroma. Its management is
capsulectomy and implant removal, not breast cancer treatment.

Metaplastic carcinoma is an invasive carcinoma and behaves unlike the rest of them. It is usually
triple-negative, frequently chemoresistant, and is discussed with the phenotypic spectrum in
[[BC-120]]. Secretory carcinoma carries the ETV6-NTRK3 fusion, which makes it one of the few
breast tumours with a tumour-agnostic targeted option [@drilon2018], and the general form of that
argument is in [[BS-5130]] and [[BS-5140]].

The boundary statement is simple and worth making explicitly. These entities are treated by
histology-specific principles drawn from sarcoma, lymphoma, and rare tumour practice. When a
pathology report names one of them, the correct next step is to leave this book's algorithms
rather than adapt them.
