## BS-6500 Next-generation endocrine agents and targeted degradation

Endocrine resistance has two distinct mechanisms and the new agents address them differently. ESR1
mutations produce ligand-independent receptor activity and are acquired under aromatase inhibitor
pressure, as described in [[BC-300]]. Ligand-independent signalling is not addressed by lowering
oestrogen, which is why the mutation confers resistance to aromatase inhibition and not to receptor
degradation.

Oral selective oestrogen receptor degraders were built for this. Elacestrant established the
proof in ESR1-mutant disease [@bidard2022emerald], and camizestrant, imlunestrant and giredestrant
extended the class [@oliveira2024; @jhaveri2025ember3]. The pattern across the class is consistent.
Benefit concentrates in ESR1-mutant tumours and is modest or absent in wild-type disease.

Targeted protein degradation is a different mechanism. Rather than blocking the receptor, a
bifunctional molecule recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase to it and directs it to the proteasome.
Vepdegestrant is the first such agent to read out in a phase III breast cancer trial. In
{{trial:veritac2}}, among 270 patients with ESR1 mutations, median progression-free survival was 5.0
months against 2.1 with fulvestrant, with a hazard ratio of 0.58. Among all 624 randomised patients it
was 3.8 against 3.6 months, with a hazard ratio of 0.83 and P equal to 0.07 [@campone2025].

That split is worth teaching rather than reporting. The same drug, the same trial, one positive
population and one negative overall result. The biomarker did not improve an effect that was there
anyway. It identified where the effect exists.

```caution
A drug positive in a biomarker-defined subgroup and negative overall has not been shown to be useless
in the wider population. It has been shown that the wider population dilutes the effect below
detection. Those are different statements and only the first is a biological claim.
```

## BS-6510 Novel conjugates, payload classes, and new targets

The conjugate field has moved on three axes at once, and keeping them separate is necessary to
evaluate any individual agent. The axes are target, payload and linker.

On target, the field has moved beyond HER2 and TROP2. HER3 is the most developed new target, with a
biological rationale that predates the conjugate, since HER3 upregulation is a recognised escape
mechanism from HER2 and PI3K pathway inhibition [@sergina2007; @hanker2021]. B7-H3 and LIV-1 are in
development. The important lesson from {{trial:destiny-b04}} and {{trial:daisy}} is that a conjugate's
activity does not require the target to be highly expressed, because payload delivered to any
expressing cell can kill neighbours through bystander effect [@modi2022; @mosele2023]. Target
expression therefore behaves less like a receptor requirement and more like a delivery address.

On payload, the shift from tubulin inhibitors to topoisomerase I inhibitors changed both efficacy and
toxicity profiles. Newer payload classes under investigation include immune agonists and
protein-degrader payloads.

On linker, cleavability determines bystander killing, and stability in circulation determines
off-tumour toxicity. Most conjugate toxicity is payload released in the wrong place.

The open question for the class is cross-resistance. A patient who progresses on a topoisomerase
I payload conjugate directed at one target may or may not respond to a different target carrying the
same payload class. The sequencing evidence available is discussed in [[BC-460]], and it is thinner
than practice requires.

## BS-6520 Bispecific and multispecific antibodies

Bispecific antibodies serve two different purposes that are often conflated.

The first is dual pathway blockade on the tumour cell. Binding two receptors or two epitopes of one
receptor can block signalling more completely than either alone, and can force internalisation.
Biparatopic HER2 antibodies use this, binding two distinct HER2 epitopes to drive receptor clustering
and internalisation, which also increases payload delivery when the antibody carries one. Bispecifics
targeting EGFR together with HER3 address the escape biology described in [[BS-6510]].

The second purpose is immune redirection. A T-cell engager binds a tumour antigen with one arm and
CD3 with the other, forcing a synapse independent of T-cell receptor specificity. This bypasses
antigen presentation entirely, which is the step most reliably lost in the immune evasion described in
[[BS-1890]].

Redirection works well in haematological malignancy and has been difficult in solid tumours, for
reasons that are specific rather than mysterious. T cells must be present in the tumour, and the
excluded and desert phenotypes in [[BC-240]] have few. The suppressive microenvironment inactivates
those that arrive. And target antigens expressed on normal tissue produce on-target off-tumour
toxicity with no therapeutic window. Breast cancer is largely an immunologically cold disease outside
a subset of triple-negative tumours, which makes it a hard setting for this mechanism rather than an
obvious one.

## BS-6530 Cellular therapies and therapeutic vaccines

Chimeric antigen receptor T cells transformed B-cell malignancy and have not transferred to breast
cancer. The obstacles are the same three that limit T-cell engagers, plus one more. There is no
breast cancer equivalent of CD19, which is to say no antigen that is uniformly expressed on tumour and
dispensable on normal tissue. HER2, TROP2, mesothelin and MUC1 are each expressed somewhere it
matters. Trafficking into solid tumour and persistence within a suppressive microenvironment remain
unsolved. Results to date in solid tumours are modest.

Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy has a stronger rationale in breast cancer than CAR-T, because
it uses the patient's own tumour-reactive repertoire rather than an engineered specificity, and
therefore does not require a shared antigen. It requires resectable tumour with adequate lymphocyte
infiltration, which reconnects it to the TIL biology in [[BS-1660]].

Therapeutic vaccines aim to generate or expand tumour-reactive T cells. Personalised neoantigen
vaccines identify mutations from the patient's own tumour, predict which generate presentable
peptides, and manufacture an mRNA or peptide product. The rationale is strong and the setting matters.
Vaccines have their best theoretical chance in the minimal residual disease state rather than in bulky
metastatic disease, where the suppressive microenvironment is established. That places them alongside
the residual-disease strategies in [[BC-770]]. Breast cancer's relatively low tumour mutational burden
constrains the approach, since fewer mutations mean fewer candidate neoantigens.

## BS-6540 Radiopharmaceuticals and novel delivery systems

Radioligand therapy delivers a radionuclide to a target-expressing cell, killing it and its neighbours
within the particle's path length. The approach is established in neuroendocrine and prostate cancer
and remains early in breast cancer [@abdelwahab2024].

The distinction from conjugates matters mechanistically. A conjugate requires internalisation and
intracellular payload release. A radioligand does not, because radiation acts at a distance. Path
length determines the scale of the bystander effect. Beta emitters travel millimetres and kill many
cells beyond the bound one. Alpha emitters travel micrometres, deposit far more energy, and are
correspondingly more precise and more dependent on accurate targeting.

The contribution this class has already made in breast cancer is diagnostic rather than therapeutic,
and one agent is not a prospect at all. Fluoroestradiol PET is approved and clinically available with
guideline standing, and it belongs in this chapter only because the rest of the class is not. It
images oestrogen receptor expression across all sites of disease at once, which is a direct
measurement of the between-lesion receptor heterogeneity a single biopsy cannot capture. A
meta-analysis pooled 12 studies and 308 participants. Clinical benefit from endocrine therapy
followed a positive scan in 66% of patients and a negative scan in 11%, a risk ratio of 3.21 with an
interval from 1.96 to 5.25 [@fes2025]. A negative scan in a tumour reported as receptor-positive on pathology
is therefore a usable result rather than an artefact to explain away.

HER2 PET does the equivalent for HER2 and has been studied for predicting conjugate benefit, and it
remains investigational [@gebhart2016]. Both address the sampling problem in Part VI more directly
than any tissue assay can, and the limitation of the approach is sensitivity for low-level expression
rather than concept.

## BS-6550 Epigenetic and transcriptional therapeutics

Epigenetic therapy in breast cancer has a clear rationale and a poor track record, and the gap between
them is informative.

The rationale is that resistance is frequently non-genetic. A cell can acquire a drug-tolerant state
through chromatin remodelling without any mutation, survive treatment, and later resume proliferation.
That mechanism is described in [[BC-310]] and is not addressed by any agent targeting a mutated
protein. Reversing the state, rather than killing the cell, is a different and appealing strategy.

The track record is that broad epigenetic agents have narrow therapeutic windows. Histone deacetylase
inhibitors and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors alter transcription globally, in normal tissue as well
as tumour. The dose that reverses the tumour state is close to the dose that disrupts haematopoiesis.

More selective approaches target specific readers, writers and erasers, or the transcriptional
machinery directly. CDK7 and CDK9 inhibition interferes with transcriptional elongation and has
particular relevance to hormone-receptor-positive disease, where oestrogen receptor signalling depends
on cofactor recruitment [@toska2017]. Selectivity against the closely related cell cycle CDKs is the
engineering problem.

The honest current position is that this class has strong mechanistic justification, no established
role in breast cancer, and a plausible future as combination partners aimed at preventing the tolerant
state rather than treating established resistance.

## BS-6560 Metabolic and mitochondrial targeting

Tumour metabolic reprogramming has been recognised for a century and has produced almost no approved
therapy, which should inform expectations rather than be ignored.

The central difficulty is that the pathways are shared. Glycolysis, glutaminolysis, fatty acid
oxidation and one-carbon metabolism are used by normal proliferating cells, and the therapeutic window
is correspondingly narrow. Tumours also switch substrates under pressure, so blocking one pathway
shifts flux to another.

Where the rationale is strongest is where a metabolic dependency is created by something else. Fatty
acid oxidation supports metastatic colonisation and dormancy survival [@gong2020], which connects to
[[BC-330]]. Metabolic adaptation contributes to endocrine and targeted therapy resistance
[@wangyy2017]. A dependency generated by a treatment is a better target than a constitutive one,
because the normal tissue is not under the same pressure.

The metformin experience is the cautionary case for the whole class. Epidemiological association,
strong preclinical rationale and a large randomised adjuvant trial that did not improve outcomes
[@goodwin2022]. The lesson is not that metabolism is unimportant. It is that population-level
metabolic association does not establish a tumour-cell-autonomous dependency, and only the latter is a
drug target.

## BS-6570 Microenvironment-directed therapy

Targeting the stroma rather than the tumour cell is attractive because stromal cells are genomically
stable and should not acquire resistance the way tumour cells do. The clinical results have been
disappointing and the reason is now reasonably well understood.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts are not one population. Single-cell work identified functionally
distinct subsets with opposing roles, including subsets that restrain tumour growth alongside those
that promote it [@costa2018; @elyada2019; @kieffer2020]. Broad depletion removes both. Several early
stromal-depletion strategies in solid tumours accelerated disease, which is consistent with removing a
restraining population.

The same logic applies to the extracellular matrix. Matrix stiffness promotes invasion, and the matrix
is also a physical barrier to dissemination. Degrading it does both things.

The approaches that remain credible are selective rather than ablative. Target the specific fibroblast
subset that drives immune exclusion rather than fibroblasts as a class. Normalise vasculature to
improve perfusion and immune infiltration rather than destroying it. Block a specific suppressive
signal such as TGF-beta in the tumour compartment rather than systemically. Each requires the ability
to identify the relevant subset in a given patient, which is a spatial profiling problem before it is
a therapeutic one.

## BS-6580 What separates a real target from a publishable one

This section gives criteria rather than opinions, because the distinction is assessable.

A real target satisfies six conditions. Genetic or pharmacological loss of the target impairs tumour
growth in a model that contains the relevant compartments, per [[BC-730]]. The dependency is present
in human tumours at a frequency worth developing against, established in clinical samples rather than
lines. The target is druggable, meaning a binding site exists or a degradation or delivery strategy
substitutes for one. A therapeutic window exists, which requires knowing what the target does in
normal tissue, not merely that the tumour needs it. Target engagement is measurable in patients, so
that a negative trial can be distinguished from a trial that never hit the target. And a biomarker
identifies the patients with the dependency before treatment, not after.

A publishable target satisfies the first condition, sometimes the second, and is silent on the rest.
That is not fraud. It is the reward structure of a literature that publishes mechanism and rarely
publishes the absence of a therapeutic window.

Two specific tests separate them quickly. Ask whether the target's normal function is known and
whether its systemic inhibition is survivable, because a target essential to haematopoiesis or cardiac
function will not produce a drug however good the tumour biology. And ask what fraction of patients
carry the dependency, because a dependency in 2% of tumours requires a screening strategy before it
requires a drug.

```interplay target=BC-350
Every platform in this chapter is evaluated against a biomarker measured on one biopsy. The
biomarker-defined split in {{trial:veritac2}} is the cleanest recent demonstration that the marker,
and not the drug, decides who benefits. It follows that the reliability of the marker sets a ceiling
on the apparent efficacy of everything here. A perfect drug allocated by an unstable marker looks like
an imperfect drug.
```
