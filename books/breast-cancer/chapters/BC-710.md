## BS-6040 The epidemiologic transition and the shifting global burden

Five-year survival for the same disease runs from under 40% to near 90% depending on where it is
treated. Almost none of that spread is biology.

Incidence and mortality do not vary together, and the discrepancy is the whole argument of this
chapter. In 2020 there were over 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer worldwide and 685 000 deaths.
By 2040, on population growth and ageing alone, the projection is over 3 million new cases and 1
million deaths each year [@arnold2022].

Age-standardised incidence ranges from under 40 per 100 000 women in some Asian and African
countries to over 80 per 100 000 in Australia, New Zealand, Northern America and parts of Europe.
Geographic variation in mortality is much smaller than variation in incidence. Countries with lower
incidence carry a disproportionate share of deaths [@arnold2022]. A country can have half the
incidence of another and a similar death rate.

The epidemiologic transition explains the incidence trend. As countries move through demographic and
lifestyle change, later first birth, fewer births, shorter breastfeeding, higher body mass index and
longer life expectancy all push breast cancer incidence up. Incidence in transitioning countries is
rising fastest, which means the burden is growing where survival is lowest.

The measurement caution is substantial and rarely stated. Incidence estimates for many countries are
modelled rather than registered. Only a minority of countries have national population-based cancer
registries with mortality linkage, and where registration is partial the incidence estimate depends
on assumptions rather than counts. This is why registry strengthening appears as an intervention in
national cancer control plans rather than only as a research tool. The burden estimates in
[[BC-020]] carry the same caveat.

## BS-6050 Late-stage presentation and the determinants of delay

The survival spread is largely a stage spread. The World Health Organization estimated
population-based age-standardised five-year net survival for women diagnosed with breast cancer
between 2017 and 2021 across all 194 Member States. Median five-year net survival ran across the six
WHO regions as follows: 39.1% in the African Region, with an uncertainty interval of 34.1% to 44.7%,
61.0% in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, 66.3% in South-East Asia, 81.1% in the Western Pacific,
84.0% in the European Region and 88.5% in the Region of the Americas [@girardi2026]. The earlier registry-based
estimates showed the same shape, with five-year survival of 90.2% in the United States and 66.1% in
India for women diagnosed between 2010 and 2014 [@allemani2018].

Concrete stage data show where the spread comes from. At a large tertiary hospital in Ghana, of 214
patients with breast cancer who underwent staging in 2021, 10.3% were stage I or II, 50% were stage
III and 39.3% were stage IV [@mensah2025].

Delay is conventionally split into three intervals, and they have different causes and different
remedies.

The patient interval runs from noticing a symptom to first contact with a health worker. It is
driven by symptom recognition, by the belief that cancer is untreatable, by cost of the first
consultation and by the availability of alternative providers.

The diagnostic interval runs from first contact to confirmed diagnosis. It is driven by referral
pathways and by the availability of imaging and pathology, which is why [[BS-6100]] treats pathology
as rate-limiting.

The treatment interval runs from diagnosis to treatment start, and beyond it sits treatment
abandonment, which has no counterpart in high-income datasets. In the Ghanaian series, all patients
were diagnosed within 31 days of presentation, and only 45 of 243 patients with confirmed breast
cancer, which is 18.5%, completed recommended treatment without abandonment [@mensah2025].

That pairing is the finding. The diagnostic interval met the international benchmark. Completion did
not come close. A programme that had optimised only diagnosis would have reported success.

## BS-6060 Resource-stratified guidelines and tiered recommendations

A guideline written for a maximal-resource setting is not merely unachievable elsewhere. It is
actively unhelpful, because it gives no ordering for what to acquire first.

Resource stratification solves a different problem from guideline adaptation. Adaptation asks what
of this guideline can we do. Stratification asks, given a defined level of resource, what sequence
of additions produces the greatest gain in outcome per unit of resource. The output is an ordered
list rather than a filtered one.

The method that produces such a list has a specific shape. Interventions are ranked by the
contribution each makes to clinical outcome rather than by their position in a conventional care
pathway [@anderson2006]. That ranking frequently inverts the order familiar from high-income
practice. Reliable oestrogen receptor testing and tamoxifen supply outrank a wider chemotherapy
formulary. Diagnostic imaging to support tissue sampling outranks population screening. Preventing
treatment abandonment outranks adding a new agent.

Stratified guidelines are also implementation documents rather than only clinical ones. They specify
a phased sequence, so that a ministry of health can cost a step and then cost the next one
[@anderson2023]. A guideline that lists everything at once cannot be budgeted against.

The failure mode of this approach is worth naming. A tier is a planning category and not a
description of a country. Within any country, tertiary centres and district hospitals sit at
different tiers, and a national designation conceals that. The tiers themselves are set out in
[[BS-6080]].

## BS-6070 The WHO Global Breast Cancer Initiative and its three pillars

The Global Breast Cancer Initiative was launched by the World Health Organization to coordinate
efforts to reduce breast cancer mortality, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries
[@anderson2021]. Its framework organises the whole of breast cancer control into three pillars, each
with one key performance indicator and one numerical benchmark.

The benchmarks are, in order: at least 60% of breast cancers diagnosed at stage I or II, all
suspected breast cancer diagnosed within 60 days of presentation to the health system, and at least
80% of patients completing recommended treatment [@boucheron2025]. The stated goal is a 2.5%
reduction in breast cancer mortality each year [@mensah2025].

Three properties of this design deserve attention, because they are what make it different from a
guideline.

The indicators are health system measurements rather than clinical ones. None of them names a drug
or a procedure. A country can meet all three with a basic-tier formulary and fail all three with a
maximal one.

The three are sequential. Early diagnosis is useless without timely workup, and both are useless
without treatment completion. A programme is therefore assessed on the weakest link rather than on
its best-performing component.

The benchmarks are thresholds rather than targets to be approached. That is a deliberate choice, and
it makes the indicators blunt. A country moving from 20% to 45% of cancers diagnosed at stage I or
II has made a large improvement and still fails the indicator.

How far current performance sits from these thresholds is not hypothetical. The African Breast
Cancer and Disparities in Outcomes cohort covered Nigeria, Uganda, Zambia and two population groups
in Namibia. Achievement of the first two indicators ranged from 22% to 49% across those groups.
Achievement of the third was below 30% in every group [@boucheron2025].

## BS-6080 Basic, limited, enhanced, and maximal resource tiers

The Breast Health Global Initiative stratification assigns every intervention to one of four levels,
defined by the contribution each resource makes to improving clinical outcome [@anderson2006].

Basic is the level at which the core services necessary for a minimally functional breast cancer
system exist. Limited adds services that produce major improvements in outcome such as increases in
survival, and is typically delivered at secondary level. Enhanced adds services that are optional
but important, improving outcome and increasing the number and quality of options available.
Maximal covers services that may be used in some high-resource settings and that should be
considered lower priority than those in the first three levels on the basis of cost-effectiveness.

Two consequences follow from this structure and both are frequently missed.

The tiers are cumulative. An enhanced-tier service delivered without the basic-tier services beneath
it does not deliver the outcome attributed to it. A HER2-directed agent supplied to a centre without
reliable receptor testing is an enhanced intervention resting on an absent basic one.

The tiers apply to services and not to countries. Any country contains institutions at several
tiers, and the planning unit is the facility.

The stratification has aged in a specific and instructive way. It was constructed when the
distinction between basic and enhanced was largely about cytotoxic chemotherapy and surgery. It now
has to accommodate biomarker-defined therapy, where the limiting resource is an assay rather than a
drug. A tumour-agnostic or biomarker-defined indication cannot be delivered at any tier that lacks
the test, which is the argument of [[BS-6100]].

```caution
Reading tier assignment as a ranking of clinical value inverts what it says. A maximal-tier
intervention is not the best available treatment. It is the one with the least favourable ratio of
outcome gain to resource consumed, which is exactly why it sits last. Several agents that dominate
high-income practice earn their tier placement from cost rather than from weak efficacy, and the
placement is a statement about opportunity cost within a fixed budget.
```

## BS-6090 Task shifting and clinical breast examination based programs

Mammographic screening requires equipment, trained radiographers, radiologists to report, and a
recall system. Where those do not exist, the relevant question is not how to acquire them. It is
whether earlier detection can be achieved without them.

Clinical breast examination delivered by trained non-physician health workers is the principal
answer that has been tested in a randomised design. In a cluster-randomised trial in Mumbai, 20
geographically distinct clusters containing 151 538 women aged 35 to 64 were allocated to screening
or to control. Women in the screening arm received four rounds of clinical breast examination by
trained female primary health workers every two years, with cancer awareness, followed by active
surveillance. Control clusters received one round of cancer awareness and active surveillance
[@mittra2021].

The downstaging result was clear. The proportion of women with stage III or IV disease was 37% in
the screening arm against 47% in the control arm. Breast cancer was detected at a younger age in the
screening arm [@mittra2021].

The mortality result was not. Breast cancer mortality was 20.82 per 100 000 person-years in the
screening arm against 24.62 in the control arm, a rate ratio of 0.85 with limits of 0.71 to 1.01.
That 15% reduction did not reach significance. A post hoc subset analysis in women aged 50 and over
gave a rate ratio of 0.71, with limits of 0.54 to 0.94. In women under 50 the rate ratio was 0.93,
with limits of 0.79 to 1.09 [@mittra2021].

```caution
The commonly quoted version of this trial is the 30% mortality reduction in women aged 50 and over [@mittra2021].
That figure comes from a post hoc subset analysis, not from the prespecified comparison, and the
prespecified comparison did not reach significance. The downstaging result is the robust one and it
is sufficient justification in a setting where late presentation dominates outcome. Quoting the
subgroup figure as though it were the trial result overstates what was shown.
```

Task shifting is not confined to detection. Where oncologist density is low, chemotherapy
administration, symptom management and follow-up can be delivered by trained nurses and clinical
officers under protocol. The limiting factor in every case is supervision capacity rather than the
procedure itself.

## BS-6100 Pathology and diagnostic infrastructure as the rate-limiting step

Every treatment decision in breast cancer is made on a pathology report. Receptor status determines
whether endocrine therapy is given. HER2 status determines whether HER2-directed therapy is given.
Grade and nodal status determine much of the rest. Without a report, the only available decision is
whether to operate.

Pathology and laboratory medicine services in low-income and middle-income countries are of both
insufficient scope and inadequate quality for that role, and four barriers have been identified
specifically. Insufficient workforce capacity. Inadequate education and training. Inadequate
infrastructure. Insufficient quality standards and accreditation [@wilson2018].

The ordering matters when a programme is designed. A donated immunohistochemistry platform without
trained staff, without cold chain for antibodies and without external quality assessment produces
results. It does not produce correct ones, and an incorrect receptor result is worse than an absent
one because it is acted upon.

The analytic validity problem is the same one set out in [[BC-110]], arriving under harsher
conditions. Fixation time, antibody clone, scoring convention and observer training all move the
result. A centre without external quality assessment has no way of knowing where its own assay sits.

This is why pathology is described as rate-limiting rather than as one component among several. Drug
supply, surgical capacity and radiotherapy capacity each deliver their benefit only to patients
correctly classified. Adding a targeted agent to a system that cannot reliably determine the target
adds cost without adding outcome.

## BS-6110 Essential medicines, biosimilars, and access to targeted therapy

The WHO Essential Medicines List identifies medicines that are priority for public health. Whether
those medicines reach patients is a separate question, and it has been measured.

In a cross-sectional survey of 948 oncologists across 82 countries, respondents selected the cancer
medicines that would produce the greatest public health benefit in their own country. The most
frequently selected were doxorubicin, cisplatin, paclitaxel, pembrolizumab, trastuzumab, carboplatin
and fluorouracil. Of the twenty most frequently selected medicines, 19 were already on the Essential
Medicines List, 12 were cytotoxic agents and 13 had received United States regulatory approval before
2000 [@fundytus2021].

That is the first finding, and it is about priorities rather than access. Frontline oncologists
worldwide prioritised old, cheap, off-patent drugs.

The second finding is about availability. Universal availability of each of those twenty medicines
was reported by 9% to 54% of respondents in low-income and lower-middle-income countries, 13% to 90%
in upper-middle-income countries and 68% to 94% in high-income countries. Catastrophic expenditure
means spending more than 40% of household consumption net of food. Its risk was reported by 13% to
68% of respondents in lower-middle-income countries, against 0% to 9% in high-income countries
[@fundytus2021].

Two implications follow.

Access failure is concentrated in medicines that are not expensive to manufacture. That points at
procurement, supply chain and regulatory capacity rather than at price alone.

Adding further expensive agents to the Essential Medicines List is of questionable value while the
existing list is unavailable to half the respondents in the poorest settings. Biosimilar entry
lowers the price of trastuzumab and does not by itself build the cold chain, the infusion capacity or
the cardiac monitoring that its use requires.

## BS-6120 Radiotherapy and surgical capacity

Radiotherapy capacity is the most unequally distributed major cancer resource, and the disparity in
machine availability between high-income countries and low-income and middle-income countries
remains a principal constraint. Workforce credentialling is a second constraint of comparable size,
since a machine without trained physicists and therapists does not treat patients [@abdelwahab2024].

One mitigation is available immediately and does not require new equipment. Replacing conventional
fractionation with hypofractionated protocols for prostate and breast cancer was projected to allow
an additional 2.2 million patients to be treated with existing resources. Of those, 1.4 million
would be patients with breast cancer [@abdelwahab2024].

That projection deserves emphasis because of what it does not require. It requires no capital
purchase and no new drug. It requires a change in schedule, and the schedule change is one that
high-income practice has already made on efficacy grounds. The evidence base sits in [[BS-3740]].

Surgical capacity is the other half. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery estimated that 5 billion
people lack access to safe, affordable surgical and anaesthesia care when needed [@ngkamstra2016].
Breast surgery is technically among the less demanding oncological operations, which cuts both ways.
It can be delivered in a district hospital with a general surgeon. It also competes for the same
theatre time, anaesthesia and post-operative capacity as every other surgical condition in that
hospital.

The interaction between the two modalities is a planning variable rather than a clinical one.
Breast-conserving surgery requires adjuvant radiotherapy. Where radiotherapy is unavailable or
unreachable, mastectomy is not an inferior choice. It is the choice that matches the available
system, and offering conservation without deliverable radiation produces worse outcomes than
offering mastectomy.

## BS-6130 Workforce capacity and training

Workforce is the constraint that money does not immediately relieve, because training takes years
and the trainees are mobile.

Four workforce groups are separately rate-limiting in breast cancer care, and a programme can be
complete in three and blocked by the fourth. Pathologists and laboratory scientists, without whom
[[BS-6100]] applies. Surgeons with oncological training. Medical oncologists or physicians trained
to deliver systemic therapy safely. Radiation oncologists, medical physicists and radiation
therapists, whose absence renders installed equipment unusable [@abdelwahab2024; @wilson2018].

Nursing sits across all four and is the group most often omitted from workforce counts.

Two structural problems recur.

Training abroad produces clinicians competent in a system they will not return to, and it selects
for emigration. Training in-country produces clinicians matched to the available resource and
requires local trainers, which is the bottleneck that in-country training exists to relieve.

Retention is governed by factors outside the training programme. Salary, availability of equipment
to practise with, professional isolation and the presence of a career structure determine whether a
trained specialist stays. A scholarship programme with no retention component transfers workforce
from a low-income to a high-income country at the low-income country's expense.

## BS-6140 Capacity building, twinning, and sustainable partnership

Twinning pairs an institution in a high-resource setting with one in a low-resource setting for a
sustained period. The model has a good record in some settings and a poor one in others, and the
difference is structural rather than a matter of goodwill.

The features that distinguish the two are identifiable in advance.

Direction of agenda-setting. A partnership in which the low-resource partner defines the priority
addresses a problem that partner has. One in which the high-resource partner defines it addresses a
problem that partner finds interesting.

Duration relative to the training cycle. Building pathology capacity takes longer than a grant
period. Partnerships funded for three years and dependent on a single individual end when either
ends.

Whether the intervention survives withdrawal. Equipment donation without a service contract,
consumables budget and local biomedical engineering leaves non-functioning equipment. Training
without a local trainer leaves a cohort that cannot be replaced.

Whether it fits the local tier. Introducing an enhanced-tier service into a system that has not
secured the basic tier moves resource away from the interventions with the larger outcome gain, as
[[BS-6080]] sets out.

The most durable form of capacity building is the least visible, which is that it produces local
trainers rather than trained locals. A programme is sustainable at the point where it can replace
its own staff without external input.

## BS-6150 Data sovereignty, equitable authorship, and avoiding extractive research

Research conducted in low-resource settings by high-resource institutions has a measurable pattern of
credit allocation.

Across 7100 articles on health in sub-Saharan Africa published between 2014 and 2016, 68.3% included
collaborators from the United States, Canada, Europe or another African country. Overall, 54.0% of
43 429 authors and 52.9% of first authors were from the country the paper was about. Where
collaborators came from the United States, Canada or Europe, local representation fell. For papers
including collaborators from top United States universities, 41.3% of all authors and 23.0% of first
authors were from the country of the paper's focus. Local representation was highest when the
collaborators were from another African country. Overall, 13.5% of papers had no local coauthor at
all [@hedtgauthier2019].

The figure to hold is the last one. One paper in seven about health in a sub-Saharan African country
had no author affiliated with the country the paper was about. Authors from elsewhere in Africa are a
separate category in that review, and local representation was highest when the collaborators came
from another African country.

Authorship is a proxy for a larger issue. Specimens, sequencing data and registry records collected
in one country and analysed, stored and published in another leave the originating country without
the capacity to use its own data. The result is that the country supplies the observations and
imports the conclusions.

Data sovereignty is the principle that a population retains governance over data derived from it.
Operationally it means locally held data, local capacity to analyse it, local approval for secondary
use, and return of results in a usable form.

The ancestry genomics literature is where this matters most directly for this book. The argument in
[[BS-5900]] is that most claims about ancestry-associated tumour biology rest on cohorts in which
race was recorded and ancestry was not. Correcting that requires genomic cohorts recruited in the
populations concerned. Building those cohorts extractively would reproduce the authorship pattern
above in the one literature where the relationship between a population and its own genomic data is
the actual subject.

```practice
Three questions distinguish a collaboration from an extraction, and all three can be asked before
the first sample moves.

Who chose the question, and could the local partner have declined it without losing the resource
attached to it?

Where will the data live after the analysis, and who can query it in five years?

Which named local investigator will be first or last author, and is that agreed in writing before
recruitment rather than negotiated after the results are in?
```
