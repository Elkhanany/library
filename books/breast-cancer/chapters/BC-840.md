## BS-7120 Why hormone receptor-positive HER2-positive disease behaves as its own entity

This chapter covers early hormone receptor-positive, HER2-positive breast cancer as a disease in
its own right. The word triple-positive is clinical shorthand rather than a guideline category. It
names the intersection of three separate assays, each with its own threshold and its own
reproducibility, and [[BC-110]] sets those out.

The intersection is not a small corner. In the largest adjuvant trial of dual HER2 blockade the
hormone receptor-positive group was the clear majority. The population column of the table below
records the split. Whatever the exact figure in an unselected population, this is most of
HER2-positive breast cancer rather than a fraction of it.

Four things make it behave differently, each of them developed in a section below.

Pathological complete response rates are consistently lower than in hormone receptor-negative
HER2-positive disease, across regimens and across two decades [[BS-7140]].

Pathological complete response also carries less prognostic information here. In a pooled analysis
of seven randomised trials it was associated with disease-free survival in HER2-positive
non-luminal disease and was not associated with it in luminal B HER2-positive disease
[@vonminckwitz2012].

The two dependencies run on different clocks, because HER2-directed therapy finishes at one year
while endocrine therapy runs for five to ten. The late recurrence hazard described in [[BS-4350]]
applies here as it does to any other hormone receptor-positive group.

Almost everything known about the entity comes from hormone receptor subgroups of trials designed
for HER2-positive disease as a whole [[BS-7170]].

What this chapter leaves to neighbours. The HER2-directed neoadjuvant backbone belongs to
[[BC-830]], and adjuvant and post-neoadjuvant HER2-directed therapy to [[BC-820]]. Choice and
duration of adjuvant endocrine therapy belongs to [[BC-790]]. The metastatic setting belongs to
[[BC-910]]. [[BS-4430]] carries the short-form treatment algorithm, and this chapter the reasoning
underneath it.

The story below is the argument the rest of this chapter is evidence for, and it carries no
figures at all. It states what each group of trials was built to find out, what came back, and
what the design cannot settle. The tables are then read as answers to a question rather than as a
list. It is one node of the [Chapter Stories](stories.html) map.

## BS-7130 Reciprocal cross-talk and what it predicts for treatment

The mechanism is set out in [[BS-1260]] and [[BC-140]] and is not restated. The short form is that
the two pathways regulate each other in both directions, so blocking one raises traffic on the
other. This section is about what that predicts in clinic.

**It predicts that single-axis therapy selects for the other axis.** Oestrogen receptor signalling
is upregulated as an adaptive survival mechanism in HER2-positive tumours under anti-HER2 treatment
[@giuliano2015]. A tumour carrying both receptors therefore has an escape route that a receptor-
negative one does not, which is the direct handoff from [[BS-1280]]. The clinical form of that
prediction is simple. HER2 blockade without endocrine therapy should underperform here, as should
endocrine therapy given without HER2 blockade.

**It predicts that the two blockades should be given together rather than in sequence.** Every gap
between them is a window in which the escape route is open. The prediction is clean and the
supporting randomised evidence in the adjuvant setting does not exist [[BS-7150]].

**It predicts a lower complete response rate.** If the oestrogen receptor supplies a survival route
that HER2-directed therapy does not close, then eradication should be less frequent when the
receptor is present. That is what the neoadjuvant trials show [[BS-7140]].

**It predicts that endocrine sensitivity should be measurable early and should matter.** In
{{trial:perelisa}}, a fall in Ki67 after two weeks of letrozole selected a group treated to surgery
without chemotherapy [@guarneri2019perelisa]. A two-week endocrine response was being used to
predict the result of a HER2-directed regimen, which only makes sense if the pathways are coupled.

**It predicts convergence on the cell cycle.** Both axes end at the same restriction point
[[BC-160]], which is the rationale for {{trial:patina}} and is developed in [[BS-7670]].

```interplay target=BC-280
A report calling both receptors positive has averaged twice, over a tumour that is uniform in
neither [[BS-2370]]. The compartment that survives HER2 blockade need not be the compartment that
survives oestrogen deprivation. Prospective assessment in the neoadjuvant setting showed that HER2
heterogeneity separated a group in which no patient reached a complete response from a group in
which most did [@filho2021], and [[BS-2330]] gives the numbers. The clinical reading for this
chapter is narrow. The lower complete response rate in triple-positive disease is partly a
statement about how much of the tumour lay outside the reach of the regimen. It is not a statement
about how much HER2 the average cell expressed.
```

## BS-7140 Lower pathological complete response rates and what that does to the post-neoadjuvant decision

The observation is unusually consistent across drug classes and eras. Dual antibody blockade,
lapatinib-containing blockade, conjugate-based regimens and conjugate-plus-taxane sequences all
produce lower complete response rates when the oestrogen receptor is present. [[BS-4430]] gives the
most recent figures from the conjugate era. The effect survives changes of drug class, which is
what makes it a property of the disease rather than of any one regimen.

Now put that next to the second finding, because the combination is what matters. Complete response
was not associated with disease-free survival in luminal B HER2-positive disease in the pooled
German analysis, while it was strongly associated with it in non-luminal HER2-positive disease
[@vonminckwitz2012]. So in this subtype the endpoint is both rarer and less informative.

The consequence falls on the post-neoadjuvant decision, where {{trial:katherine}} and
{{trial:destiny-b05}} both assign escalation on the presence of residual invasive disease. Residual
disease is more common in this group, for reasons that are partly biological rather than a failure
of the treatment given. More triple-positive patients are therefore routed into escalation by a
trigger that carries less prognostic information than it does in receptor-negative disease.

That is not an argument against escalating, but an argument about what the trigger means. The
distinction is the one made in [[BS-4690]]. A response-adapted trial demonstrates that a population
defined by residual disease benefits from a specific drug. It requires neither that the defining
measure be prognostic nor that a regimen producing more complete responses produce longer survival.

```caution
The mirror error is more dangerous than the first one. A complete response in triple-positive
disease is regularly read as permission to de-escalate what follows. The trials that produced these
response rates did not test that. {{trial:phergain}} and {{trial:wsg-tpii}} both gave chemotherapy after
residual disease, then kept dual blockade running for a year afterwards in every patient. A
complete response inside such a design tells you the tumour responded. It does not tell you that
the rest of the plan was unnecessary.
```

## BS-7150 Endocrine therapy alongside HER2 blockade in the adjuvant setting

Every patient in this chapter receives both treatments. The question is whether they should
overlap, which no randomised trial in the adjuvant setting has answered.

The mechanistic case for overlapping them is the one in [[BS-7130]]. Oestrogen receptor signalling
rises under anti-HER2 pressure [@giuliano2015], so the months between finishing HER2-directed
therapy and starting endocrine therapy are months in which the escape route is unopposed. On that
reasoning the two should start as close together as the chemotherapy schedule allows.

The case against is practical rather than biological, because nobody has shown harm from concurrent
administration and nobody has shown benefit either. Aromatase inhibitor arthralgia, tamoxifen
symptoms and trastuzumab infusions accumulate in the same weeks. Most of the adherence that is ever
lost is lost in that first year [[BS-6800]].

What the neoadjuvant trials contribute is limited but real. In {{trial:wsg-adapt-tp}}, adding
endocrine therapy to twelve weeks of trastuzumab emtansine changed the complete response rate
hardly at all. The conjugate itself accounted for a large difference against trastuzumab with
endocrine therapy [@harbeck2017adapt]. In {{trial:tbcrc006}}, letrozole was given concurrently with
dual HER2 blockade throughout, on an explicit hypothesis that blocking both receptors was required
[@rimawi2013]. Neither trial isolates the contribution of timing, because neither randomised it.

Two practical points sit outside the trials. Endocrine therapy in this group runs long after
HER2-directed therapy has finished. The late recurrence hazard in [[BS-4350]] is therefore the
dominant long-term risk. Extended duration is decided on the same grounds as in HER2-negative
disease [[BS-6780]]. And in the metastatic setting, receipt of endocrine therapy at all has been
associated with better survival in this subtype in a real-world cohort [@carausu2022]. That is an
association in advanced disease and not a randomised comparison in early disease, but the direction
is consistent with the mechanism.

```practice
Start endocrine therapy as soon as chemotherapy finishes. Give it alongside the remainder of the
HER2-directed year rather than after it. There is no evidence of harm from the overlap, and the
mechanism argues against leaving a gap.

Do not delay endocrine therapy for radiotherapy, since the two are routinely given together.

Treat the first year as the adherence problem it is. A patient managing infusions, radiotherapy and
a new aromatase inhibitor at the same time is the patient most likely to stop the tablet.

Plan the endocrine duration conversation for the point at which HER2-directed therapy ends. That is
when treatment appears to the patient to be finishing. It is also the point at which most of the
remaining benefit has yet to be delivered.
```

## BS-7160 Chemotherapy omission and the de-escalation questions specific to this group

This is the live de-escalation question. It is more live here than in receptor-negative disease for
one reason. A triple-positive tumour has a second systemic therapy that works, which a
receptor-negative one does not.

Three strategies have been tested and all three work in some patients.

Antibodies with endocrine therapy and no chemotherapy produced complete responses in a minority of
patients in {{trial:tbcrc006}}, {{trial:perelisa}} and {{trial:wsg-tpii}}. A conjugate without
chemotherapy produced them in a larger minority in {{trial:wsg-adapt-tp}}. Response-adapted
strategies used a two-week Ki67 fall or an early PET response to decide who continued without
chemotherapy, in {{trial:perelisa}} and {{trial:phergain}}.

The selection tools are the weak point, because both are continuous measurements converted into a
gate. A two-week Ki67 threshold and a PET metabolic response threshold are allocation conventions.
The reproducibility problem set out in [[BS-6930]] applies directly to the first of them.

{{trial:wsg-tpii}} is the trial to read closely, because its design explains both its result and its
limits. Substituting endocrine therapy for the weekly taxane more than halved the complete response
rate, from 56.4% to 23.7%. Five-year survival was nonetheless close to complete in both arms. The
reason is in the protocol rather than in the biology. Chemotherapy was mandatory after residual
disease, so most of the endocrine arm received it anyway. The trial demonstrates that a
response-guided strategy is safe. It does not demonstrate that chemotherapy can be omitted, which
is a different claim.

That gap is the general shape of the problem. Demonstrating omission requires showing that events
did not accumulate, which needs more patients, longer follow-up and a non-inferiority margin
somebody has to justify [[BS-6370]]. No trial confined to early triple-positive disease has been
built at that size.

```evidence setting=early subtype=HR+/HER2+ line=neoadjuvant modality=endocrine sort=year caption=Neoadjuvant_trials_combining_endocrine_therapy_with_HER2_blockade,_the_evidence_base_for_chemotherapy_omission.

```practice
Outside a trial, chemotherapy remains part of treatment for node-positive or larger triple-positive
tumours. Where it is omitted, say plainly that the supporting evidence is phase 2 and
response-adapted.

The de-escalation with the strongest evidence is not omission of chemotherapy. It is omission of
the anthracycline, which {{trial:train2}} supports irrespective of hormone receptor status.

In a small, node-negative tumour the de-escalated regimens in [[BS-7000]] apply to this group as
they do to receptor-negative disease. The endocrine therapy that follows is the longer half of the
treatment.
```

## BS-7170 Subgroup evidence from the HER2 trials, and its limits

Almost every statement made in this chapter rests on a hormone receptor subgroup of a trial powered
for HER2-positive disease as a whole. That is worth saying explicitly rather than leaving implicit,
and [[BC-740]] sets the standard this section is held to.

Start with what the design does well. Most of the trials in the table stratified randomisation by
hormone receptor status, which means the subgroups are balanced for the factors randomisation
balances. A stratified subgroup is better behaved than an unstratified one. Stratification supplies
neither power nor a randomised comparison for the question of whether receptor status modifies the
treatment effect.

{{trial:aphinity}} is the instructive case. The hormone receptor-positive and receptor-negative
subgroup hazard ratios for adjuvant pertuzumab differ in point estimate and overlap substantially
in interval [@piccart2021aphinity], and [[BS-4430]] gives both. The defensible reading is that
nothing in those data distinguishes the two groups. The common reading, that pertuzumab works
better in triple-positive disease, requires an interaction the trial did not demonstrate. A later
exploratory subanalysis of the same trial went further and located the largest benefit in a
receptor-positive subset defined by HER2 amplification level [@agostinetto2026]. That analysis is
hypothesis-generating and its authors say so.

Now the ceiling on the direct evidence. The largest randomised trial confined to early
triple-positive disease enrolled 375 patients, and the next two enrolled 207 and 64
[@harbeck2017adapt; @tpii2026; @guarneri2019perelisa]. Those are the trials that studied this
entity rather than containing it. Everything else is a subgroup.

The asymmetry that follows is worth naming. Escalation decisions in this group rest on large
randomised trials whose populations included it, which is a reasonable basis. De-escalation
decisions rest on small trials designed for it, which is a weaker basis for a stronger claim.
Practice has been more willing to de-escalate on the second than to question the first.

```caution
A subgroup result does not become more reliable because the subgroup is large. The hormone
receptor-positive subgroup of {{trial:aphinity}} is large enough that its estimate is precise. It
is still not a randomised comparison between receptor-positive and receptor-negative disease.
Precision within a subgroup says nothing about whether the subgroups differ [[BS-6390]].
```

## BS-7180 Unresolved questions and what reads out next

The largest gap is an absence rather than a pending readout.

{{trial:compassher2-rd}} tests escalation of post-neoadjuvant treatment for residual HER2-positive
disease. Its eligibility is restricted to high-risk residual disease, defined as oestrogen
receptor-negative or node-positive [@osullivan2021compassher2rd]. Receptor-positive, node-negative
residual disease is therefore outside it. That is a large part of the triple-positive residual
population, and precisely the group in which [[BS-7140]] argues that residual disease is least
informative about risk. The trial that would settle what to do with them is not running.

Three readouts will change something. Overall survival from {{trial:destiny-b05}} will show whether
the invasive disease-free survival gain in residual disease converts into longer life. Its
population was assembled on residual disease, in which this chapter's group is over-represented for
the reasons given in [[BS-7140]]. Longer follow-up from the response-adapted trials will show
whether the strategy holds beyond the horizons currently reported. And the adjuvant question raised
by {{trial:patina}} has no early-disease trial behind it, so whether CDK4/6 inhibition belongs in
early triple-positive disease is unasked rather than unanswered [[BS-7670]].

Two questions will probably never be answered directly. Concurrent against sequential endocrine
therapy alongside HER2 blockade would need a large trial for a difference nobody expects to be
large [[BS-7150]]. Chemotherapy omission in an unselected triple-positive population would need a
non-inferiority trial whose margin would be hard to justify to a patient [[BS-6370]].

What a reader should take from that list is a calibration rather than a plan. This chapter
describes the best-treated common subtype in breast cancer. It is also the one whose treatment
rests most heavily on inference from populations assembled to answer a different question.
