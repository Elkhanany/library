## 1A · What a return is

- *Near 1, the logarithm is almost a straight line.* The curve y = ln(1 + x) for x from −0.5 to +1, drawn with the straight line y = x and the parabola y = x − x²/2. The reader drags a point along the x axis and reads the three values and the gaps between them. It would show that the line is excellent for daily moves, the parabola is good for monthly ones, and both fail for moves of 50% or more.
- *Property 2: log returns add over time.* A price path of 20 trading days drawn above a row of bars, one bar per day's return. A toggle switches the bars between simple and log returns, and a running total is drawn at the end of the row. With log returns the running total lands exactly on the whole period's log return; with simple returns it misses, and the miss grows as the reader raises a volatility slider.
- *The normal curve fits log returns.* Two bell curves over the same axis of one-year outcomes, one assuming normal simple returns and one assuming normal log returns, with the same mean and a volatility slider from 10% to 100%. A shaded area marks outcomes below a price of zero. The simple-return curve's shaded area grows visibly as volatility rises; the log-return model never has one.
- *What drag does to an account.* A histogram of final wealth after 10 years across 10,000 simulated paths, with μ and σ sliders. Vertical lines mark the mean, e^(μt), and the median, e^(Gt). As volatility rises the histogram stretches into a long right tail: the mean stays put while the median, and the share of paths that end below where they started, slide down.
- *The convention in this book.* A calculator for the rule "accept extra volatility only for enough extra mean". The reader sets two volatilities; the figure draws the drag curve σ²/2 against σ, marks both points, and reports the extra arithmetic mean the more volatile strategy needs just to compound as fast. It would make the quadratic cost of volatility visible: small at low volatility, steep at high.
- *The convention in this book.* The compounding table as a figure. The reader sets an interest rate and the number of payments a year (1 to 365, then continuous) and watches the growth of $1 approach e^r, with a second slider for the number of years. It would show where e comes from, and how little the compounding frequency matters at low rates.

## 1B · The shape of returns

- *What a return distribution is.* Two histograms side by side on the same dollar axis: the coin flip's two bars and the short put's four buckets, each bar's height its probability. The reader drags the probability of each bucket (the figure keeps the total at 100%) and watches the mean, the median and the standard deviation move. It would show how a small change in the 2% crash bucket moves the mean far more than the median.
- *The kurtosis.* A bet builder that shows moments rather than expected value. The reader sets a win probability and the sizes of the win and the loss, and the figure draws the two bars and reports the mean, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis. Moving only the sizes leaves the skewness fixed while the mean swings; moving only the win probability swings the skewness. It would make the two-outcome skew formula something the reader can feel.
- *Why selling options produces negative skew.* A normal curve and a short-volatility distribution drawn with the same mean and standard deviation, with the median and the mean of each marked. The reader drags a "tail weight" slider that moves probability from the peak into a far-left bump. The mean slides left past the median, and past zero, while the peak barely changes: a positive median beside a negative mean.
- *Same mean, same spread, different shape.* Strategies A and B as two monthly histograms on one axis, with the mean, the 5th percentile and the expected shortfall of each marked. A slider sets B's win rate from 80% to 99% while the figure keeps B's mean and standard deviation fixed at A's, re-solving B's win and loss sizes. As the win rate rises, B's loss grows rarer and deeper, its skewness and kurtosis climb, and its expected shortfall pulls away from A's while the mean-and-spread summary never changes.
- *Fat tails: rare events are not as rare as the normal curve says.* The left tail of the normal curve on a logarithmic probability axis, from 1 to 25 standard deviations, with a second curve for a fat-tailed distribution scaled to the same standard deviation and a kurtosis the reader sets from 3 to 20. Markers show the 1987 crash and the other events named in the text. It would show the two curves agreeing near the middle and parting by dozens of orders of magnitude in the tail.
- *Stress scenarios, not percentiles.* Waiting for the tail. A simulated monthly P&L record for the short 30-delta put, drawn as a running account balance, with a slider for the monthly crash probability (0.5% to 5%) and a seed. Beside it, the curve of the probability that n months pass with no crash, 0.98 to the power n at the default. It would show how often a trader sees one, two or three calm years before the first crash, and how convincing the balance looks until then.
- *Stress scenarios, not percentiles.* Skew of a book of spreads. The reader sets the number of spreads held (1 to 100) and a "common crash" slider from fully independent to fully shared. The figure plots the distribution of the book's total P&L and reports its skewness. With independent spreads the skewness shrinks like one over the square root of the count; as the crash becomes shared, it stays near the single spread's −2.67.

## 1C · Expected value and edge

- *Two famous coin flips.* The breakeven curve p* = L/(G + L) drawn over the plane of win rate (horizontal) against the ratio of average loss to average win (vertical, on a log scale). The region above the curve is shaded as negative expected value, the region below as positive. The reader drops a point for any strategy by entering its win rate and its average win and loss, and vertical guides mark the "85% win rate" and "92% probability of profit" claims. It would show at a glance how large a loss a high win rate can afford.
- *Why the trap feels safe.* Many simulated traders running Scenario B, each drawn as a cumulative profit line over 60 monthly rounds, with the expected-value line of −$0.30 a round drawn through them. A slider moves a vertical "today" line across the months, and a readout gives the share of traders who are still ahead. Early on, most lines are rising and most traders are ahead; the share falls as the months pass and the rare losses arrive, until the lines cluster around the downward-sloping expected value.
- *A more realistic version: selling a put.* A waterfall chart of the short put's expected value. It starts from the premium, subtracts the expected payout at expiry, then the extra loss from closing early under a margin call, then commissions and the bid-ask spread, ending at the net edge. Sliders set the average loss given a loss, the chance of a forced close and the cost per trade, and the final bar turns from positive to negative as the reader pushes them.
- *Expected utility: why dollars are not enough.* The curve U(W) = ln W for wealth from $10,000 to $200,000, with a slider for current wealth and a second for the size of a symmetric gain or loss. The figure marks the utility gained and lost as two vertical bars and reports their ratio. It would show the loss bar outgrowing the gain bar as the stake grows relative to wealth, and both shrinking toward equality as wealth grows: concavity made visible.
- *Compute expected log return, not expected dollars.* A growth curve for one bet. The reader sets the win probability and the sizes of the win and the loss, and the figure plots the expected log growth of the account against the fraction of the account staked, from 0% to 100%. The curve rises, peaks at the Kelly fraction, then falls through zero, while a straight line shows dollar expected value rising without limit. It would show that past a certain size, a bet with edge loses money for the account.

## 1D · Risk-adjusted return

- *Trading frequency and the Sharpe ratio.* The square-root law of frequency. The reader sets a per-trade Sharpe ratio and the correlation between successive bets, and the figure plots the annual Sharpe ratio against the number of bets a year on a logarithmic axis, from 12 to 100,000. With independent bets the line rises as the square root of the count; as the correlation rises, the line flattens and stops climbing. It would show why high frequency pays only when the bets are genuinely independent.
- *Why a low Sharpe ratio caps leverage.* A leverage dial. The reader sets a strategy's Sharpe ratio and volatility and drags leverage from 1 to 10. The figure plots the compound growth rate kμ − k²σ²/2 as a hump over leverage and, beneath it, the probability of ever losing 50% and 90% from the ruin formula. It would show growth peaking at the Kelly leverage, falling to zero at twice that, and the ruin curves climbing to certainty at the same point.
- *The Sortino ratio.* Sharpe against Sortino. A return distribution whose skewness the reader sets, holding the mean and standard deviation fixed, with the part below zero shaded. Readouts give the Sharpe ratio, the downside deviation and the Sortino ratio. Positive skew lifts the Sortino ratio well above the Sharpe ratio; negative skew pulls it down toward the Sharpe ratio, as with the PUT Index.
- *Expected shortfall and tail beta.* A scatter of a strategy's monthly returns against the market's, with the worst 5% of market months highlighted. The figure fits one line through all the points and a second through the highlighted ones, and reports the ordinary beta and the tail beta. A toggle switches between a trend strategy, a buy-and-hold index and a put-writing strategy, showing a put-writer whose ordinary beta looks modest but whose tail beta is near 1.
- *How sure are you? The probabilistic Sharpe ratio.* The uncertainty of a Sharpe ratio. Sliders set the observed per-period Sharpe ratio, the number of observations, the skewness and the kurtosis, and the figure draws the normal approximation to the true Sharpe ratio's distribution with the area above a chosen threshold shaded as the PSR. It would show the curve widening as the skew turns negative or the kurtosis rises, and narrowing only slowly as the sample grows.
- *The deflated Sharpe ratio: when you tried many strategies.* A selection-bias machine. The reader chooses a number of strategies from 1 to 1,000, all with a true Sharpe ratio of zero, and a backtest length. The figure simulates their returns, draws a histogram of the observed Sharpe ratios, highlights the best one and plots its equity curve. It would show a beautiful equity curve emerging from pure noise once the number of tries passes a few dozen, and how a longer backtest narrows the histogram and tames the best result.
- *The five-question stress test.* A stress-test worksheet. The reader enters the portfolio's value, the share given to a put-writing sleeve, the sleeve's expected shortfall and its S2 and S3 losses as percentages, and a largest acceptable monthly loss. The figure returns each loss in dollars and as a share of the portfolio, marks which limits are breached, and solves for the largest sleeve that passes. It would turn the five questions into numbers the reader can check before a trade.

## 1E · Drawdown, leverage and margin

- *Drawdown: the fall from the last peak.* The drawdown record of one equity path as a histogram of its daily drawdowns. The reader slides α from 80% to 99%, and the figure shades the drawdowns beyond the α percentile, marks their average as CDaR-α and marks the single worst as MaxDD. It shows CDaR as a tail average that moves smoothly with α, while MaxDD rests on one episode.
- *Drawdown: the fall from the last peak.* A drawdown simulator. It draws 10-year equity paths for a strategy with a 5% mean return and 10% volatility, each with its running peak as a line above it and its drawdown shaded beneath. The reader switches between normal and fat-tailed returns with the same mean and volatility, and sets the number of paths. A histogram of the paths' maximum drawdowns sits alongside, showing the normal model clustering near 25% while the fat-tailed version puts real weight at 40% to 50%.
- *What it takes to recover.* A recovery calculator. It plots the gain needed, D/(1 − D), against the loss D from 0% to 90%, with a second curve for the years to recover at a growth rate the reader sets. The reader drags a marker along the loss axis and reads off both numbers, and sees how steeply the curves turn upward past a 50% loss.
- *Three ways to take leverage.* A leverage cost comparison. For a $1M account taking $0.4M of extra exposure, three bars give the yearly cost of each route: a margin loan at a rate the reader enters, futures at SOFR plus 10 to 20 bps, and options replication at 50 to 150 bps of embedded leverage drag. The reader sets SOFR, the loan rate and a holding period in years, and a line beneath accumulates each cost over time. It shows how a gap of about a percentage point a year compounds, before any path risk is counted.
- *Margin: the deposit that can be called.* A margin cushion through a stress episode. Two lines run across 30 trading days: account equity, falling with the positions' mark, and the margin requirement, rising as the stress test and the broker's add-ons expand. The reader sets the starting leverage, the depth of the sell-off and how far the requirement expands, and the day the lines cross is marked as the margin call. It shows a comfortable cushion vanishing when both lines move at once.
- *Path dependency: surviving to the payoff.* The put spread's path against its payoff. The upper panel draws SPX over the spread's year, and the lower panel draws the spread's mark beside a flat line at its expiry payoff. The reader sets the size of the early fall, the rise in implied volatility and the account's cushion. When the mark crosses the cushion the figure closes the position, and it shows the locked-in loss beside the loss the spread would have taken at expiry.
- *Path dependency: surviving to the payoff.* Terminal loss against path loss for a short put spread, simulated over many index paths. One histogram shows the loss at expiry; a second shows the worst mark each path reached before expiry. The reader sets the number of contracts and the margin cushion, and the figure counts the paths that would have been force-liquidated. It shows why a size that looks safe against the terminal histogram can be 30% to 50% too large against the path histogram.

## 1G · Where an edge comes from

The reader sets the premium, the crash size and the crash frequency, and a readout gives the long-run growth rate, the maximum drawdown and the longest time under water. - *Source 3: institutional frictions and constraints.* A rebalancing-flow calculator. - *What it costs.* Edge against cost per trade. - *Why the taxonomy matters in practice.* A classifier for a claimed edge. The reader answers four questions: who is on the other side, why they accept a loss, whether the pattern would survive publication, and what it costs per trade. It turns the operational rule into a habit a reader can practice on headlines.
- *Mixed-source edges.* Stacked bars for trend following, VRP and momentum, each split into its risk-premium, behavioral and institutional parts, with weights the reader can adjust within the ranges "mostly", "partly" and "small". A second view ages each part at its own rate of decay over 20 years. It shows momentum shrinking most and VRP holding up, because of what each is made of.
- *From four sources to five categories.* A flow diagram from the four sources on the left to the five categories on the right, with the width of each band set by how many of the book's edges take that route. The reader hovers over a band to list its edges and their chapters. It shows the dominant routes and the exceptions, such as VRP running to a tilt and BAB to a factor.

A second panel runs both through a stress the reader sets, with the fall in SPX and the rise in implied volatility on sliders, and plots each position's loss against the capital it tied up. It shows ES losing in line with the index while the structure loses more than it cost.
- *Example 10: sizing from a stress loss.* A loss-budget sizer. The reader sets the portfolio's value, the S2 budget as a percentage, the strike, the premium and the stress scenario. The figure computes the loss per contract, the floor of the budget divided by it, and the S3 loss and collateral at that count, with a bar marking the S3 loss against a second limit the reader chooses. It shows the count falling in steps as the budget tightens, and the S3 check sometimes removing one more contract.

## 13F · Setup cards

- *What a setup card is.* A card with live checks. The reader enters today's readings: the composite signal, the VIX and VIX/VIX3M, ADX, the stage, the sign of GEX and the 21-day realized volatility. Every card in the chapter lights each of its rows green or red, and a column of verdicts reads trade, reduce, halt or skip. It shows how one change in the regime, such as the VIX crossing 22, switches several cards off at once.
- *Where the cards and the chapters disagree.* A swing card as a sizing sheet. The reader picks one of the four cards and enters the portfolio, the entry, the stop and, for an option, its delta. The sheet fills in the size from the card's risk percentage, the notional and its ratio to the portfolio, the targets in R and in price, and the date of the time stop. It shows the four cards producing very different notionals from the same 1% of risk, because their stops sit at different distances.
- *Risk overlay cards.* An event-overlay calculator. The reader sets the portfolio, the normal risk per trade, the stop distance and the implied move of a coming FOMC meeting or earnings report. The figure applies the overlay's rules, halving the size, widening the stop, testing the veto and capping the position so that the implied move costs no more than 2% of the portfolio, and draws the position before and after. It shows how much smaller a trade becomes inside an event window.
- *Card 01: long equity.* Card 01's kill rule through time. Two synthetic lines, a cyclically adjusted earnings yield and a 10-year yield, run across the years with their gap shaded and a line at −1%, above bars for 12-month earnings growth. The reader sets how many months count as "sustained", and the rule lights only where the gap stays below −1% while earnings growth is negative. It shows that the rule needs both conditions at once, and how the definition of "sustained" moves the dates on which it fires.
- *Card 08: low volatility.* An equity sleeve built from the cards. A stacked bar divides a sleeve the reader sizes among QUAL, MTUM, a value fund, USMV and the index, with each tilt's slider limited to its card's range. Bands mark the totals of Chapter 10F, 25% to 40%, and Chapter 10A, 15% to 30%. A second bar applies each card's kill rule to a scenario the reader picks, such as a V-shaped rally, and shows which tilts would be cut.
- *Card X10: revenge trading after a loss.* Temptation against evidence. The ten do-not-trade cards laid out as a grid, each showing its pitch on the front. The reader turns a card to see the number that rejects it: 49 bps gone after 2015, 7% shrunk below 1%, a negative mean behind a positive median, 0.2% of liquidity, 690% against 5,500%, VXX at −99.84%. A counter records how many pitches the reader would have accepted before turning the card.
- *The whole framework on one card.* A funnel of 100 trade ideas. The reader sets how many pass each gate: a known source and category, the retail haircut, the conviction score, the stress test and the loss budget. The funnel narrows to the ideas that reach a card, about 20 at the Lab's figure, and a readout contrasts the capital each surviving idea receives with an equal split across all 100.

## 13G · The portfolio

- *The reference allocation.* Capital against risk. Two bars divide the same portfolio, one by dollars and one by share of volatility, with a band for each sleeve's range in the reference table. The reader sets each sleeve's dollars, volatility and correlation with equity, and the risk bar redraws. It shows a calm sleeve taking little risk for its dollars, and a sleeve that moves with equity taking more risk than its volatility alone suggests.
- *Sizing from a stress loss.* A risk-contribution calculator. The reader sets the dollars, volatility and pairwise correlations of up to five sleeves, and a stacked bar shows each sleeve's share of risk beside its share of dollars. A button solves for the dollar weights that give equal contributions, and a second for the Edge Book's pragmatic target. It shows how a sleeve's correlation with equity changes its share of risk as much as its own volatility does.
- *What "survivable" means.* The stress table as a live grid. Rows are sleeves and columns scenarios, with each cell editable, and a toggle replaces the Edge Book's allowances with values computed from the cards: contracts times the put's floor for the VRP, the card's ranges for trend and carry. Totals, the gain needed and the years to recover at a rate the reader sets update beneath. It shows which rows drive the S3 loss, and how much the answer depends on trend paying in a crash.
- *The gut check.* The gut check as a path. A portfolio path falls to S3's loss of 30% and then takes one more 10% day, with the gain needed to recover drawn beside it. The reader sets the extra fall and a panic threshold, and a second line follows an investor who sells at the threshold and buys back a year later. It compares the cost of exiting near the bottom with the cost of sizing 25% smaller from the start.
- *The behavioral cooling-off period.* Three levels of kill criteria along a crash. A synthetic path of the portfolio and each sleeve falls through S1 to S3 and recovers, with the three levels drawn as lanes of lights beneath: each strategy's own criterion, the category rules and the portfolio's 25% line. The reader sets the depth of the crash and the sleeves' sizes, and sees which lights fire, in what order, and which sleeves keep running mechanically through the halt.
- *What good looks like.* Where the variance of P&L comes from. A bar splits a year of monthly P&L into intended strategies, execution friction and unplanned trades, with the Edge Book's bands of 85% to 95%, 5% to 15% and near zero marked. The reader sets each part's standard deviation and their correlations, and the shares update. It shows how quickly a few unplanned trades push the intended share below 85%.
- *The selectivity principle.* Selectivity as a funnel over a year. Each trading day produces a handful of observations, and the reader sets the share that passes each gate: interpretation, the conviction score and sizing. A calendar heat map shows the days with a new position, mostly blank, beside a second calendar for a trader with no gates. A readout compares the two books' trade counts and, for costs the reader sets, what trading costs each one.

## 13H · Managing total vega

- *Adding up the book's vega.* Estimating a VIX beta. A scatter of daily changes in a stock's at-the-money implied volatility against daily changes in the VIX, with a fitted line whose slope is the stock's VIX beta. The reader picks a stock from a menu of synthetic examples and a window of 60 to 500 days, and sees the slope and its spread, with the Lab's range for that kind of stock shaded. It shows why a beta is an estimate with its own uncertainty, and why earnings days pull a single name's points off the line.
- *How much is too much.* A book's vega by position. Horizontal bars show each position's beta-adjusted vega, stacked into the aggregate, with the band from $500 to $1,500 per point shaded. The reader adds a position a month from a menu, such as one iron condor, and watches the stack cross the band while each bar stays small. A second panel shows the P&L of the whole book along a VIX path the reader draws, beside the sum of the positions' stop budgets.
- *Hedging short vega: the macro vega overlay.* Three hedges side by side. For a book of short vega the reader sets, bars compare a half hedge built from VIX calls 90 to 180 days out, from SPX puts struck at 90% to 92% and from SPX spreads 60 to 90 days out: its cost, the vega it adds and the delta it brings. A path of the VIX and SPX that the reader draws then shows each hedged book's P&L through a spike. It makes plain why the puts suit a book already long delta and the calls a book that is not.
- *Common failure modes.* Two VIX calls through a slow stress. The value of a VIX call expiring in 3 weeks and of one expiring in 4 months, both struck at 25, drawn along a VIX path that drifts sideways for a month and then spikes. The reader sets the timing of the spike, and sees the short-dated call expire almost worthless before the stress arrives while the long-dated one still pays.
- *The trade journal.* A month of journal entries as a review screen. Each trade is a row with its setup, regime, conviction score, R-multiple and slippage, and the reader sorts and groups them. Bars summarize the average R-multiple by setup and by regime, and flag trades whose pre-trade fields were filled in after entry. It shows the monthly review surfacing the one setup that loses money in a weak regime.
