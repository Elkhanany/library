Up to this point the book has described what the dealer does. This chapter answers a deeper question: why the dealer's delta-hedging strategy is the unique correct strategy, and what mathematical structure its hedging P&L actually has. It is the most technical chapter in the book, and also the one that justifies every claim that follows.

If mathematics is not your native language, read slowly and accept the steps one at a time. Each formula is followed by what it says in plain words, while the heaviest algebra sits in boxes you can open or skip. The payoff for that patience is large. By the end of Part VII, one idea organizes every options strategy: every delta-hedged option position is, fundamentally, a bet on realized volatility against implied volatility, and nothing else.

::: plain
A dealer who holds an option and the right number of shares against it has a book that does not care which way the market moves over the next instant. A book with no risk must earn what cash earns, or else someone could borrow cash, buy the book and keep the difference without risk. That one requirement, no free money, pins down the option's price. What is left of the book's profit and loss is gamma's gain from the size of each move, set against theta's charge for the passing of time.
:::

## Five steps from a hedged book to an equation

The derivation runs in five steps, each building on the last and each doing one specific piece of intellectual work:

1. **A model for prices:** assume the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion (GBM).
2. **Itô's lemma:** derive how the option's value changes when the price moves at random.
3. **The hedged book:** build the delta-hedged portfolio, and see that it has no random part.
4. **No arbitrage:** a portfolio with no risk over the next instant must earn the risk-free rate.
5. **The Black-Scholes-Merton equation:** write down what that demands of the option's value, a consistency condition.

After the five steps comes the gamma-theta P&L decomposition that Chapter 5C previewed, which drives everything in the dealer-mechanics framework. The chapter ends by connecting option replication to variance swaps and to the way the VIX is built, the bridge to Part IX.

## A model for prices

Black and Scholes (1973) and, independently, Merton (1973) assumed that the underlying's price follows a **[[geometric Brownian motion]]**. That phrase rests on two older ideas: the random walk, and the Brownian motion it becomes when its steps grow very small.

### From coin flips to Brownian motion

A **[[random walk]]** (Chapter 3B) is a path built from independent steps. The plainest version flips a fair coin at each step, moving up by \(h\) on heads and down by \(h\) on tails. Each step has mean zero and variance \(h^2\). Because the steps are independent, their variances add, so after \(n\) steps the position has mean zero and variance \(n h^2\). Its standard deviation is \(h\sqrt{n}\), the square-root-of-time rule of Chapter 1A.

Now let each step take \(\Delta t\) years, so that \(n\) steps cover \(t = n\,\Delta t\) years. To give the walk a volatility \(\sigma\), its variance after one year must be \(\sigma^2\) however finely the year is sliced, which fixes the size of each step:

\[ h = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} \]
{#eq:step}

With this step, the variance after \(t\) years is \(n\sigma^2\Delta t = \sigma^2 t\), whatever \(\Delta t\) is. The step must shrink like the *square root* of the time it takes, not like the time itself. That one scaling rule is the source of everything unusual in this chapter.

::: worked Why the step shrinks like a square root
**Step 1: the right scaling, the square root.** Take \(\sigma = 0.20\) and monthly steps, so that \(\Delta t = 1/12\) and [eq:step] gives \(h = 0.20 \times 0.289 = 0.0577\). Twelve such steps give a standard deviation of \(0.0577 \times \sqrt{12} = 0.20\). With daily steps, \(\Delta t = 1/252\) and \(h = 0.0126\), and 252 of them give \(0.0126 \times 15.87 = 0.20\) again.

**Step 2: the wrong scaling, in proportion to time.** Suppose instead that the step shrank in proportion to its time, \(h = \sigma\,\Delta t\). Monthly steps of 0.0167 give a year's standard deviation of \(0.0167 \times 3.46 = 0.058\). Daily steps of 0.00079 give only \(0.00079 \times 15.87 = 0.013\). The finer you slice, the less random the year becomes, until the walk is a straight line.
:::

As \(\Delta t\) shrinks toward zero with the right scaling, the coin-flip walk converges to a **[[Brownian motion]]**, written \(W_t\). The sum of many small independent steps is close to normal, whatever each step looks like, so the coin flips disappear into a bell curve. A Brownian motion has four defining properties:

- It starts at zero: \(W_0 = 0\).
- Its change over any interval of length \(\Delta t\) is normally distributed, with mean zero and variance \(\Delta t\).
- Its changes over intervals that do not overlap are independent of each other.
- Its path is continuous, with no jumps.

The path is continuous but so jagged that it has no slope at any point: zoom in on any stretch and it looks as rough as the whole. The notation \(dW\) means its change over a very short time \(dt\). That change is normal, with mean zero and variance \(dt\), so its typical size is \(\sqrt{dt}\). For short times \(\sqrt{dt}\) is far larger than \(dt\): one trading day is \(dt = 1/252 = 0.0040\) of a year, but \(\sqrt{dt} = 0.063\), almost 16 times larger.

Adding a steady trend gives **Brownian motion with drift**, \(X_t = a\,t + b\,W_t\): a line of slope \(a\) with noise of volatility \(b\) wrapped around it. Chapter 1D used it for the logarithm of an account's wealth, which drifts at the growth rate \(G\) with volatility \(\sigma\).

A readout gives the standard deviation of the year-end value across 1,000 paths, which stays at 0.20 as the steps multiply while the paths turn from staircases into rough curves. ### Geometric Brownian motion

Geometric Brownian motion applies the noise to the price in proportion to the price:

\[ dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW \]
{#eq:gbm}

In words: in a small instant of time \(dt\), the price \(S\) changes by a deterministic amount, \(\mu S\,dt\), called the **drift**, and a random amount, \(\sigma S\,dW\), called the **diffusion**. Here \(\mu\) is the expected return per year and \(\sigma\) the volatility. By construction, \(dW\) is normally distributed with mean zero and variance \(dt\).

Multiplying the noise by \(\sigma S\) scales it by the current price and the volatility. That makes volatility a percentage per unit of time rather than a number of dollars per unit of time. It matches what you see in markets, where volatility scales with the price level: a $1,000 stock has bigger dollar moves than a $10 stock at the same volatility. Dividing both sides by \(S\) gives the form Chapter 1A used, \(dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW\). The simple return over the instant is a drift plus normal noise.

This is the **central modeling assumption** of the entire Black-Scholes framework. Prices wiggle in proportion to themselves, and log returns are normally distributed in continuous time. The model is wrong: real returns have fat tails, as Chapter 1B established. But it is wrong in ways that can be corrected at the margins without rebuilding the whole framework. For establishing why dealers hedge the way they do, geometric Brownian motion is fine.

One subtlety needs care: the parameter \(\mu\) is the *instantaneous expected return* in simple-return space, not in log-return space. The expected log return is \(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\), the variance drag of Chapter 1A, which Step 2 derives. The two parameters live in different return conventions, both valid as long as you know which one you are using.

::: worked One day of drift against one day of noise
**Step 1: the drift over one trading day.** With \(S = 100\), \(\mu = 0.08\), \(\sigma = 0.20\) and \(dt = 1/252\), the drift is \(\mu S\,dt = 8/252 = 0.032\), about 3 cents.

**Step 2: the noise over the same day.** The random part has a standard deviation of \(\sigma S\sqrt{dt} = 20 \times 0.063 = 1.26\), about $1.26, so on a typical day the noise is 40 times the drift.

**Step 3: square both.** The drift squared is only \(0.032^2 = 0.0010\), while the noise squared averages its variance, \(1.26^2 = 1.59\), about 1,600 times larger. Step 2 of the derivation turns on this gap.
:::

## Itô's lemma

The option's value depends on the price and on time, \(V(S, t)\), so it moves whenever \(S\) moves. Ordinary calculus has a rule for this, the chain rule, which would give \(dV = (\partial V/\partial S)\,dS\) for the price's part. **That is wrong here**, because \(S\) is random. The correct rule is Itô's lemma, the chain rule for Brownian motion. It rests on one surprising fact: a squared move does not vanish.

### Why the square of a move does not vanish

For a smooth path, the change over a short time \(dt\) is proportional to \(dt\), so its square is proportional to \(dt^2\). Over a year cut into \(n\) steps, those squares add up to \(n \times (1/n)^2 = 1/n\), which vanishes as the steps shrink. That is why ordinary calculus drops every squared change.

A Brownian motion behaves differently, because each squared step \((\Delta W)^2\) has mean \(\Delta t\), the variance of the step. Add up the squared steps over a year, and the total has mean 1, a full year, however finely you slice. Its spread shrinks as the slicing gets finer, because the squared steps are independent: for normal steps, the total's standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2/n}\).

::: worked Adding up squared steps over one year
The table cuts one year into monthly, daily and five-minute steps (78 five-minute bars in a trading day, 252 trading days).

| Steps in the year | Sum of \((\Delta W)^2\): mean | Its standard deviation | Sum of \((\Delta t)^2\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 1 | 0.41 | 0.083 |
| 252 | 1 | 0.089 | 0.0040 |
| 19,656 | 1 | 0.010 | 0.00005 |

**Step 1: read the last column, the squared time steps.** They add up to \(1/n\), which vanishes as the steps multiply.

**Step 2: read the middle columns, the squared Brownian steps.** They add up to the elapsed time, 1 year, with a spread that falls from 41% to 1%, so that in the limit the sum *is* the elapsed time.
:::

The running sum of a path's squared changes is its **[[quadratic variation]]**, which for Brownian motion grows exactly like time. That result is usually written as three rules for products of small changes:

| Product | Becomes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| \(dt \times dt\) | 0 | summed over a year it is \(1/n\), which vanishes |
| \(dt \times dW\) | 0 | each is of size \(dt^{3/2}\), which also vanishes when summed |
| \(dW \times dW\) | \(dt\) | summed, it is the elapsed time |

Applied to [eq:gbm], the rules keep only the noise's contribution to the squared move:

\[ (dS)^2 = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt \]
{#eq:ds2}

The drift's square and the cross term vanish, as the worked example suggested: over one day the drift squared was 1,600 times smaller than the noise squared, a gap that widens as the interval shrinks.

[FIGURE IDEA: Three sums on one path. A single Brownian path over one year, with a slider for how finely it is sampled, from 12 to 20,000 steps. Three running totals are drawn beneath it: the sum of the squared changes, which settles on the elapsed time; the sum of the squared time steps, which sinks to zero; and the sum of the absolute changes, which grows without limit. It would make the rule that the square of dW is dt something the reader sees converge.]

::: familiar
The rule that \((dW)^2 = dt\) is the law of large numbers you rely on in every trial. One patient's response to a drug is unpredictable, but the response rate across 1,000 patients is pinned down tightly. Each squared step of a Brownian path is likewise a noisy measurement of the time it took, with a standard deviation about 1.4 times its mean. Add up thousands of them and the noise averages away, leaving the elapsed time. The relative spread falls as \(\sqrt{2/n}\), just as a sample mean's standard error falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
:::

### The chain rule with one more term

Chapter 5C approximated a change in value by delta times the move plus half of gamma times the move squared. Written for a function of both price and time, that second-order Taylor expansion is

\[
\begin{aligned}
dV \approx{}& \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}dS \\
&+ \tfrac12\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}(dS)^2
\end{aligned}
\]

plus terms in \(dt^2\), \(dt\,dS\) and higher powers, all of which vanish by the rules above. In ordinary calculus the \((dS)^2\) term vanishes too, but here, by [eq:ds2], it is \(\sigma^2S^2\,dt\), of the same order as \(dt\). Substituting it gives **[[Itô's lemma]]**, the chain rule for a random price:

\[
\begin{aligned}
dV ={}& \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}dS \\
&+ \tfrac12\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2\,dt
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:ito}

The extra term is there because \((dS)^2\) is not zero. For ordinary smooth functions, \((dx)^2\) is of order \(dt^2\), which you drop in the limit. For Brownian motion, the variance of \(dS\) over the interval \(dt\) is \(\sigma^2S^2\,dt\), of the same order as \(dt\) itself, which you **must** keep. The term \(\tfrac12(\partial^2V/\partial S^2)\,\sigma^2S^2\,dt\) is **gamma showing up explicitly**. It is the quadratic variation contribution that makes options different from linear instruments.

This is the central technical insight of stochastic calculus. The second derivative matters when the underlying is random, and it does not when the underlying is deterministic. Every "interesting" property of options derives from this single mathematical fact.

In the notation of Chapter 5C, where \(\partial V/\partial S = \Delta\), \(\partial^2V/\partial S^2 = \Gamma\) and \(\partial V/\partial t = \Theta\), Itô's lemma becomes

\[ dV = \Theta\,dt + \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\,dt \]
{#eq:itogreeks}

Read it this way: over a small interval, the option's value changes by a time-decay piece, \(\Theta\,dt\), a directional piece, \(\Delta\,dS\), which moves with the underlying, and a curvature piece, \(\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\,dt\). The curvature piece is always positive for long-option positions and reflects the convexity benefit.

::: worked Itô's lemma on the logarithm of the price
**Step 1: the derivatives of the logarithm.** Take \(V = \ln S\), which does not depend on time, so its only derivatives are the first, \(1/S\), and the second, \(-1/S^2\).

**Step 2: apply [eq:ito].** The curvature term is \(\tfrac12 \times (-1/S^2) \times \sigma^2S^2\,dt = -\tfrac12\sigma^2\,dt\), so \(d\ln S = dS/S - \tfrac12\sigma^2\,dt\). Substituting \(dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW\) from [eq:gbm] gives

\[ d\ln S = (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW \]

**Step 3: read it.** The log price is a Brownian motion with drift \(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\): the variance drag of Chapter 1A, now exact. With \(\mu = 8\%\) and \(\sigma = 20\%\), the log price grows at \(0.08 - 0.02 = 6\%\) a year. The ordinary chain rule, which drops the curvature term, would have said 8%.
:::

## The hedged book

Now build the dealer's book. Suppose the dealer has bought an option from a customer and hedges it by selling \(\Delta\) shares of the underlying, the [[delta hedging]] of Chapter 6C. The book is worth

\[ \Pi = V - \Delta S \]
{#eq:book}

where \(\Delta = \partial V/\partial S\) is set at the start of the instant and held fixed through it. Over the instant the short shares lose \(\Delta\,dS\), so the book changes by \(d\Pi = dV - \Delta\,dS\). When [eq:itogreeks] is substituted for \(dV\), its \(\Delta\,dS\) meets the \(-\Delta\,dS\) of the shares and cancels it:

\[ d\Pi = (\Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2)\,dt \]
{#eq:dpi}

Look at what just happened: **the \(dS\) terms cancelled.** The book's change has no random component, so over the instant it is risk-free. That is what delta hedging accomplishes: it removes the \(dW\) term and leaves only the deterministic \(dt\) terms.

This is the mathematical statement of what Chapter 6C said informally: a delta-hedged book is, at the instant of the hedge, directionless. The \(dW\) noise from the underlying has been hedged away. The remaining motion of the book is entirely deterministic, driven by theta and gamma alone, at first order.

Two remarks keep this honest. The first is that "at the instant" matters: delta changes as soon as \(S\) moves, so the hedge must be reset continually, which is gamma's chase in Chapter 6D. The second is that a dealer who *sold* the option holds \(-\Pi\), long \(\Delta\) shares against a short option. Every sign flips, while the argument that follows is unchanged.

The sum crosses zero at moves of plus and minus σ√dt, marked as the breakeven, showing the random part cancelled and only a bet on the size of the move left.]

## No free money

A book that is risk-free over the next instant must earn the risk-free rate \(r\), otherwise there is free money. This is the **no-arbitrage condition**, the most consequential single principle in derivatives pricing. If the hedged book earned more than \(r\), a trader could borrow at \(r\), buy the book and keep the difference with no risk, an [[arbitrage]]. If the book earned less, the trader would short it and lend the proceeds at \(r\). Either trade pushes prices until the gap closes, so

\[ d\Pi = r\,\Pi\,dt \]
{#eq:noarb}

The book earns the risk-free rate on its current value. Combining this with [eq:dpi], and writing \(\Pi = V - \Delta S\):

\[ (\Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2)\,dt = r(V - \Delta S)\,dt \]

Cancel \(dt\) from both sides and rearrange:

\[ \Theta + \tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2 = rV - r\Delta S \]
{#eq:greekpde}

## The Black-Scholes-Merton equation

Write the Greeks back out as derivatives and move \(r\Delta S\) to the left:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \\
&\quad + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = rV
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:bsm}

This is the celebrated **Black-Scholes-Merton [[partial differential equation]]** (PDE). A partial differential equation is a relation among a function's partial derivatives. Its solution is not a number but a whole function, here the option's value at every price and time, fixed once you say what it must equal at expiry. Robert Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this work, jointly with Myron Scholes. Fischer Black, who would have shared the prize, had died in 1995.

::: worked The reference call satisfies the equation
Chapter 5C's reference call has \(S = K = 100\), 30 days to expiry, \(\sigma = 0.25\), \(r = 0.04\) and no dividend. Its value is 3.0211, its delta 0.53256, its gamma 0.055476 and its theta −19.3457 a year.

**Step 1: the time term.** The first term of [eq:bsm] is theta itself, \(-19.3457\).

**Step 2: the delta term.** The second term, the interest on the shares the delta represents, is \(rS\Delta = 0.04 \times 100 \times 0.53256 = 2.1302\).

**Step 3: the gamma term.** The third term, gamma's curvature, is \(\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = 0.5 \times 0.0625 \times 10{,}000 \times 0.055476 = 17.3363\).

**Step 4: compare the sides.** The left side sums to \(-19.3457 + 2.1302 + 17.3363 = 0.1208\), exactly the right side, \(rV = 0.04 \times 3.0211 = 0.1208\), to four decimals.
:::

With a dividend yield \(q\), the short shares in the book pay out \(q\,\Delta S\,dt\) over each instant. Carrying that cost through Steps 3 and 4 turns the term \(rS\,\partial V/\partial S\) into \((r - q)S\,\partial V/\partial S\). That is the version whose solution carries the factor \(e^{-qT}\) of Chapter 5C's formula.

### A consistency condition, not a formula

The PDE is not a pricing formula but a **consistency condition**: any option price function \(V(S, t)\) that does not satisfy it creates an arbitrage. Solve it with the condition that at expiry, with no time left, a call is worth \(\max(S - K, 0)\). The solution is the Black-Scholes call formula of Chapter 5C, with \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) as defined there:

\[ C = S e^{-qT}N(d_1) - K e^{-rT}N(d_2) \]

More profoundly, the PDE encodes the **recipe** by which a dealer can replicate the option using only the underlying and cash. The dealer is not "pricing the option" in some metaphysical sense but **manufacturing** it through dynamic hedging. The shares and cash, adjusted as the price moves, form a **[[replicating portfolio]]** that ends with exactly the option's payoff.

This is the deepest insight in the entire framework. An option's payoff looks as if it needs unusual cleverness to construct, because it is asymmetric and convex. In fact it can be built from a simple time-varying position in the underlying plus cash. The cost of building it is exactly the Black-Scholes price, no more and no less, under the model's assumptions. Any deviation from that price creates an arbitrage.

The practical implication is that when you buy an option, you are buying the right to a payoff that the dealer constructs for you through their hedging. The premium you pay is the cost of the construction kit, plus the dealer's margin, plus compensation for residual risks the model does not capture: jump risk, transaction costs and fat tails. The [[variance risk premium]] of Chapter 5E is the cumulative spread between what dealers charge and what the model says is fair. It is the structural compensation dealers require to be in this business.

### Where the expected return went

Look again at [eq:bsm]: the expected return \(\mu\) appears nowhere in it. It was in the price model, [eq:gbm], and it reached Itô's lemma through \(dS\). It disappeared in Step 3, when the hedge cancelled the \(dS\) terms, the only route by which the drift could reach the book. So the option's price does not depend on how fast anyone expects the underlying to grow.

That settles the question Chapter 4B left open: which probabilities turn an expected payoff into a price. Because \(\mu\) does not matter, you may price as if the underlying grew at the risk-free rate, then discount the expected payoff at that rate. Probabilities chosen that way are the **[[risk-neutral probabilities|risk-neutral probability]]** of Chapter 5C.

::: worked One step of replication
**Step 1: the setup.** Take Chapter 4B's toy, with \(r = 0\) to keep the arithmetic clean: an underlying at 100 ends at 110 or 90, where a call struck at 100 pays 10 or 0.

**Step 2: build the replica.** Hold \(a\) shares and \(b\) dollars of cash, and require the replica to pay what the call pays in both outcomes: \(110a + b = 10\) and \(90a + b = 0\). Subtracting gives \(20a = 10\), so \(a = 0.5\) and \(b = -45\), a loan of $45. The share count \(a\) is the call's delta over the step.

**Step 3: its cost.** The replica costs \(0.5 \times 100 - 45 = 5\) today, so the call must also cost 5, or buying one and selling the other is an arbitrage.

**Step 4: the probabilities do not matter.** With a 50% chance of the rise, the expected payoff is 5, the price. With a 70% chance, the expected payoff is 7, but the replica still costs 5, so the price is still 5. The probability that makes the expected payoff equal the price is the one under which the underlying's expected value is today's price, which here is 50%: the risk-neutral probability.
:::

At each node the figure shows the shares and cash of the replica. A readout gives the replica's cost beside the Black-Scholes price, and the two converge as the steps multiply, while moving the real-world probability changes the expected payoff but not the cost.]

## The hedging P&L: gamma against theta

Now the PDE gives the most important formula in this book. Suppose the dealer delta-hedges continuously, but the realized volatility \(\sigma_R\) differs from the volatility used to price the option, the [[implied volatility]] at entry \(\sigma_I\). Over each instant, the book's gain above what the same money would earn as cash is approximately

\[
\begin{aligned}
d\Pi - r\Pi\,dt \approx{}& \tfrac12\Gamma S^2 \\
&\times \Big[\Big(\frac{dS}{S}\Big)^2 - \sigma_I^2\,dt\Big]
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:pnl}

where \(\Gamma\) is computed at the implied volatility.

::: grind Where the hedging P&L comes from
**Step 1: expand using the actual move.** The dealer values and hedges the option at \(\sigma_I\), so \(\Theta\), \(\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\) are the Black-Scholes values at \(\sigma_I\). Over the instant, the Taylor expansion gives \(dV \approx \Theta\,dt + \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^2\). Here \((dS)^2\) is the square of the move that actually happened, not its model average.

**Step 2: add the hedge and the financing.** The short shares cancel \(\Delta\,dS\), as in Step 3. Subtracting what the book would have earned as cash, \(r(V - \Delta S)\,dt\), leaves

\[
\begin{aligned}
d\Pi - r\Pi\,dt \approx{}& \tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^2 \\
&+ (\Theta - rV + r\Delta S)\,dt
\end{aligned}
\]

**Step 3: use the PDE at the implied volatility.** The option's price satisfies [eq:greekpde] with \(\sigma_I\) in place of \(\sigma\), which, rearranged, says \(\Theta - rV + r\Delta S = -\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma_I^2S^2\).

**Step 4: substitute and factor.** The second term of Step 2 becomes \(-\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma_I^2S^2\,dt\), so that factoring out \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\) gives [eq:pnl]. The only change from Step 3 of the derivation is that the squared move is now the realized one, not \(\sigma_I^2S^2\,dt\).
:::

Read [eq:pnl] this way: **over each interval, the delta-hedged dealer earns gamma times the difference between the realized squared return and the implied variance.** This is the entire economic content of a delta-hedged option position. If the squared returns average the implied variance, the bracket averages zero and the book earns nothing beyond cash. If they do not, the book earns or pays the difference, scaled by \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\), with these signs:

- If you are **long** the option (long gamma) and [[realized volatility]] **exceeds** implied, you make money. Every wiggle in spot lets you [[scalp gamma|gamma scalping]], buying low and selling high in your hedge, at a rate that in aggregate exceeds the theta you are paying.
- If you are long the option and realized falls **short** of implied, you lose, because you bought too much volatility.
- If you are **short** the option (short gamma, the dealer's typical position), the signs flip. You earn the implied volatility, as the theta you collect, and pay the realized volatility, as the rebalancing losses.

A short-gamma dealer **wants quiet markets**, while a long-gamma dealer **wants moves**.

::: worked One day, then sixty
Take the figure below at its starting settings: a long at-the-money option, strike 100 with spot at 100, 60 trading days to expiry, implied volatility 20% and a 4% rate. Its gamma is 0.0404, so \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2 = 202\) per share, with time counted in trading days, \(dt = 1/252\).

**Step 1: the breakeven move.** The bracket in [eq:pnl] is zero when \(|dS/S| = \sigma_I\sqrt{dt} = 0.20/15.87 = 1.26\%\). Days with larger moves pay the hedged long option, and days with smaller moves cost it.

**Step 2: a day at 30% realized volatility.** A typical day at 30% is a move of \(0.30/15.87 = 1.89\%\), which makes the bracket \(0.000357 - 0.000159 = 0.000198\). The day earns \(202 \times 0.000198 = 0.040\) a share, or $4.01 a contract of 100.

**Step 3: quieter days.** A 0.5% move gives \(202 \times (0.000025 - 0.000159) = -0.027\) a share, a loss of $2.70 a contract. A day with no move at all costs $3.21, the full implied variance of one day.

**Step 4: sixty days.** If \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\) stayed at 202, sixty typical days at 30% would earn about \(60 \times \$4.01 = \$241\). It does not stay there, because spot wanders away from the strike, where gamma is smaller. The figure's average over its 99 seeds is about $180, and every one of them ends positive.
:::

::: insight
Under the standard delta-hedged framework, an option position is, at first order, a bet on realized against implied volatility. It is the dominant P&L driver in the absence of large vega or skew dynamics.
:::

This is the punchline of the gamma-theta decomposition. Three choices set up the bet: strike selection affects the size of gamma, expiration selection affects its speed, and the implied volatility at entry sets the implied side. To first order, the resulting P&L is a function of whether realized exceeded implied.

### The honest caveats

The "and nothing else" framing skips several real-world second-order effects that matter in practice:

- **Vega risk on IV moves:** a delta-hedged position is *not* vega-neutral. If implied volatility changes between entry and exit, that mark-to-market [[vega]] P&L is a separate component on top of the gamma-against-realized term. For long-dated options with large vega, it can dominate.
- **Discrete hedging:** the decomposition assumes continuous rebalancing, while real hedging happens in steps. The *path* of spot between rebalances adds variance around the theoretical gamma-scalping P&L. Two traders running the same position with different hedge frequencies realize different P&L from the same gap between realized and implied.
- **Skew and surface dynamics:** options that sit on a steep part of the volatility surface accrue P&L from vanna and volga as spot and volatility move together. Those terms are not captured by the simple \(\Gamma \times (\sigma_R^2 - \sigma_I^2)\) decomposition, and Chapter 7B works them through.
- **Transaction costs:** hedging is not free, so the gamma-scalping P&L is *gross*. Net P&L can be meaningfully smaller, especially in less liquid underlyings or on short-dated options, where rebalancing is frequent.

For most retail-scale, short-to-medium-dated, large-cap delta-hedged positions, the first-order framing of realized against implied captures the bulk of P&L variability. For longer-dated positions, exotic surface trades or low-liquidity underlyings, the second-order terms matter and should be modeled explicitly.

### Hedging in steps, and paying for each step

The second and fourth caveats pull against each other, as Chapter 6E showed. Hedging more often tames the path's noise but pays the spread more often. The next figure puts both into one book. It holds long at-the-money calls on a $600 underlying and delta-hedges them along one simulated path of five-minute bars, 78 to a trading day. The path's volatility equals the implied volatility, so hedging continuously earns nothing on average before costs.

Four schedules hedge the same path: on every bar (the row labelled continuous), five times a day, at each daily close and every three days. On each bar, a schedule's P&L adds up three things. The first is the gamma-theta term of [eq:pnl]. The second is the delta that the stale hedge leaves open, the calls' current delta less the hedge held, times the bar's move. The third is the cost of any rebalance, including the first hedge: the shares traded, times the price, times the transaction cost. Hedging on every bar leaves no delta open, so that schedule's curve is the pure gamma-theta P&L less its costs. Sliders set the implied vol (5% to 80%, starting at 20%), the days to expiry (5 to 90, starting at 30) and the number of contracts (1 to 500, starting at 100). Two more set the transaction cost (0 to 20 basis points, starting at 2) and the seed (1 to 500, starting at 7). Below the chart, a table gives each schedule's final P&L. Set the cost to zero and step through seeds, then raise the cost.

[FIGURE: hedging-friction-simulator]

At the starting settings, the 100 calls carry about 5,340 shares of delta. Hedging on every bar rebalances 2,339 times and ends about $21,500 down, of which about $19,400 is transaction cost. Five hedges a day end about $15,200 down, the daily close about $11,500 up and every three days about $4,500 up. All four share the same gamma-theta term, a loss of about $2,100 on this path, so everything else in each result is open delta and cost.

One seed proves little, so look across all 500 of the figure's seeds. At zero cost the four schedules each average close to zero, while the spread of final P&L widens as hedging gets rarer. Its standard deviation is about $3,100 hedging every bar, $9,600 at five a day, $20,600 daily and $35,600 every three days. At 2 basis points the average cost runs the other way: about $19,000, $5,400, $2,700 and $1,800.

Both patterns follow square-root rules in the hedging interval \(\delta t\). Over one interval the move, and with it the open delta, grows like \(\sqrt{\delta t}\), which makes the error the interval adds grow like \(\delta t\). Summed over \(T/\delta t\) independent intervals, the total error grows like \(\sqrt{\delta t}\). Hedging five times less often gives about \(\sqrt5 = 2.2\) times the spread, as from $9,600 to $20,600. The shares traded at each rebalance also grow like \(\sqrt{\delta t}\), but the number of rebalances falls like \(1/\delta t\), so the total cost grows like \(1/\sqrt{\delta t}\) as the interval shrinks. Hedging 15.6 times as often, 78 bars a day against 5, should cost about \(\sqrt{15.6} = 3.9\) times as much, and the figure gives 3.5.

No schedule wins on both counts: the spread falls as the interval shrinks while the cost rises. That is the trade-off of Chapter 6E, which finds where the net result is best for an SPX book.

## Why the dealer feedback loop is built into the maths

This is also the punchline for why aggregate dealer positioning moves the market. Suppose dealers are collectively short gamma. By [eq:pnl] they are mechanically losing money to volatility, and the more volatility there is, the more they lose. To minimize losses they hedge aggressively, which means buying as the market rises and selling as it falls. **That hedging creates volatility**, which makes them lose more, which makes them hedge more.

The feedback loop is mathematically built into the gamma-theta decomposition just derived. It is not a story or a metaphor: it falls out of the PDE. Chapter 8B formalizes it into the effective-volatility equation, which quantifies how dealer feedback amplifies or dampens the underlying's volatility depending on the sign of aggregate dealer gamma.

## The variance swap connection

Add up [eq:pnl] over the life of the option. Each instant's gain is weighted by \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\), which changes as spot and time move. Now suppose you held a position whose gamma falls like \(1/S^2\) as spot rises, so that \(\Gamma S^2\) stays at a constant, \(g\). The weight then comes out of the sum. Write \(P\) for the book's total gain above cash over a life of \(T\) years:

\[
\begin{aligned}
P &\approx \tfrac{g}{2}\Big[\sum\Big(\frac{dS}{S}\Big)^2 - \sigma_I^2 T\Big] \\
&= \tfrac{g}{2}\,T\,(\sigma_R^2 - \sigma_I^2)
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:varswap}

Here \(\sigma_R^2 = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum (dS/S)^2\) is the realized variance, annualized. A payoff proportional to realized variance less a fixed number is exactly a **[[variance swap]]** (Chapter 4B). So integrating the hedging P&L over the life of the option and weighting by \(1/S^2\) gives, almost exactly, the payoff of a variance swap. This is not coincidence but the foundation of model-free variance replication, and the reason the VIX (Chapter 9A) is constructed as a weighted strip of options with \(1/K^2\) weights.

Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou's 1999 Goldman Sachs paper, "More Than You Ever Wanted to Know About Volatility Swaps", showed how. A portfolio of options weighted by \(\Delta K/K^2\) across all strikes, where \(\Delta K\) is the spacing between strikes, replicates the payoff of a variance swap when it is dynamically delta-hedged. The intuition is that weighting by \(1/K^2\) creates a "gamma flat" portfolio. Its \(\Gamma S^2\), which turns a squared return into dollars, is the same wherever spot is. That is exactly the property required to replicate realized variance.

::: worked A strip with flat dollar gamma
Take options 30 days from expiry at 20% implied volatility and a zero rate, one at every strike from 40 to 300, $1 apart.

**Step 1: a single strike at 100.** An option struck at 100 has \(\Gamma S^2\) of 695 with spot at 100, but only 122 at spot 90 and 0.32 at spot 80. Its sensitivity to squared returns lives near its strike.

**Step 2: equal weights at every strike.** One option at every strike gives \(\Gamma S^2\) of 6,421 at spot 80, 10,033 at 100 and 14,447 at 120. The sensitivity is spread out now, but it grows with spot.

**Step 3: weights of 1/K².** Holding \(1/K^2\) of each option gives \(\Gamma S^2 = 1.000\) at spot 80, at 100 and at 120. Every squared return then counts the same wherever spot has wandered, which is what a variance swap pays for.
:::

This is the mathematical thread connecting dealer hedging to the VIX. The VIX is, fundamentally, the cost of replicating variance through delta-hedged options. Every option you trade contributes some amount of replicated variance to the dealer's book. Aggregate dealer gamma is, essentially, the aggregate variance-replication exposure of the entire options ecosystem. Chapter 9A returns to this connection in detail.

::: practice
- **An unhedged option is not this bet:** a short put you hold without a hedge makes or loses money mostly on direction, through delta. The formula [eq:pnl] describes only the part of its P&L that a hedge would leave.
- **Know the breakeven move before you trade:** it is \(\sigma_I/\sqrt{252}\) a day, 1.26% at 20% implied volatility. A seller of hedged options profits when the squared daily moves average less than its square, and a buyer when they average more.
- **Count the rebalancing costs:** a retail account that hedges a short straddle with MES futures (Chapter 2A) pays the spread at every adjustment. Hedging less often saves costs but widens the spread of outcomes, as the friction figure showed.
- **Know what a bought option costs you:** its premium pays for the dealer's construction, their margin and the residual risks they carry. The variance risk premium (Chapter 10C) is what that costs a buyer on average.
:::

::: warn When it fails
The derivation assumes geometric Brownian motion with one constant volatility, continuous hedging at no cost, and a price that never jumps. When the price gaps, a hedge set before the gap is wrong after it, and no rebalancing can follow a move that happens all at once. The dealer then carries the jump risk that the premium was meant to cover. Implied volatility also moves, which is the vega caveat, and the surface moves with spot, which Chapter 7B takes up. Finally, the no-arbitrage step needs someone able to borrow, lend and trade at the model's prices. When capital is scarce, the limits to arbitrage of Chapter 1G can hold prices away from the model's for some time.
:::

::: rule
- **Treat any option you hedge as a volatility trade:** take it only when your forecast of realized volatility over its life differs from the implied volatility by more than your hedging costs. Go long gamma if you expect more realized volatility, short if less.
- **Size a short-gamma book for the large days, not the average day:** by [eq:pnl], a day at twice the breakeven move costs three days of the implied variance the book collects, and a day at three times costs eight.
- **Choose a hedging interval deliberately:** hedging less often widens the spread of outcomes roughly with the square root of the interval, and hedging more often raises costs with one over that square root (Chapter 6E).
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "delta-neutral means no risk"
A delta-hedged book has no risk from direction only for an instant. Over a day its P&L is [eq:pnl], which can be large. A short-gamma book loses eight days' worth of collected implied variance on a single day that moves three times the breakeven. Delta-neutral means the bet has changed from direction to volatility, not that the bet has gone.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading the PDE as a forecast
The Black-Scholes-Merton equation says nothing about where the price goes, because the expected return has dropped out of it. Risk-neutral probabilities are pricing weights, not forecasts. The \(N(d_2)\) of Chapter 5C is the chance of a call finishing in the money in a world where the underlying grows at the risk-free rate, not in the real one.
:::

::: recap
- A Brownian motion's change over a time \(dt\) is normal with variance \(dt\), so its squared changes add up to the elapsed time. That is the rule \((dW)^2 = dt\), while \((dt)^2\) vanishes.
- Itô's lemma is the chain rule plus one term, \(\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2\,dt\): gamma earning from the size of moves.
- A hedge of \(\Delta\) shares cancels the random part of the book, and no arbitrage makes the book earn the risk-free rate. Together they give the Black-Scholes-Merton equation, a consistency condition whose solution is the Black-Scholes formula and in which \(\mu\) does not appear.
- A continuously hedged book earns \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2[(dS/S)^2 - \sigma_I^2\,dt]\) above cash: at first order a bet on realized against implied, with vega, discrete hedging, surface dynamics and costs as the caveats.
- Weighted by \(1/K^2\), a strip of hedged options has a flat \(\Gamma S^2\) and replicates a variance swap, the construction behind the VIX.
:::

::: check
?? Why must a random walk's step shrink like \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), rather than like \(\Delta t\), for the walk to become Brownian motion?
!! A year of \(n = 1/\Delta t\) independent steps of size \(h\) has variance \(n h^2 = h^2/\Delta t\). For that to stay at \(\sigma^2\) as the steps shrink, \(h^2\) must equal \(\sigma^2\Delta t\), so \(h = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\). With \(h = \sigma\Delta t\) instead, the variance is \(\sigma^2\Delta t\), which vanishes, and the walk becomes a straight line.

?? Apply Itô's lemma to \(V = S^2\), and find \(dV\).
!! Here \(\partial V/\partial S = 2S\), \(\partial^2V/\partial S^2 = 2\) and \(\partial V/\partial t = 0\). By [eq:ito], \(dV = 2S\,dS + \sigma^2S^2\,dt\), so that substituting [eq:gbm] gives \(dV = (2\mu + \sigma^2)S^2\,dt + 2\sigma S^2\,dW\). The ordinary chain rule would miss the \(\sigma^2S^2\,dt\), which is why the square of the price drifts up faster than \(2\mu\).

?? Use [eq:bsm] to recover the reference call's theta from its other Greeks.
!! Rearranged, the equation gives \(\Theta = rV - rS\Delta - \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = 0.1208 - 2.1302 - 17.3363 = -19.3457\) a year. Divided by 365, that is a loss of about 5.3 cents a share a day, as Chapter 5C found by repricing.

?? A dealer is short the at-the-money option of the worked example, with \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2 = 202\) and implied volatility 20%. What does the day cost per contract if spot moves 2.52% today, twice the breakeven?
!! The bracket in [eq:pnl] is \(0.0252^2 - 0.04/252 = 0.000635 - 0.000159 = 0.000476\). For the long option that is a gain of \(202 \times 0.000476 = 0.096\) a share, so the short dealer loses about $9.62 a contract. That is three times the $3.21 of implied variance the short collects on a quiet day.

?? In the one-step tree, suppose the underlying ends at 120 or 90, with the strike at 100 and \(r = 0\). Price the call and find the risk-neutral probability of the rise.
!! The replica needs \(120a + b = 20\) and \(90a + b = 0\), so \(a = 2/3\), \(b = -60\) and the replica costs \(66.67 - 60 = 6.67\). The risk-neutral probability \(p\) makes the expected price 100: \(120p + 90(1 - p) = 100\), so \(p = 1/3\). The expected payoff at that probability is \(20/3 = 6.67\), the price.

?? Why does a strip weighted by \(1/K^2\) replicate a variance swap, while a single at-the-money option does not?
!! By [eq:pnl], each instant's gain is weighted by \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\). A single option's \(\Gamma S^2\) is large near its strike and tiny far from it, so its P&L depends on where spot happened to be when the big moves came. The \(1/K^2\) strip has the same \(\Gamma S^2\) at every spot, so every squared return counts equally, and the total is a fixed multiple of realized less implied variance.
:::
