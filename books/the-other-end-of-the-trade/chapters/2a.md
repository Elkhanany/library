Before Parts VI to IX describe the dealer's end of the trade, you need to be precise about what is being traded. People say "the S&P 500" as if it were a single object. On a screen it is at least six distinct products: an index level, an exchange-traded fund, a futures family and three different options markets. Each has its own settlement rules, contract size, tax treatment, dealer ecosystem and trading hours. Confusion between them produces a surprising amount of bad retail decision-making, including some of the most expensive mistakes an American retail trader can make.

Part II also covers the layer beneath the product: the **[[market microstructure]]** through which orders actually execute. Between your click in a brokerage app and a printed trade sits a routing decision. It determines who fills you, at what price, on what venue and at what cost. Understanding this layer separates retail traders who lose 50 [[basis points|basis point]] per [[round trip]] to invisible costs from those who lose 5. Together, what to trade and how to execute it set the operational baseline that every later strategy assumes.

The S&P 500 is a number: the market value of about 500 large companies, divided by a scaling factor that S&P adjusts whenever the list changes. You cannot buy that number directly, only one of the six contracts that pay according to it. They look alike on a chart and behave very differently in an account: how big one contract is, whether it ends in cash or in shares, when it can trade and how it is taxed.

## The index: a list and a formula

The S&P 500 is a list of approximately 500 large American companies, often called large-cap stocks, chosen by the S&P U.S. Index Committee. Its level, quoted under the ticker [[SPX]], summarizes what those companies' shares are worth. The committee admits a company on five criteria:

- **Size:** its **[[market capitalization]]**, the share price times the number of shares, must be at least $20.5 billion as of 2025. S&P raises this minimum periodically.
- **Domicile:** the company must be based in the United States.
- **Liquidity:** its shares must have a high public float and high turnover, so that they trade often and in size.
- **Financial viability:** its as-reported earnings must be positive in the most recent quarter and over the trailing year.
- **Public float:** at least 50% of its shares must be available to the public, as of 2025.

The committee meets quarterly to make additions and deletions, which take effect at rebalances on the third Friday of the rebalance month. Trading volume on those days has historically spiked as index-tracking funds adjust their holdings, because a fund that copies the index must buy every addition and sell every deletion.

### Weighting by float

The SPX level is a **float-adjusted, market-cap-weighted index**. Each company counts in proportion to the market value of its publicly tradeable shares, its **[[public float]]**. The float leaves out insider holdings, government holdings and other restricted shares, because those do not trade. Adding up these values and dividing by a number called the **[[divisor|index divisor]]** gives the level:

\[ \text{SPX}_t = \frac{\sum_i P_{i,t}\,F_{i,t}}{D_t} \]
{#eq:spx}

Here \(P_{i,t}\) is the price of company \(i\) at time \(t\), \(F_{i,t}\) its number of float shares, and \(D_t\) the divisor. The sum on top is the float-adjusted market capitalization of the whole index, in dollars. The divisor turns that dollar total into a level, and S&P updates it continuously.

::: worked A three-stock index
**Step 1: build the level.** Take a hypothetical index of three stocks:

| Stock | Price | Float shares | Float value | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $100 | 2,000 | $200,000 | 40% |
| B | $50 | 4,000 | $200,000 | 40% |
| C | $20 | 5,000 | $100,000 | 20% |

The float values add up to $500,000, so with a divisor of 500 the index stands at \(500{,}000/500 = 1{,}000\).

**Step 2: move one stock.** Stock A rises 10%, to $110. Its float value becomes $220,000 and the total $520,000, so the level is \(520{,}000/500 = 1{,}040\). The index rose 4%, which is A's 10% times A's 40% weight.
:::

That last step is general: while the divisor and the float shares stay fixed, the index return is the weighted average of the stocks' returns:

\[ R_{\text{index}} = \sum_i w_i\,R_i \]
{#eq:wavg}

Here \(R_i\) is the [[simple return]] of stock \(i\) over the period (Chapter 1A), and its weight \(w_i\) is its share of the index's total float value at the start. The largest companies therefore move the index most.

::: grind Why the index return is a weighted average
Write \(V_{i,t} = P_{i,t}F_i\) for stock \(i\)'s float value, with the float shares \(F_i\) and the divisor \(D\) fixed over the period. By [eq:spx], the divisor cancels from the index's growth factor:

\[ \frac{\text{SPX}_t}{\text{SPX}_{t-1}} = \frac{\sum_i V_{i,t}}{\sum_j V_{j,t-1}} \]

Multiply and divide each term on top by \(V_{i,t-1}\):

\[ \frac{\text{SPX}_t}{\text{SPX}_{t-1}} = \sum_i \frac{V_{i,t-1}}{\sum_j V_{j,t-1}}\cdot\frac{V_{i,t}}{V_{i,t-1}} \]

The first fraction in the sum is the weight \(w_i\), and the second is stock \(i\)'s [[growth factor]] \(1 + R_i\). The weights add up to 1, so subtracting 1 from both sides leaves [eq:wavg].
:::

## The divisor

The divisor has one purpose: to keep the index level continuous across structural events that would otherwise create artificial jumps. These are events that change the float-adjusted market capitalization without reflecting any real change in shareholder value:

- Index reconstitution, the committee's additions and deletions.
- **[[Spin-offs|spin-off]]**, in which a company hands its shareholders the shares of a subsidiary.
- Special dividends that effectively return capital outside the float.
- Other corporate actions of the same kind.

The divisor is the load-bearing piece nobody talks about. Three details about how it behaves are commonly misstated.

### Ordinary dividends do not change it

When a constituent pays an ordinary cash dividend, its stock price drops by roughly the dividend on the **[[ex-dividend date]]**, the first day a buyer no longer receives it. The index level drops accordingly, and the divisor stays put. This is why SPX is a **[[price-return index]]**: the drift from dividend payments is built into the published level. Total-return tracking lives in a separate index, SPTR, which accrues the dividends in a dividend index of its own.

In the three-stock index, suppose C pays a $1 dividend. Its price falls from $20 to $19 and its float value by $5,000, so the level falls from 1,000 to 990. The shareholders lost nothing: the $5,000 left the share price and arrived as cash. The price-return index records a 1% fall, while a **[[total-return index]]** would count the cash and record none.

### Ordinary splits do not change it either

A 2-for-1 **[[stock split]]** gives each shareholder two shares for every one held and halves the share price. In a float-cap-weighted index the float share count doubles as the price halves, so price times float shares is unchanged and the divisor is unaffected. The price-weighted Dow Industrials are different: that index adds up prices rather than values, so its divisor *must* change for every split.

In the toy index, a 2-for-1 split leaves A at $50 with 4,000 float shares, still $200,000 of value. A price-weighted index of the same three stocks adds up \(100 + 50 + 20 = 170\) dollars of price before the split and 120 after. To stay at the same level, its divisor must shrink by the same factor, \(120/170\).

### What does change it

Three kinds of event actually move the divisor:

- **Committee additions and deletions:** an addition increases the divisor, so that the index does not gap up. A deletion decreases it, so that the index does not gap down.
- **Spin-offs:** the parent's float market capitalization drops on the spin date, so the divisor drops to compensate.
- **Special dividends** large enough for S&P's methodology to classify them as a return of capital.

In each case, the new divisor is set so that the level is the same just after the event as just before it:

\[ D_{\text{new}} = D_{\text{old}} \times \frac{V_{\text{after}}}{V_{\text{before}}} \]
{#eq:divisor}

where \(V\) is the index's total float value, at unchanged prices, just before and just after the event.

::: worked Three events in the toy index
**Step 1: an addition.** The committee adds stock E, with a float value of $150,000. The total rises from $500,000 to $650,000, and [eq:divisor] raises the divisor from 500 to \(500 \times 650{,}000/500{,}000 = 650\). The level stays at \(650{,}000/650 = 1{,}000\) instead of gapping up to 1,300.

**Step 2: a deletion.** Start again, and let the committee delete stock C instead. The total falls to $400,000 and the divisor to 400, so the level stays at 1,000 instead of gapping down to 800.

**Step 3: a spin-off.** Start again, and let A spin off a subsidiary worth $20 a share, which does not join the index. A's price drops from $100 to $80 and its float value from $200,000 to $160,000. The divisor drops to \(500 \times 460{,}000/500{,}000 = 460\), and the level stays at 1,000.
:::

S&P discloses the divisor publicly, a number in the eight-billion range that drifts as the committee makes adjustments, but financial media rarely cite it. Without it, the SPX level would gap visibly every time the committee reconstituted the index or a constituent spun off a subsidiary, and there are dozens of those events a year. That the SPX level you saw yesterday is comparable in real economic terms with the level five years ago is entirely the divisor's job.

::: insight
The SPX index level is a real number, computed from prices. The SPX trade is a [[derivative|financial derivative]] contract on that number. The two are connected by a divisor that is, in some sense, the most important number in equity finance, and one that 99% of retail traders have never heard of.
:::

::: familiar
The divisor does for the index what a conversion factor does when a laboratory changes its assay. A patient's series of results stays comparable across the switch because the change of method is calibrated out, so that a change in the number reflects the patient. The divisor calibrates out changes in the index's membership, so that a change in the level reflects prices.
:::

The figure takes the toy index through each event in turn, always from its starting state. Watch the divisors: the split moves only the price-weighted twin's, while the addition, the deletion and the spin-off move both.

## One number, six products

Four of the six products are options or futures, which Part IV and Part V teach properly. For now you need three definitions:

- A **[[call option]]** gives its buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy the [[underlying]] at a fixed **[[strike price]]** on or before its **[[expiry]]**. A **[[put option]]** gives the right to sell at the strike instead. The buyer pays the seller a **[[premium]]** for that right (Chapter 5A).
- A **[[futures contract]]** is an agreement to buy or sell at a set price on a future date, with only a deposit paid up front (Chapter 4B).
- An **[[exchange-traded fund]]** (ETF) is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange like a stock. [[SPY]] is an ETF that tracks the S&P 500.

The same underlying exposure, the level of the S&P 500, is delivered through six different contracts. Each has its own price scaling, settlement convention, tax treatment, trading hours and dealer infrastructure. Confusion between these products is one of the most expensive sources of retail error. The first table gives each contract's terms.

| Product | Full name | Multiplier | Tick | Settlement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPX | S&P 500 cash index options | $100 × index level | $0.05 or $0.10 | Cash; AM (monthly) or PM (weekly, 0DTE) |
| XSP | Mini-SPX options | $100 × index level, one tenth of SPX | $0.05 or $0.10 | Cash; PM |
| SPY | S&P 500 ETF options | 100 shares | $0.01 or $0.05 | **Physical**: shares delivered |
| ES | E-mini S&P 500 futures | $50 per point | 0.25 point = $12.50 | Cash; quarterly (Mar, Jun, Sep, Dec) |
| MES | Micro E-mini futures | $5 per point | 0.25 point = $1.25 | Cash; quarterly |
| VIX | VIX options | $100 × index level | $0.05 | Cash; AM, Wednesday expiries |

The second table gives how each is taxed, when it trades and why it matters.

| Product | Tax | Hours, Eastern Time | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| SPX | 1256 60/40 | 9:30 to 4:15, plus extended | Largest contract size; institutional default; AM settlement has expiry-day quirks |
| XSP | 1256 60/40 | 9:30 to 4:15 | Retail-friendly; same tax treatment as SPX; cleaner for cash-secured strategies |
| SPY | **Ordinary**: short-term gains if held under 1 year | 9:30 to 4:00, plus extended | Most liquid options on earth by volume; physical settlement means assignment risk |
| ES | 1256 60/40 | Near 24 hours, Sunday to Friday | Cleanest leveraged equity exposure; financing built into the basis |
| MES | 1256 60/40 | Near 24 hours, Sunday to Friday | Retail-sized ES, one tenth the size, with the same tax treatment |
| VIX | 1256 60/40 | Standard, plus extended | European-style; settles to a special opening quotation on Wednesday morning, which differs from the VIX close |

A few terms in the tables need unpacking. The **[[multiplier|contract multiplier]]** converts a quoted price into dollars per contract. The **[[tick|tick size]]** is the smallest step in which the price can move. Settlement is how a contract is closed out at expiry, in cash or by delivering shares. "1256 60/40" refers to **[[Section 1256|Section 1256 contract]]** of the Internal Revenue Code: gains are taxed as 60% [[long-term|long-term capital gain]] and 40% [[short-term capital gains|short-term capital gain]], whatever the holding period (Chapter 2E).

Strictly, VIX options track the index's expected [[volatility]] rather than its level. The [[VIX]] is Cboe's index of the [[implied volatility]] of S&P 500 options over the next 30 days (Chapter 9A). It belongs in the table because it is built from SPX options and trades beside them. Cboe is the exchange that lists SPX, XSP and VIX options.

### What one contract controls

A contract's **[[notional value|notional]]** is the dollar exposure it controls:

\[ \text{notional} = m \times L \]
{#eq:notional}

Here \(m\) is the multiplier, in dollars per point, and \(L\) is the level of what the contract tracks. With SPX at 6,000, a single contract of each product represents:

- **One SPX option** represents $600,000 of notional exposure to SPX.
- **One [[XSP]] option** represents $60,000, exactly one tenth of that.
- **One SPY option** represents 100 shares of SPY at roughly $600 each, which is $60,000. SPY is designed to track one tenth of the SPX index level, so 1 SPY option is about 1 XSP option in notional. Operationally, though, the two are very different products.
- **One ES futures contract** represents $50 per point × 6,000, which is $300,000 of notional.
- **One MES futures contract** represents $5 per point × 6,000, which is $30,000.
- **One VIX option** represents $100 × the VIX level, around $1,500 if the VIX is at 15.

The same arithmetic gives the dollar value of one tick. One tick of ES is 0.25 point, or \(0.25 \times \$50 = \$12.50\) a contract, and one tick of MES is $1.25. An SPX option quoted in steps of $0.05 moves \(0.05 \times \$100 = \$5\) a contract per tick.

::: practice
Notional, not the number of contracts, sets a position's size against your account (Chapter 1E). On a $100,000 account, one SPX option controls 6 times the account, while one XSP option controls 0.6 times. A [[cash-secured put]] must hold the strike's full notional in cash: $600,000 for an [[at-the-money]] SPX put with the index at 6,000, and $60,000 for the XSP equivalent. Only the second fits the account at all.
:::

## Settlement: AM, PM and the special opening quotation

Two SPX options expiring on the same Friday can settle to *different* prices, because monthly SPX options expire AM while weekly and [[0DTE]] SPX options expire PM. AM settlement uses the **[[special opening quotation]]** (SOQ), a synthetic opening print built from the first reported trade of each constituent stock that morning. PM settlement uses the official 4:00 PM close. Times in this chapter are Eastern Time (ET), the time zone of the New York markets.

The AM-PM gap is small on most days and large on event Fridays. Suppose half a dealer's book is hedged against AM-settled SPX and the other half against PM-settled SPX. The dealer then carries intraday risk that the AM and PM prices differ. That risk is non-trivial on the monthly [[options expiration]] (OPEX) Friday, the third Friday of the month. It is one of the reasons OPEX Friday opens can show a sharp, uncorrelated move in the first ten minutes. Dealers are reconciling the SOQ they actually got with the SOQ their books were hedged against.

This mechanic is invisible on retail charts, but it is the canonical reason that "the first thirty minutes of OPEX Friday are weird". If you trade through OPEX morning, knowing why it is weird is the difference between being hit by it and trading around it.

### A level that no screen showed

A secondary effect comes from the way the SOQ is built. On a typical day, all 500 components open within a few seconds of 9:30. On a stressed day, with heavy overnight news, large global moves or a **[[circuit breaker]]** scenario, components can stagger their opens over many minutes. The resulting SOQ is a synthetic level that may differ measurably from any SPX value quoted at any given moment. Cash-settled options that reference the SOQ pay or receive cash on that synthetic value, not on the continuous SPX after the open.

::: worked An SOQ built from staggered opens
**Step 1: the opens.** Return to the three-stock index, which closed on Thursday at 1,000 with a divisor of 500. On a stressed Friday, A's first trade comes at 9:30, at $101, and C's at 9:31, at $20. B does not open until 9:41, when its first trade is at $48. By then A trades at $99 and C at $19.80.

**Step 2: the levels.** In this toy, a stock that has not yet traded counts at its previous close. The table gives the running index at two moments and the SOQ.

| | A | B | C | Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thursday's close | $100 | $50 | $20 | 1,000 |
| Running index at 9:31 | $101 | $50, not yet open | $20 | 1,004 |
| Running index at 9:41 | $99 | $48 | $19.80 | 978 |
| SOQ: each stock's first trade | $101 | $48 | $20 | 988 |

**Step 3: the settlement.** The SOQ row takes each first trade, whenever it happened: \(101 \times 2{,}000\) plus \(48 \times 4{,}000\) plus \(20 \times 5{,}000\) is $494,000 of float value, and dividing by 500 gives 988. No screen ever showed 988. A put struck at 990 settles 2 points in the money and pays \(2 \times \$100 = \$200\) a contract, where the 9:31 level would have paid nothing and the 9:41 level $1,200.
:::

Traders running expiration-day strategies who do not understand this can be surprised by a 30-cent or larger discrepancy between the settlement they expected and the one they got.

## Cash settlement against physical settlement

SPX, XSP, ES, MES and VIX all settle in cash, while SPY settles in shares. The difference is enormous for any strategy held to expiry.

**[[Cash settlement]]** means an [[in-the-money]] option simply credits or debits the cash difference between the strike and the settlement price. No shares are delivered, there is no [[exercise]] decision to make, and nothing is left open to gap between expiry and the next morning. You wake up on Monday with cash exactly equal to the in-the-money amount, or no change if the option expired [[out-of-the-money]].

**[[Physical settlement]]** means an in-the-money short option ends with shares changing hands at the strike. Being on the receiving end of an exercise is **[[assignment]]**. What changes hands depends on which side you wrote:

- **Short call assigned:** you must *deliver* 100 shares per contract at the strike. If the call was a **[[covered call]]**, because you own the shares, the shares are sold at the strike. If it was a **[[naked|naked option]]** call, you end up short the stock at the strike, exposed to overnight upside.
- **Short put assigned:** you must *receive* 100 shares per contract at the strike, which means buying them. If the put was cash-secured, your cash converts into stock at the strike. If it was a naked put in a [[margin]] account, the long stock consumes **[[buying power]]**.

Either way, you wake up to a position you did not have when you went to sleep, marked at whatever price the market reopens at. The gap between Friday's 4:00 PM mark and Monday's open is yours to absorb. For SPY it is typically small, a fraction of a percent on a normal weekend. On event weekends or during stress, the Monday gap can be 3% to 5% or more. Anyone running SPY short puts with a strike near Friday's close carries a weekend [[gap risk]] that was never priced into the trade.

::: worked The same put, settled two ways
**Step 1: the position.** You sold one SPY put struck at $600 for a hypothetical premium of $3, or $300 a contract. On expiry Friday, SPY closes at $599, $1 in the money.

**Step 2: physical settlement.** You are assigned and buy 100 shares for $60,000. Bad news breaks over the weekend, and SPY opens on Monday 3% lower, at $581.03. Your shares are worth $58,103: a loss of $1,897 against the strike, or $1,597 after the premium.

**Step 3: cash settlement.** Now suppose you had sold the XSP put struck at 600 instead, and XSP also closed at 599. It pays out \(1 \times \$100 = \$100\) in cash at Friday's close, you keep $200 of the premium, and Monday's gap never touches you. The difference between the two outcomes, $1,797, is exactly the gap: 100 shares times the $17.97 fall.
:::

### SOQ risk on a cash-settled expiry

Cash settlement removes the Monday gap, but an AM-settled option still carries a morning one. AM-settled contracts, the monthly SPX options on Friday and VIX options on Wednesday, expire to the SOQ built from that morning's first trades. The SOQ can differ materially from the previous day's close, through overnight news and the staggered opening rotation. A short put that was safely out of the money at 4 PM on Thursday can be in the money at Friday's SOQ. **Close AM-settled positions on the afternoon before expiry, rather than holding them into the SOQ.**

### Single stocks

For individual stocks the gap risk is dramatically worse. Single-stock options are not the focus of this book, which touches them only in Part XI. Earnings releases, merger and acquisition announcements and other surprise news can produce gaps of 10% to 20%. Anyone running short puts on individual names is structurally short jump risk. The [[variance risk premium]] they collect, the premium option sellers earn for bearing that risk (Chapter 10C), may or may not be enough to compensate.

## Why XSP suits a retail account

Put-writing means selling puts, month after month, to collect their premium. For any cash-secured systematic put-writing strategy, **XSP is the retail-correct choice**. It gives you four things at once:

- **Size:** the same SPX exposure, scaled to one tenth of the notional, so that the position is sized appropriately in any retail account.
- **Tax:** the same 1256 60/40 tax treatment as full SPX.
- **Settlement:** cash settlement, so there is no Monday-gap risk.
- **Exercise:** **[[European-style|European-style option]]** exercise, which allows exercise only at expiry, so there is no early assignment.

A SPY option is an **[[American-style option]]**: its holder can exercise it on any day up to expiry, so its seller can be assigned early (Chapter 12H).

Most retail traders use SPY puts because SPY is what they see. It is the most-quoted equity ETF, it is prominently displayed in brokerage apps, and many retail-targeted strategy videos use it as the example. SPY is operationally inferior on every dimension except spread width. There it is slightly tighter: typically $0.01 to $0.02 on at-the-money SPY options, against $0.05 to $0.10 on XSP.

The tax difference alone is large, and Chapter 2E works it through. It is often quoted as approximately 12 to 14 percentage points of tax on every dollar of gain, for a top-bracket American individual taxpayer. Chapter 2E shows that a like-for-like comparison of the two tax rates gives about 10 points. On a strategy that captures 8% a year, the quoted gap is about 100 basis points of after-tax return a year, and the like-for-like gap about 80, turned on or off by instrument choice alone. Over a decade of [[compounding]] on a substantial account, that gap is a small house.

::: rule
For a cash-secured, systematic put-writing strategy on the S&P 500:

- **Trade XSP, not SPY:** SPY wins only on spread width, and it loses on tax, settlement and early assignment.
- **Size from notional:** one SPX option is ten XSP options, so choose the product whose single contract your account can secure.
- **Do not hold AM-settled positions into the SOQ:** close them on the afternoon before expiry.
:::

## Trading hours: ES tells you where SPX opens

Cash SPX trades only during **[[regular trading hours]]**, 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET. The futures, ES and MES, trade nearly 24 hours, from Sunday 6:00 PM ET through Friday 5:00 PM ET, with a brief daily maintenance window. SPX options have extended hours on Cboe, from 8:15 PM ET the previous evening through 9:15 AM ET. The cash SPX *index*, though, does not exist outside regular hours.

When financial media report "S&P futures up 0.4% overnight", that is ES: the cash SPX index simply does not exist between 4:00 PM and 9:30 AM. The opening print of SPX at 9:30 AM is built from the actual opening trades of all 500 components. That is why it sometimes prints away from where futures were trading thirty seconds earlier. Traders watch ES overnight to anticipate the SPX open. The **[[basis|futures basis]]** between ES and SPX during overnight hours encodes the market's evolving consensus.

### Why ES and SPX stay together

A future is tied to its index by the [[cost of carry]], the cost of carrying the index until the future expires. Holding the stocks costs you interest on the money tied up in them and pays you their dividends. The future skips both, so its fair price is the index grown at the interest rate less the dividend yield:

\[ F = S\,e^{(r-q)T} \]
{#eq:fair}

Here \(F\) is the fair futures price, \(S\) the index level, \(r\) the risk-free interest rate, \(q\) the index's dividend yield and \(T\) the time to the future's expiry, in years. The factor \(e^{(r-q)T}\) is [[continuous compounding]] at the net rate \(r - q\) (Chapter 1A). The basis, \(F - S\), shrinks as expiry approaches, and at expiry, where \(T\) reaches zero, the future converges on the index. Chapter 4B treats futures in full, including why their price carries the cost of financing.

::: worked Fair value, and the arbitrage that enforces it
**Step 1: the fair price.** Take SPX at 6,000, a hypothetical interest rate of 4%, a dividend yield of 1.3% and three months to expiry, so \(T = 0.25\). Then \((r - q)T = 0.027 \times 0.25 = 0.00675\), and [eq:fair] gives \(F = 6{,}000 \times e^{0.00675} = 6{,}040.6\). The basis, the future's premium over the index, is about 40 points.

**Step 2: a rich future.** Suppose ES trades at 6,060 while the index is at 6,000. The future is 19.4 points above its fair value. An arbitrageur sells ES and buys the basket of SPX stocks, locking in about \(19.4 \times \$50 = \$970\) a contract before costs. The selling pushes ES down and the buying pushes the stocks up until the gap closes.

**Step 3: an overnight move.** Now suppose ES settles at its fair 6,040.6 at the close and rises 0.4% overnight, to 6,064.8. Dividing by the same carry factor puts the implied SPX open at \(6{,}064.8/1.00677 = 6{,}024\), also 0.4% above the close.
:::

This structural [[arbitrage]] keeps the two locked together during regular hours. If ES trades meaningfully above SPX, adjusted for financing and dividends, arbitrageurs short ES and buy a basket of SPX components. If ES trades meaningfully below SPX, they do the reverse. The trade runs continuously during regular hours and keeps the ES-SPX basis tight. Overnight, nobody can arbitrage directly against a cash SPX that does not exist. The basis can then drift, and it becomes a forward-looking estimate of the open.

## Why dealers hedge in ES

Parts VI to IX describe dealer [[hedging]] in detail. A dealer who has sold an SPX option must hedge the resulting [[delta]] exposure, the option's sensitivity to small moves in the index. The dealer typically does this in **ES futures** rather than in cash SPX stocks or SPY, for three reasons:

1. ES has tighter spreads at institutional size than SPY.
2. ES trades nearly 24 hours while cash SPX does not, so overnight risk can be hedged.
3. ES margining under **[[SPAN|SPAN margin]]**, the futures exchanges' risk-based system, is more capital-efficient than margining SPY shares under [[Reg-T|Reg-T margin]] (Chapter 2E).

When Part VI describes the dealer buying shares to hedge a short call, the literal-share reading is wrong. The dealer is buying ES futures contracts in size, with cash-share hedging used only at the margin to flatten the remaining basis. The flow shows up in the SPX cash market only indirectly, through the arbitrage that links the two. This matters operationally: the highest-frequency [[intraday momentum]] effects of dealer hedging (Chapter 6C) show up most clearly in ES prints, not SPY prints. The two are correlated enough that SPY shows them too, slightly delayed.

The reader steps a hedge through the chain and sees the delay at each stage. It makes clear why hedging flow appears first and most clearly in ES.]

::: practice
For leveraged exposure to the index without options, ES and MES carry 1256 tax treatment, with the financing built into the basis rather than charged as interest. MES, at one tenth of ES, is the size a retail account can hold. An overnight headline about "S&P futures" is about ES, and it is the best available guide to where SPX opens.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Cash settlement removes the weekend gap, not every gap. An AM-settled option still settles to an SOQ that can sit far from the previous close, and on a stressed morning to a level no screen showed. XSP's spread is also wider than SPY's, so a strategy that trades in and out often, rather than holding to expiry, pays that extra spread each time. Chapter 2D prices that trade-off between spread and settlement.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: treating SPY and XSP as the same trade
They track the same index at about the same notional, so it is tempting to treat them as interchangeable. They differ in settlement, exercise style and tax treatment. A short SPY put can be assigned early, can leave you holding shares through a Monday gap, and is taxed under the ordinary rules. The XSP put does none of these.
:::

::: recap
- SPX is the float-adjusted market value of about 500 companies divided by a divisor. S&P adjusts the divisor so that additions, deletions, spin-offs and special dividends do not move the level. Ordinary dividends and splits leave it alone.
- Six products trade on the index: SPX, XSP and SPY options, ES and MES futures, and VIX options. They differ in multiplier, settlement, exercise style, tax and trading hours.
- Notional, the multiplier times the level, sets a position's size: $600,000 for one SPX option at 6,000, and $60,000 for XSP or SPY.
- Physical settlement leaves you holding the Monday gap. AM cash settlement leaves you holding the SOQ.
- ES trades around the clock and is tied to SPX by financing and dividends. Dealers hedge SPX options in ES.
:::

::: check
?? A stock in the index pays a $2 ordinary dividend and later splits 3-for-1. Which event changes the divisor?
!! Neither event changes it. The dividend lowers the price and the level while the divisor stays put, which is why SPX is a price-return index. The split triples the float shares and cuts the price to a third, so price times float shares is unchanged, and so is the divisor.

?? The committee deletes a company whose float value is $1 of every $50 in the index. By what factor does the divisor change, and why?
!! By [eq:divisor], it is multiplied by \(49/50 = 0.98\), a 2% cut. The index's total float value falls by 2%, so the divisor must fall by 2% for the level not to gap down.

?? With SPX at 5,000, what notional does one SPX option, one XSP option and one ES contract control?
!! One SPX option controls \(\$100 \times 5{,}000 = \$500{,}000\), one XSP option a tenth of that, $50,000, and one ES contract \(\$50 \times 5{,}000 = \$250{,}000\).

?? You are short a monthly SPX put, 1% out of the money at Thursday's close, which expires on Friday. Why might you close it on Thursday afternoon?
!! It is AM-settled, so it settles to Friday's SOQ, built from each component's first trade that morning. Overnight news and a staggered open can put the SOQ below the strike, even at a level no screen showed. Closing on Thursday removes that risk, at the price of the premium left in the option.

?? Why is a short SPY put near the money on a Friday afternoon riskier than a short XSP put at the same strike?
!! If it ends in the money, SPY settles physically. You are assigned 100 shares a contract and hold them over the weekend, absorbing whatever gap the market opens with on Monday. The XSP put settles in cash at Friday's close and leaves nothing open.

?? With SPX at 6,000, an interest rate of 5% and a dividend yield of 1%, what is the fair price of a future six months from expiry?
!! Here \((r - q)T = 0.04 \times 0.5 = 0.02\), so [eq:fair] gives \(F = 6{,}000 \times e^{0.02} = 6{,}000 \times 1.0202 = 6{,}121\). The basis is about 121 points.
:::
