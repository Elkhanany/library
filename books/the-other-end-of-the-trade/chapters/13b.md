## What conviction should mean

"High conviction" should not mean "I believe in this strongly." It should mean that **every independent layer of evidence converges**: the evidence is robust, the mechanism can be stated, the regime is supportive, execution is feasible and the size is survivable. If any single layer fails, conviction collapses, however strong the signal feels.

::: plain
Before a trade gets money, ask five questions. Is the evidence good? Do you know why it works? Does today's market suit it? Can you actually trade it? Can you survive it going wrong? Score each answer from 0 to 1 and multiply the five scores together. A trade that fails one question badly scores near zero, whatever its other answers.
:::

### Four conditions first

The Edge Book came to its score through a reviewer's challenge. The reviewer of its second version pointed out that it never defined "high conviction" formally, and without a definition a framework is just a collection of edges with no clear threshold for action. The first answer was a four-part test. Trade only if all four conditions hold:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\hat{\mu}_{\text{net}} > 0, \quad \text{ES}_{95} < L_{\text{tol}}, \\
&\Pr(\text{margin breach}) < \epsilon, \\
&\rho_{\text{tail}} \text{ acceptable}
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:admit}

Here \(\hat{\mu}_{\text{net}}\) is the estimated expected return after costs, and \(\text{ES}_{95}\) is the [[expected shortfall]] at 95%, the average loss in the worst 5% of outcomes (Chapter 1B). \(L_{\text{tol}}\) is the largest tail loss you can tolerate, \(\epsilon\) is a small probability you choose, and \(\rho_{\text{tail}}\) is the [[tail correlation]] of the trade with the positions you already hold (Chapter 1F). In plain English:

- Expected value must be positive after costs.
- The tail loss must be survivable.
- The probability of a margin failure must be low.
- Correlation with your existing positions in stress must be acceptable.

Chapter 1F wrote a threshold version of the same idea, in which the net return must beat penalties on tail risk and on execution cost.

::: worked Four conditions on a put-writing sleeve
**Step 1: the sleeve.** Suppose a $500,000 portfolio puts $100,000 into cash-secured monthly XSP puts, with a hypothetical positive net expected return, so the first condition holds. Chapter 1D put the PUT index's monthly \(\text{ES}_{95}\) at roughly −8%, which here is $8,000, or 1.6% of the portfolio.

**Step 2: the next two conditions.** If your tolerance \(L_{\text{tol}}\) is 5% of the portfolio, $25,000, the tail loss passes. The puts are fully secured by cash, so no margin call can breach the account, and the third condition passes too.

**Step 3: the fourth.** In a crash the sleeve falls with the stocks you already hold, since its tail beta is about 1.0 (Chapter 10A). Whether that is acceptable depends on how much equity the portfolio carries, which no formula answers for you.
:::

These four conditions are *necessary* but not jointly sufficient: strong evidence and good mechanism design also matter. So the Edge Book extends them to a score over five domains.

## The conviction score

For any candidate move \(i\) at time \(t\), the **[[conviction score]]** is

\[ C_i(t) = E_i\, M_i\, R_i(t)\, X_i(t)\, S_i \]
{#eq:conviction}

Each of its five factors is a score from 0 to 1:

| Factor | Domain | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| \(E_i\) | Evidence quality | How well supported is the edge in academic and practitioner work, and has it been replicated? |
| \(M_i\) | Mechanism clarity | Can you say why the edge exists, and what would make it disappear? |
| \(R_i(t)\) | Regime alignment | Is today's market regime supportive of this specific move? |
| \(X_i(t)\) | Execution feasibility | Can you actually trade it, given its liquidity, spread, fill quality and operational complexity? |
| \(S_i\) | Sizing survivability | Can you size it so that S2 is operational and S3 recoverable, with [[kill criteria|kill criterion]] written down? |

The two factors marked \((t)\) move with the market, so the same move can score differently next week. Evidence, mechanism and sizing belong to the move itself and to the size you choose. The Edge Book sets three thresholds:

- **\(C \ge 0.5\): high conviction.** Allocate, at full intended size.
- **\(0.3 \le C < 0.5\): medium conviction.** Take a small allocation or test the sizing.
- **\(C < 0.3\): insufficient conviction.** Do not trade.

The thresholds are heuristic but disciplined. The point is not the exact number but the multiplicative structure, which forces every domain to clear the bar.

### Why multiply

The multiplicative form is the load-bearing assumption. A single factor at 0 makes the whole product 0, and one weak factor drags down every strong one. Three examples show it, each with its other factors at 1:

- Stellar evidence with unsurvivable sizing, \(E = 0.95\) and \(S = 0.10\), gives \(C = 0.095\), however good the paper looks.
- Strong evidence in a poor regime, \(E = 0.9\) and \(R = 0.2\), gives 0.18, which is not high conviction.
- A great mechanism with unsurvivable sizing, \(M = 0.9\) and \(S = 0.1\), gives 0.09.

Each domain is a *necessary* condition. In operational reality you cannot compensate for one broken dimension with extra strength in another, and the product encodes exactly that.

::: worked Averaging hides the broken factor
**Step 1: one weak factor.** Take four factors at 0.9 and a fifth at 0.2. Their average is \((4 \times 0.9 + 0.2)/5 = 0.76\), which looks strong.

**Step 2: the product.** Their product is \(0.9^4 \times 0.2 = 0.656 \times 0.2 = 0.131\), below the floor of 0.3: do not trade.

**Step 3: no weak factor.** Five factors at 0.9 multiply to \(0.9^5 = 0.59\), comfortably high. The average hides the one factor that the product exposes.
:::

How good must each factor be? If all five sat at one level \(g\), their product would be \(g^5\). So the level that a score \(C\) demands of the factors, on average, is its fifth root:

\[ g = C^{1/5} \]
{#eq:geomean}

This \(g\) is the geometric mean of the five factors. High conviction, \(C = 0.5\), needs \(g = 0.5^{1/5} = 0.87\), and the floor of 0.3 needs \(g = 0.79\). A single factor at 0.6 therefore demands that the other four average \((0.5/0.6)^{1/4} = 0.955\) for high conviction, which leaves little room for any other weakness.

## The five factors, scored

The Dealer Mechanics Lab gives a scoring guide for each factor as it applies to swing trades. The Edge Book's guide, written for strategies in a portfolio, differs in places, and both appear below.

**E: evidence.** How well supported is the setup in academic and practitioner work, and has it been replicated across datasets and periods? The bands follow the [[evidence tiers|evidence tier]] of Chapter 10A:

- **Tier 1**, peer-reviewed, multi-decade and replicated: 0.8 to 1.0. The Lab's examples are Jegadeesh-Titman momentum, Connors RSI(2) mean reversion in trending markets, and Ni-Pearson-Poteshman [[pinning]] near options expiration (OPEX).
- **Tier 2**, strong empirical results that are sensitive to the sample: 0.5 to 0.8.
- **Tier 3**, thin, recent or decayed evidence: 0.0 to 0.4.

Chapter 11H grades Connors's specific RSI(2) rule lower than this list does. It calls the rule practitioner work, published in trade books rather than peer-reviewed journals, which rests on an academic short-horizon reversal effect.

**M: mechanism.** Can you articulate *why* this setup has a positive expected value?

- A clear mechanism with academic support, or a source identified among the four of Chapter 1G (risk premium, behavioral, institutional or microstructure): 0.7 to 1.0.
- A partial story about the mechanism: 0.4 to 0.7.
- "I do not know why this works": 0.0 to 0.3.

Evidence and mechanism are different questions. Evidence asks *whether* the effect has held up in data, while mechanism asks *why* it should hold in the future and what would stop it.

**R(t): regime.** Is the current regime supportive of this specific setup? This factor changes with time. For long swing trades the Lab reads it from the [[composite signal]] of Chapter 11G, a score from −100 to +100, together with three other readings:

- A composite of +40 or more, Stage 2 confirmed (Chapter 11E), positive [[gamma exposure]] (GEX, Chapter 8A) and contango: 0.8 to 1.0.
- A composite of +10 to +40, a transition from Stage 2 to Stage 3, and neutral GEX: 0.5 to 0.7.
- A composite below 0, Stage 4, negative GEX and backwardation: 0.0 to 0.3.

The bands skip composites between 0 and +10. Chapter 11G calls that zone neutral, and trades only its highest-conviction setups there, at half size. The Edge Book's examples are for strategies rather than setups. A backwardated VIX curve scores low for short volatility, trend in the middle of a drought scores low, and the even week of the FOMC cycle scores high for equity (Chapter 10G).

[FIGURE IDEA: The regime factor through time. The top panel draws a composite signal over two years, shaded by the bands above; the middle panel converts it into the regime factor R; the bottom panel draws the conviction score of one fixed setup, with the other four factors held at the reader's settings. Lines at 0.3 and 0.5 mark where the setup is tradeable. It shows one setup moving in and out of tradeability with nothing but the regime changing.]

**X(t): execution.** Can you actually trade this in a retail account, with good liquidity, a low spread and clean operations?

- SPX, SPY, QQQ or the Mag-7 stocks with standard order types: 0.85 to 1.0. The Edge Book gives a liquid ETF 0.9 to 1.0.
- A multi-leg index option with manageable spreads: 0.6 to 0.8 in the Lab's guide, and 0.5 to 0.7 in the Edge Book's.
- A thin single-stock option with wide spreads: 0.2 to 0.4 in both.
- A strategy that needs infrastructure you do not have: 0.0 to 0.2.

**S: sizing.** Can you size the trade so that the S2 stress scenario (SPX −20%, VIX up 25 points) is *operational* and S3 (SPX −35%, VIX up 50) is *recoverable*, with explicit kill criteria? Operational means the account keeps working through S2, with no margin breach and no forced sale. Recoverable means the S3 loss is one the account can repair (Chapter 1E).

- Sized for survivability, with kill rules committed in writing and a drawdown plan documented: 0.85 to 1.0.
- Sized "comfortably", without explicit stress testing: 0.5 to 0.7.
- Sized aggressively, without kill criteria: 0.1 to 0.3.

## One setup in two regimes

::: worked A Trend Pullback on SPX in calm and in stress
**Step 1: the setup.** Take the canonical RSI(2) pullback in Stage 2, the Trend Pullback of Chapter 11H. Hold the setup, its evidence and its mechanism fixed, and change only the regime.

**Step 2: a calm regime.** SPX is above its rising 200-day [[simple moving average]] (SMA), the VIX is at 14 and the composite is +50. GEX is positive, above the [[gamma flip]], and the term structure is in contango. The scores are \(E = 0.85\), \(M = 0.90\), \(R = 0.85\), \(X = 0.90\) and \(S = 0.90\), so \(C = 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.90 = 0.527\). High conviction: take the trade at full intended size.

**Step 3: a stress regime.** SPX is in the transition from Stage 3 to Stage 4, the VIX is at 28, the term structure is backwardated and the composite is −30. GEX is negative, and a [[Consumer Price Index]] (CPI) report has just shocked the market. Evidence and mechanism are unchanged, but \(R = 0.20\), \(X = 0.65\) and \(S = 0.40\), so \(C = 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.20 \times 0.65 \times 0.40 = 0.040\). Insufficient conviction: do not enter, and reduce existing positions.

The same setup, with identical evidence and mechanism, went from 0.527 to 0.040 as the composite fell from +50 to −30. **This is what operational discipline looks like: selectivity is mostly not trading.**

## The same numbers for a put-writing sleeve

The Edge Book scores the [[VRP harvest]], at-the-money (ATM) monthly cash-secured XSP puts at 15% of the portfolio, with exactly the factor values above. Its reasons differ, since this is a strategy held for years rather than a trade held for days, and it adds a third scenario.

::: worked The VRP harvest in three settings
**Step 1: VIX at 14, contango, a normal regime.** The evidence is Tier 1 (Bondarenko, Carr and Wu, Jurek and Stafford): 0.85. The mechanism is a risk premium with a clear theoretical foundation: 0.90. The VIX is low to normal, the curve is in contango and no event is pending: 0.85. XSP is liquid, with tight spreads, and easy to roll: 0.90. At 15% of the portfolio the size is well within survivable bounds, and holding the puts cash-secured removes margin risk: 0.90. So \(C = 0.53\), high conviction: allocate.

**Step 2: VIX at 28, backwardated for 5 days, after a CPI shock.** Evidence and mechanism are the same. Backwardation would trigger the strategy's kill criterion, so the regime scores 0.20. Spreads are wider in volatility but still tradeable: 0.65. Scaling down is mandatory in this regime, and the full 15% is unsafe: 0.40. So \(C = 0.040\), insufficient conviction: enter no new positions, and reduce existing ones.

**Step 3: VIX at 18, a normal regime, but 50% of the portfolio.** Evidence, mechanism, regime and execution score 0.85, 0.90, 0.85 and 0.90 again. But 50% of the portfolio's notional in short puts means that a gap to a VIX of 30 with SPX down 20% likely force-liquidates, so sizing scores 0.20. Then \(C = 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.85 \times 0.90 \times 0.20 = 0.117\), insufficient conviction.
:::

In Step 2 the same strategy, with the same evidence and mechanism, loses its regime alignment and its sizing survivability, and \(C\) drops by an order of magnitude. That is what discipline looks like. In Step 3 the evidence and the regime do not matter, because the size is wrong, and the framework correctly catches it.

The warning about forced liquidation in Step 3 assumes puts written on margin. Fully cash-secured puts cannot be force-liquidated by a margin call (Chapter 1E). A 20% fall still costs them close to 20% of their notional, less the premium collected, which at a 50% allocation is about 10% of the portfolio.

## Scoring a popular strategy: GEX-level scalping

Consider the rule "buy SPX at the [[put wall]], sell at the [[call wall]]", run on dealer-positioning data from vendors such as SqueezeMetrics or SpotGamma.

::: worked GEX-level scalping
**Step 1: evidence.** The academic literature on dealer gamma (Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman) supports *directional* effects, but specific rules for trading levels have not validated since 2022. A Cboe study of 2025 found that hedging by market makers (MMs) of 0DTE options is about 0.2% of liquidity. Evidence scores 0.25.

**Step 2: mechanism.** The general story of gamma hedging is plausible. The specific mechanism, that dealers must hedge at exactly these strikes, is not robustly supported and depends on assumptions about who is long and who is short at which strikes: 0.30.

**Step 3: regime.** Dealers are sometimes net long gamma and sometimes net short, and public GEX models do not reliably tell which: 0.40.

**Step 4: execution.** SPX is liquid, but the strategy needs specific intraday entries near a wall, which makes it complex to execute: 0.50.

**Step 5: sizing.** Where to put the stop is unclear, since there is no natural exit if the wall does not hold. The crash risk is asymmetric as well: if the wall fails, you are caught long as the index falls through the put strikes. Sizing scores 0.35.

**Step 6: the score.** \(C = 0.25 \times 0.30 \times 0.40 \times 0.50 \times 0.35 = 0.005\).
:::

That is effectively zero conviction. It is not a judgment of the people who trade the idea: the framework is correctly identifying that a popular strategy fails even basic conviction screening. Most of its appeal is narrative, and the multiplicative structure exposes that.

Both traditions of this book reject scalping level by level (Chapters 10A and 13F). They differ on how much the *sign* of dealer gamma should count. The Dealer Mechanics Lab reads it into the regime factor above, and dealer walls into the Real Value score below. The Edge Book gives dealer positioning only low confidence, as a minor input to its regime filters (Chapter 10H).

## Scoring a slow allocation: trend through DBMF

::: worked A 15% allocation to DBMF
**Step 1: the factors.** The evidence is Tier 1, from Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen's 140-year record and from Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen: 0.90. There are several complementary mechanisms, under-reaction, compensation paid by hedgers and liquidity provision, and their decomposition is well understood: 0.85. In 2023 and 2024 trend was mixed, in a drought between strong years, so the regime scores 0.55: this is mid-cycle, and you should not allocate at a peak. DBMF is a single ETF, easy to buy: 0.95. A 15% allocation is well within survivability, and trend has positive crash skew, with rare upside in stress: 0.90.

**Step 2: the score.** \(C = 0.90 \times 0.85 \times 0.55 \times 0.95 \times 0.90 = 0.36\), medium conviction.
:::

The regime score is the binding constraint. The *structural* case for the allocation is strong, with high evidence, mechanism, execution and sizing, but the timing of the entry is poor. The Edge Book's operational implication is to build the position over time rather than in one purchase, and to scale up if a drawdown creates a better entry point. This kind of nuance is exactly what the conviction score is meant to surface.

Chapter 10D, from another chapter of the Edge Book, advises against timing trend's entries at all: buy and hold across its cycles. The two pieces of advice meet in staging, which spreads the entry without waiting for a signal. Scaling up after a drawdown is a timing decision that Chapter 10D would not make.

## Five ways a trade fails the score

Both sources name the same five recurring patterns that look like edge but score low. Each has an example from swing trading and one from a portfolio, a factor that collapses, and a rule.

1. **Strong evidence, hostile regime.** A trend pullback in a Stage 4 market, or a VRP harvest in backwardation. Evidence and mechanism are high, and the regime is low. Respect the regime filter even when the evidence behind the setup is strong.
2. **Right idea, oversized.** A single swing trade holding 25% of the portfolio, or put-writing holding 50%. Every factor is high except sizing. Edge cannot compensate for an unsurvivable size, because sizing is multiplicative, not additive.
3. **Beautiful backtest, no mechanism.** Optimized parameters that give a great paper Sharpe ratio with no articulable reason to work in the future. Evidence is medium to high, and mechanism is low. A backtest without a mechanism is a warning sign of data mining, which produces high Sharpe ratios in any sufficiently large sample (Chapter 1D).
4. **Real edge, wrong infrastructure.** A cross-sectional dispersion edge that needs daily multi-leg, delta-hedged positions on 50 names, or an option-mispricing edge that needs a portfolio of 200 names, rebalanced and hedged daily. Evidence is high, and execution is low. An edge that needs infrastructure you lack is not an edge for you.
5. **Vivid narrative, thin evidence.** GEX scalping, with a compelling story about dealer positioning and no backtest evidence behind it. The mechanism feels high while the evidence is low. A compelling narrative is not evidence quality.

The framework catches all five systematically, because its multiplicative structure forces every domain to score on its own.

::: insight
High-conviction swing trading is mostly not trading. Most observations do not survive interpretation. Most interpretations do not survive the conviction score. The trades that remain have cleared all five gates, and the Dealer Mechanics Lab concludes that they should therefore be larger and more durable than the trades you would otherwise make.
:::

## The trade-admission checklist

The score is a number, but it should rest on a written checklist that every candidate move passes through:

1. **Source identified.** Which of the four is it: risk premium, behavioral, institutional or microstructure?
2. **Category assigned.** Foundation premium, factor, overlay, filter, or rejected (Chapter 10A)?
3. **Evidence tier.** T1, T2 or T3, with the primary source cited.
4. **Mechanism stated.** Why does this edge exist, and what would make it disappear?
5. **Academic Sharpe ratio.** What does the literature suggest?
6. **Retail haircut applied.** What is your realistic user-account Sharpe ratio (Chapter 13A)?
7. **Regime check.** Is the current regime supportive, and what would invalidate it?
8. **Tail loss calculated.** The \(\text{ES}_{95}\), and the profit or loss in S2 and in S3.
9. **Margin behavior.** How far does the margin requirement expand under stress?
10. **Position size.** Survivable in S2 (operational) and recoverable in S3, with no irrecoverable loss?
11. **Kill criteria.** Specific, measurable and committed in advance.
12. **Correlation to the existing book.** Does the trade duplicate exposures you already hold?
13. **Conviction score.** Is \(C = E \times M \times R \times X \times S \ge 0.5\)?

If you cannot tick all 13 boxes, the trade is not ready for admission. The Edge Book insists that this is not bureaucracy but the discipline that prevents 80% of bad trades.

The checklist also beats intuition, which links strategies to vivid narratives rather than to the quality of their edge. It forces you to state each domain *separately* before you commit capital. Stories die under separate analysis. Real edges survive it.

::: familiar
The checklist works like a trial's eligibility criteria, which are conjunctive. A patient who meets eleven inclusion criteria and fails the twelfth is not enrolled, however strongly they meet the other eleven. The conviction score is a graded version of the same rule: a factor near zero acts like a failed criterion, while a middling factor shrinks the product rather than excluding the trade outright.
:::

## The decision tree

The same analysis can be walked as seven questions, in order:

1. **What is this?** A risk premium, or a behavioral, institutional or microstructure edge? And a foundation premium, factor, overlay, filter or rejected strategy?
2. **Is the evidence robust?** Peer-reviewed, multi-decade and replicated is T1. Strong and empirical but sensitive to the sample is T2. Thin or decayed is T3, and likely not a trade.
3. **What is the mechanism?** Can you say why it works and what would make it stop? Has anything changed that affects it?
4. **Does it survive the haircut?** Apply the haircut from academic to user account. Is the realistic Sharpe ratio still positive, and materially better than the simpler alternative of just holding SPX?
5. **What is the worst-case path?** Compute the stress profit or loss for falls of 10%, 20% and 35% in SPX, the margin expansion, and the risk of forced liquidation.
6. **What is the kill rule?** Name a specific trigger, a pre-committed action and the criteria for revalidating.
7. **Compute the conviction score.** At 0.5 or more, allocate. From 0.3 to 0.5, trade small or test. Below 0.3, do not trade.

This is not seven separate analyses. It is one analysis, structured to surface the issues that informal evaluation misses.

## What the score is not

- **A promise.** High-conviction trades still lose. The score measures expected risk-adjusted return after frictions, not certainty.
- **A reason to size big.** Even at \(C = 0.7\), the size comes from the tail loss and the portfolio context. Conviction tells you *whether* to trade. It does not tell you *how much*.
- **A substitute for a backtest.** The score is a thinking tool, and backtests, stress tests and live paper trading keep their value.
- **A static number.** Regime and execution shift over time, and the score updates as conditions change.

What the score *is*: a forcing function for honest evaluation across every domain before you commit capital. It works because its multiplicative structure mirrors reality. You cannot compensate for unsurvivable sizing with great evidence, or for poor execution with a strong mechanism.

### Where the traditions differ on size

The two sources disagree about the second point. The Edge Book keeps conviction as a gate: it decides whether a trade happens, and the tail loss and the loss budget of Chapter 13C decide its size, although a medium score does call for a small or test allocation. The Dealer Mechanics Lab lets conviction scale the size. It takes high-conviction trades at full intended size and medium ones at a reduced size, 60% to 80% in the figure, and its worked examples in Chapter 13D follow that rule. Both size from an explicit loss budget, so they agree on the ceiling: conviction never takes a trade above the size the loss budget allows. They differ only on whether a medium score should shrink it below that.

::: warn Common mistake: "my intuition is strong, so I do not need to score it"
Strong intuition is your unconscious mind applying patterns from past experience. It is useful, but it is also exactly the channel through which behavioral biases enter. The score forces a conscious evaluation that surfaces what your intuition skipped. If your intuition agrees with the score, you have cross-validated. If they disagree, one of them is wrong, and only the score leaves a written audit trail you can review.
:::

## Single stocks: the Real Value score

The conviction score is calibrated for index and broad-market swing positions, where regime, execution, evidence and sizing dominate. A single stock, particularly with an options overlay, has layers that the conviction score does not capture cleanly. For single stocks the Dealer Mechanics Lab uses a companion, the **[[Real Value score]]**:

\[ \text{RV} = F\,T\,O\,G\,V\,X\,S \]
{#eq:rv}

Each of its seven factors runs from 0 to 1, and the trade is taken at \(\text{RV} \ge 0.4\). That is slightly lower than the conviction score's 0.5, because more factors, all at most 1, multiply a uniformly decent position down faster. By the reasoning of [eq:geomean], the level each of seven factors must average is \(\text{RV}^{1/7}\), and \(0.4^{1/7} = 0.88\). That is almost exactly the 0.87 that high conviction asks of five factors, so the two thresholds set the same bar.

**F: fundamentals.** Revisions to revenue and to earnings per share (EPS) trend the right way, the margin trend is constructive, and the dispersion of analysts' estimates narrows as a consensus forms. Guidance revisions are positive, or negative for a short, and the sector or industry has a tailwind.

- 0.9 to 1.0: rising estimates, narrowing dispersion, positive guidance and large institutional buying.
- 0.5 to 0.7: stable estimates, mixed analyst views and a neutral sector.
- 0.2 to 0.4: falling estimates, widening dispersion, and mixed or negative guidance.
- 0: a clear negative break in the fundamentals.

**T: technical structure.** Stage 2 is confirmed, the base is a proper consolidation rather than noise, [[relative strength]] against the benchmark is good, volume dries up on the pullback, and the pivot or reclaim is clean.

- 0.9 to 1.0: a textbook Stage 2 pullback, with tight consolidation, strong relative strength and a clean pivot.
- 0.5 to 0.7: Stage 2, but with an imperfect base or mediocre relative strength.
- 0.2 to 0.4: choppy structure, weak relative strength and an ambiguous pivot.
- 0: Stage 4, or distribution in Stage 3.

**O: option flow signature.** It looks for five things: net premium bought at the ask in the direction of the thesis, and opening transactions rather than closing ones, which rising [[open interest]] (OI) confirms the next day. It also wants trades initiated on the thesis's side, flow repeated across sessions rather than a one-off, and a consistent change in skew and implied volatility.

- 0.9 to 1.0: large net premium bought at the ask in the supporting direction, with opening transactions confirmed and the flow repeated over 3 or more sessions.
- 0.5 to 0.7: modest aligned flow in a single session, whose opening status is unclear.
- 0.2 to 0.4: mixed flow, possibly closing transactions, and hard to interpret.
- 0: flow opposite to the thesis.

Flow alone is never a signal. O is a *confirmation* factor, and F or T must already score 0.6 or more before O matters.

**G: GEX path support.** Does dealer positioning support the move? Is the price below a call wall that becomes a magnet on the upside, or above a put wall that makes a floor? Is the gamma flip near, making the regime unstable?

- 0.9 to 1.0: clear path support, such as a long thesis with positive GEX above the flip, the call wall well above the target and the put wall well below the entry.
- 0.5 to 0.7: a neutral path, with flat GEX and no walls nearby.
- 0.2 to 0.4: an obstructed path, such as the gamma flip in the way or a call wall directly overhead on a long thesis.
- 0: GEX actively opposes the move, such as negative GEX under a long mean-reversion thesis.

**V: vol-value.** Is the option priced reasonably against realized volatility, event risk and its own history of implied volatility? Is the [[IV percentile]] attractive for the direction, and is the [[expected move]] plausible against the [[average true range]] (ATR) and the announced catalysts?

- 0.9 to 1.0: the option is cheap against realized volatility and its history, which favors a buyer, or rich against realized, which favors a seller.
- 0.5 to 0.7: the option is fairly priced, with no advantage to either side.
- 0.2 to 0.4: the option is mispriced against the direction, rich for a buyer or cheap for a seller.
- 0: structural arbitrage against the trade, such as buying a long call into an IV percentile above 90.

**X: execution quality.** A tight bid-ask spread, enough liquidity at the intended size, a favorable time of day, and no known fragility window. The fragile windows are the last 30 minutes, the first 5 minutes after the open, and the lunch hours for less liquid names. This is the conviction score's X.

**S: sizing survivability.** The position survives a stop blown through by 3 times its distance, its correlation with the existing book is accounted for, and the book's total vega and delta stay within portfolio limits (Chapter 13H). This is the conviction score's S.

After computing RV, classify it:

| RV | Reading | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 or more | A real signal: fundamentals, technicals, flow and the volatility surface all agree | Take it, at full size (Chapter 13C) |
| 0.3 to 0.5 | Borderline: most factors aligned, one or two weak | Take it at half size, or skip it for the next setup |
| 0.15 to 0.3 | Mixed: enough green to tempt, enough red to be dangerous | Skip it: a common loss-maker for retail traders |
| Below 0.15 | Avoid | Mostly noise: do not trade |

The table's borderline band starts at 0.3, below the threshold of 0.4. Between the two, skipping satisfies both rules.

::: worked A single-stock score, and a bad vehicle
**Step 1: a strong candidate.** Suppose a stock scores 0.9 on fundamentals, technical structure, option flow and GEX path, 0.8 on vol-value, 0.95 on execution and 0.9 on sizing. By [eq:rv], \(\text{RV} = 0.9^5 \times 0.8 \times 0.95 = 0.449\). That clears 0.4 but falls in the borderline band, so take it at half size.

**Step 2: the same thesis through an overpriced option.** If the option is rich against the direction, vol-value falls to 0.25 and \(\text{RV} = 0.9^5 \times 0.25 \times 0.95 = 0.140\), in the avoid band. The thesis has not changed, only its expression, which is the second of the pathologies below.
:::

Four single-stock pathologies show up as a low RV at a glance:

1. **Flow-only noise.** Massive call buying at the ask, but no fundamental story (F below 0.4) and no technical setup (T below 0.4). It drives chasing trades that fade at once, and RV is typically below 0.1.
2. **Good thesis, bad vehicle.** F and T are both above 0.7, but V is below 0.3 because the option is priced unreasonably. The thesis is real, but you are paying too much for its expression. It often resolves to trading the shares instead of the options.
3. **Good setup, bad path.** F, T and O are all above 0.6, but G is below 0.3, with a call wall directly overhead or the gamma flip in the way. The directional thesis is solid, but dealer mechanics block its path. Wait for the obstacle to clear, or pick a different strike or vehicle.
4. **Stretched, stretching further.** Every factor looks fine alone, but the stock has already moved 30% in the prior month, so the story is in the price. Here RV correctly downgrades the trade, because F should ask whether the story is already reflected in the price, not just whether it is positive.

[FIGURE IDEA: A Real Value worksheet. Seven sliders, one per factor, with the product, its band from the table and a flag for each pathology whose conditions the settings meet: flow-only noise when O is high and F and T are low, a bad vehicle when V is low, a blocked path when G is low. It also marks when O is being counted without F or T at 0.6, showing how often a vivid flow alert rests on nothing else.]

The two scores are not redundant. **Use the conviction score for index swings and the Real Value score for single-stock swings.** They share the execution and sizing factors, and differ in the layers that matter for each kind of decision.

## In practice

::: practice
- Score every candidate in writing before you enter, and record the five factor values in your journal, not just the product.
- Name the binding constraint and ask what would fix it. A low R says wait, a low S says trade smaller and a low X says change the vehicle. A low E or M says it may not be a trade at all.
- Rescore open positions when the regime moves. A position that scored 0.53 in calm can score 0.040 a week later, which says reduce it, not hold and hope.
- Use the same scoring guide every time, the bands above or your own written version of them, so that your scores stay comparable.
:::

::: warn When it fails
- **Optimism compounds.** Each factor is a judgment, and small biases multiply. Scoring five factors at 0.95 where 0.90 was honest inflates \(C\) by \((0.95/0.90)^5 = 1.31\), about a third, which is enough to turn 0.45 into 0.59 and a medium trade into a high one.
- **The score ignores the rest of the book.** Two high-conviction trades on correlated names are one large bet, and the checklist's correlation box catches that, not the score (Chapter 13D, Example 10).
- **A score goes stale.** Regime and execution move, so a number computed last week is not today's.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: What optimism does to a product. The reader enters honest scores for five factors and a bias added to each, from 0 to 0.10. Two bars show the honest and the biased products against the 0.3 and 0.5 lines, and a curve plots the inflation against the size of the bias. It shows a bias too small to notice in any one factor moving a trade across a threshold.]

::: rule
- **Score every trade** on all five factors before entry, in writing, and name the binding constraint.
- **At \(C \ge 0.5\),** take the trade at the size the loss budget of Chapter 13C allows.
- **From 0.3 to 0.5,** take a small or test size; the figure's guide is 60% to 80% of the intended size.
- **Below 0.3,** do not trade, and reduce any open position whose score has fallen there.
- **For single stocks,** use the Real Value score: skip below 0.4, take half size from 0.4 to 0.5, and full size above.
:::

## Where this fits

Chapters 1B to 1F gave you the primitives, Chapter 1G the sources of edge, Chapter 1D the reasons the Sharpe ratio misleads, and Chapter 13A realistic retail expectations. This chapter ties them into a decision rule: the conviction score that decides whether a candidate move becomes a position. Chapter 10A's taxonomy then decides how to implement a move that passes, as a foundation premium, factor, overlay or filter, and Chapters 10B to 10I supply the edges one by one. Chapter 13E walks the full conviction analysis through each major strategy. The conviction framework is the spine of the book: every other chapter either defines the inputs to the score or applies it to specific strategies.

::: recap
- High conviction means that every independent layer converges: evidence, mechanism, regime, execution and a survivable size.
- The conviction score multiplies five factors from 0 to 1, \(C = E\,M\,R\,X\,S\). High conviction starts at 0.5 and trading stops below 0.3, so the factors must average about 0.87 for a high score.
- One weak factor sinks the product. The same setup scores 0.527 in calm and 0.040 in stress, and the same strategy scores 0.117 at an unsurvivable size.
- The score says whether to trade, and the loss budget of Chapter 13C caps how much. The two traditions differ on whether a medium score should shrink the size further.
- For single stocks, the Real Value score multiplies seven factors and takes trades at 0.4 or more.
:::

::: check
?? State the conviction score. Why is it multiplicative rather than additive?
!! \(C = E \times M \times R \times X \times S\), each factor from 0 to 1. Each domain is a necessary condition, and a product lets a single failed domain sink the score, which an average cannot. Four factors at 0.9 and one at 0.2 average 0.76 but multiply to 0.131, below the floor of 0.3.

?? A candidate scores \(E = 0.6\), \(M = 0.7\), \(R = 0.9\), \(X = 0.8\) and \(S = 0.9\). What is \(C\), and what binds?
!! \(C = 0.6 \times 0.7 \times 0.9 \times 0.8 \times 0.9 = 0.272\), below 0.3, so do not trade. Evidence, at 0.6, is the binding constraint. A Tier 2 edge with only a partial mechanism cannot reach high conviction even in a good regime.

?? Why does "strong intuition" not satisfy any of the five domains?
!! Intuition is pattern-matching from past experience, and it runs through exactly the channel by which behavioral biases enter. It attaches to vivid narratives rather than to evidence quality, so it can make a mechanism feel strong while the evidence is thin. Each domain must be scored consciously, separately and in writing.

?? What is the difference between evidence, \(E\), and mechanism, \(M\)?
!! Evidence asks whether the effect has held up in data: peer-reviewed, replicated, over decades. Mechanism asks why it should keep holding: who pays for it, and what would make it stop. A data-mined backtest can have evidence of a kind and no mechanism, while a compelling story can have a mechanism and no evidence. Both score low.

?? In the VRP harvest example, how does \(R(t)\) change between the calm scenario and the stress scenario, and what follows?
!! It falls from 0.85 to 0.20, because a backwardated VIX curve would trigger the strategy's kill criterion. With execution and sizing also lower, \(C\) drops from 0.53 to 0.040, an order of magnitude. The operational implication is to enter no new positions and to reduce existing ones.

?? Why is the Real Value score's threshold 0.4 rather than 0.5?
!! It multiplies seven factors instead of five, and more factors below 1 multiply down faster. The level each factor must average is the seventh root of the threshold, \(0.4^{1/7} = 0.88\), about the same bar as \(0.5^{1/5} = 0.87\) for the conviction score.
:::
