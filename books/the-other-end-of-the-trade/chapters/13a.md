## The problem is rarely the edge

The earlier parts of this book were about understanding the mechanism. This part is about the operational discipline that turns that understanding into compound returns. Its framework is built for swing trading: holding positions in [[SPX]], [[SPY]], [[QQQ]] and the [[Mag-7|Magnificent Seven]] stocks (Chapter 2D lists them) for 2 to 20 trading days. That horizon is where most of the framework lives. It is also where the dealer mechanics of Parts VI to IX have their sharpest practical use.

The retail trader's problem is usually not a lack of [[edge]], since most have access to genuinely tradeable setups. The problem is the gap between paper performance and live performance, a gap dominated by behavioral and operational factors. **A strategy with a paper [[Sharpe ratio]] of 0.80 routinely shows 0.20 to 0.40 in a retail account.** The gap does not mean that academics are wrong, or that your backtest is. Backtests sometimes are wrong, but assume yours is not. The gap exists because real implementation adds friction at several layers. Skipping that analysis is the single biggest cause of disappointment when traders try to replicate published strategies.

This part closes the gap in eight chapters, beginning with this one, which measures it as the **[[retail haircut]]**: the share of a paper Sharpe ratio lost on the way to your account. Chapter 13B builds the conviction score, \(E \times M \times R \times X \times S\), a test of whether a setup deserves capital. Chapter 13C runs every trade through a five-step pipeline, from observation to invalidation, and sets kill rules at three levels. Chapters 13D and 13E reason through ten swing trades and ten portfolio decisions from end to end. Chapter 13F compresses each setup onto a one-page card, and Chapters 13G and 13H manage the whole portfolio and its exposure to volatility.

::: plain
A backtest shows a strategy through clean glass. Between it and your account stand several more panes: data the backtest never saw, the prices you actually get, the tax you owe and the decisions you make under stress. Each pane lets only part of the light through, and the losses multiply. Knowing roughly how much light arrives, before you start, is most of what separates a plan from a disappointment.
:::

## Two sleeves: mechanical and discretionary

The rules of this part govern one portion of your portfolio, not all of it, so the split comes first. A **[[mechanical sleeve]]** is a rules-only [[sleeve]] that executes predefined entries and exits without discretionary override. Its defining property is that no human judgment intervenes between signal and execution. Four examples show the range:

- A systematic program of SPX [[cash-secured puts|cash-secured put]], written on a schedule.
- A 200-day moving-average crossover allocation, which holds the index while its price is above its 200-day [[moving average]] and holds cash while it is below.
- A Connors RSI(2) mean-reversion bot on QQQ, a program that buys short pullbacks by rule (Chapter 11C defines the RSI).
- A fixed monthly [[iron condor]] sale at 30 days to expiry (DTE).

The two traditions of this book disagree about the last example. The Dealer Mechanics Lab teaches the condor (Chapter 12C), while the Edge Book rejects running condors systematically in a retail account (Chapter 10I). Either way, the example shows what "mechanical" means.

The **[[discretionary sleeve]]** is where you choose each trade by judgment inside the framework. It holds the swing trades of Part XI and the whole tactical layer of this book. The split matters because a mechanical sleeve provides **baseline market exposure** that keeps compounding while discretionary judgment is suspended. Judgment is suspended during the drawdown timeouts of Chapter 13C, personal or health events, regime confusion, or plain loss-of-conviction phases.

If the mechanical sleeve is the portfolio's heartbeat, the discretionary sleeve is its thinking layer. A trader who runs only discretionary positions has no baseline. If discretion fails, as it sometimes does, the whole portfolio sits in cash earning Treasury yields.

A reasonable structure for a retail swing-trading account is **60% to 80% mechanical and 20% to 40% discretionary**. Weight the mechanical sleeve toward systematic equity exposure, through factor funds or buy-and-hold, plus modest systematic premium selling. The stopping rules of this part apply to the discretionary sleeve only. They include halting all trading after a 25% drawdown (Chapter 13C) and letting the conviction score govern entries (Chapter 13B). Mechanical sleeves run regardless.

::: worked A 70/30 split through a timeout
**Step 1: the split.** A $400,000 account held at 70% mechanical and 30% discretionary has $280,000 in rules-only sleeves and $120,000 for swing trades.

**Step 2: the timeout.** Suppose losses trip the 30-day halt on discretionary trading. New swing entries stop, but the $280,000 keeps its market exposure. If the market rises a hypothetical 2% during the month, the mechanical sleeves gain $5,600. An account run wholly on discretion would have sat in cash and earned only the Treasury yield.
:::

The reader sets the split from 0% to 100% mechanical and the day a 30-day halt begins, and the discretionary band goes flat during the halt while the mechanical band keeps moving with the market. ## Two routes from paper to account

Every strategy degrades through layers of friction, each one compounding on the one before. Which layers apply depends on where the paper Sharpe ratio came from. The Dealer Mechanics Lab follows a swing strategy that you build and backtest yourself. The Edge Book follows a published academic strategy on its way into an index, then a fund, then your account.

### A strategy you backtest yourself

For a strategy of your own, the Dealer Mechanics Lab names four layers:

1. **Paper [[backtest]].** Baseline performance on historical data, as a backtest engine produces it. Fills are at the [[mid-quote]], the midpoint of the bid and the ask, with no costs beyond a token commission, no taxes and idealized entries.
2. **Out-of-sample.** The same strategy and parameters on data they have never seen, which is where overfitting shows (Chapter 3A). A paper Sharpe ratio of 1.0 from in-sample optimization typically delivers 0.5 to 0.7 [[out-of-sample]] on truly fresh data. The gap is the deflation factor of López de Prado's work on the [[deflated Sharpe ratio]] (Chapter 1D).
3. **Live execution.** Real [[bid-ask spreads|bid-ask spread]] on entry and exit, real [[slippage]] whenever your size exceeds the depth at the top of the book, and real timing. You do not always see a setup form in real time as cleanly as it appears in the backtest. Real-world execution typically costs another 20% to 30% of the Sharpe ratio.
4. **After tax, after behavior.** Tax friction depends on the instrument: gains on SPY and QQQ held a year or less are taxed as ordinary income. SPX, [[XSP]] and ES futures (Chapter 2A) get the 60/40 treatment of [[Section 1256 contracts|Section 1256 contract]] instead (Chapter 2E). Behavior adds early exits, missed setups, inconsistent position sizes and the urge to revenge-trade after a loss.

Behavioral friction is the largest single source of loss in the chain, and the one most retail traders deny exists.

### A strategy you read about

For a published strategy, the Edge Book counts the layers from the academic paper to your account:

| Layer | What is measured | Typical Sharpe haircut | Source of friction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic | A long-short factor portfolio with daily rebalancing, mid-quote fills and no taxes | Baseline | Idealized |
| Investable index | CBOE PUT, BTOP50, MSCI Quality | −15% to −30% | Index construction rules, monthly rebalancing, real bid-ask spreads |
| ETF or fund, live | PUTW, DBMF, KMLM, MTUM | A further −10% to −25% | Expense ratio, tracking error, manager dispersion |
| User, after tax | Your account | A further −10% to −30% | Slippage, taxes, behavior, sub-optimal sizing |

An **[[investable index]]** follows published rules that a fund can actually track, with real rebalancing dates, real prices and limited capacity. The [[PUT index|CBOE PUT Index]] sells SPX puts by rule (Chapter 10C). The BTOP50 is a live composite of large trend-following funds (Chapter 10D), and MSCI Quality is the index that the quality fund QUAL tracks (Chapter 10F). The live layer wraps such strategies in [[exchange-traded funds|exchange-traded fund]]: PUTW writes puts, DBMF and KMLM follow trends, and MTUM holds momentum stocks.

### Why each layer takes a haircut

**Academic to investable index.** Academic factor portfolios assume what no real index can keep: daily rebalancing, mid-quote pricing with no bid-ask spread, and no transaction costs, or unrealistically low ones. They also assume no borrow costs for short sales, or implausibly easy borrowing, and infinite capacity with no [[market impact]]. Daily rebalancing alone is impractical at scale.

Real-world index construction replaces these idealizations with monthly or quarterly rebalancing, real execution prices, capacity limits, and defined methodologies that may not implement the theoretical signal optimally. The typical haircut is 15% to 30%: larger for strategies that depend on micro-cap exposure, daily rebalancing or dispersed positions, and smaller for liquid index strategies.

One methodology choice can cost more than that: the academic [[betting against beta]] (BAB) portfolio has a BAB Sharpe ratio of 0.78 (Frazzini and Pedersen 2014). A value-weighted version, which weights each stock by its [[market capitalization]], earns 0.49 (Novy-Marx and Velikov 2022). That is a haircut of 37% from a single choice.

**Investable index to ETF or fund.** Wrapping an index in a tradable product adds four frictions:

- An [[expense ratio]], typically 40 to 100 bps a year for factor ETFs and 60 to 100 bps for [[managed futures]] funds. The broad factor ETFs of Chapter 10F charge less, 0.15% for QUAL and for MTUM.
- [[Tracking error]] from cash drag, the timing of dividend reinvestment, and sampling.
- Manager discretion in active funds, or methodology drift in passive ones.
- Tax inefficiency, which depends on the wrapper's structure.

The typical haircut is 10% to 25%, larger for active funds and complex strategies and smaller for vanilla index-tracking ETFs. The put-writing record shows it: the PUT index's Sharpe ratio of about 0.55 to 0.60 becomes a PUTW Sharpe ratio of about 0.30 to 0.45 over 2016 to 2024. PUTW also changed its strategy slightly in 2023, moving to weekly options. Before then it tracked the PUT index more closely. That wrapper drift is a haircut beyond the expense ratio.

**ETF or fund to your account.** For an allocation held as ETFs and left alone, this layer is small. For a strategy you run yourself, it is large, and it has five parts:

- **Slippage.** Your entries and exits never match the index's exactly, so even small slippage compounds over hundreds of trades.
- **Taxes.** XSP options get Section 1256 treatment, with gains taxed 60% as long-term capital gain (LTCG) and 40% as short-term (STCG). SPY and other ETFs get the standard treatment. In a taxable account an ETF's monthly distributions create ordinary-income tax events, and rebalancing realizes capital gains. For an active strategy in a taxable account, the tax drag can be 100 to 200 bps a year.
- **Behavior.** Investors trail the funds they own by 100 to 200 bps a year, most of all in volatile, complex, non-intuitive strategies.
- **Sizing.** Real users under-size on entry, testing a strategy small first, and over-size after wins, wishing they had allocated more.
- **Errors.** Missed rebalances, trading errors and timing mistakes.

The typical haircut for this layer is 10% to 30%: smaller for ETF-only allocations, larger for self-managed options strategies.

## The haircut equation

Each layer multiplies whatever the layer before it left. Write \(\text{SR}_{\text{paper}}\) for the Sharpe ratio on paper, and \(h_i\) for the share of the Sharpe ratio that survives layer \(i\), a number between 0 and 1. The Sharpe ratio you realize in your account is

\[ \text{SR}_{\text{user}} = \text{SR}_{\text{paper}}\; h_1 h_2 h_3 h_4 \]
{#eq:haircut}

The product \(h_1 h_2 h_3 h_4\), also written \(\prod_{i=1}^{4} h_i\), is the share of the paper Sharpe ratio that survives. One minus the product is the total **drag**, the share that is lost.

::: worked Three layers at 25% each
**Step 1: the product.** Take a strategy with an academic Sharpe ratio of 0.8 and a 25% haircut at each of the Edge Book's three layers. Each layer keeps \(h = 0.75\), so together they keep \(0.75^3 = 0.42\).

**Step 2: the result.** By [eq:haircut], \(\text{SR}_{\text{user}} = 0.8 \times 0.42 = 0.34\), a cumulative haircut of \(1 - 0.42 = 58\%\). This is not a pessimistic case: the Edge Book calls it typical.
:::

### Typical values

The Dealer Mechanics Lab gives typical factors for a competent retail swing trader who operates with discipline:

| Factor | Layer | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| \(h_1\) | Out-of-sample decay | 0.70 to 0.85 |
| \(h_2\) | Execution slippage and timing | 0.80 to 0.90 |
| \(h_3\) | Tax friction, which depends heavily on the instrument, XSP or SPY | 0.85 to 0.95 |
| \(h_4\) | Behavior, which depends on your discipline | 0.70 to 0.90 |

Their typical product is 0.35 to 0.58, while multiplying the ends of the four ranges gives 0.33 at worst and 0.65 at best. So a paper Sharpe ratio of 1.0 typically delivers a user Sharpe ratio of 0.4 to 0.6 in practice. **This is the median outcome, not a pessimistic projection.** The Dealer Mechanics Lab also states the equation's typical product more broadly, as 0.30 to 0.60, so that 30% to 60% of the paper Sharpe ratio survives. At the start of its chapter it puts the realized range for a paper 1.0 at 0.3 to 0.5. The product of its own factors, 0.35 to 0.58, lies between those two statements.

The execution factor of 0.80 to 0.90 is a cut of 10% to 20%, milder than the 20% to 30% of the layer's own description. The table's values assume a competent, disciplined trader. Until your own record shows that you are one, plan with the larger cut.

The Edge Book splits its last layer in two and gives its own four factors:

| Factor | Layer | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| \(h_1\) | Academic to investable index | 0.70 to 0.95 |
| \(h_2\) | Investable index to ETF | 0.75 to 0.90 |
| \(h_3\) | ETF to self-managed | 0.80 to 0.95 |
| \(h_4\) | Self-managed to user, after tax | 0.65 to 0.90 |

For most strategies the product falls between 0.30 and 0.60, although the ends of these ranges multiply to 0.27 and 0.73.

Use the equation explicitly: when you read a paper claiming a Sharpe ratio of 0.8, multiply by 0.40 as your default expectation, which gives 0.32. If the strategy is unusually clean, with low complexity, an ETF-implementable form and good tax efficiency, use a higher multiplier of 0.55 to 0.70. If it is unusually friction-heavy, with active timing, several legs or other complexity, use 0.25 to 0.35. The Dealer Mechanics Lab states the same rule from its side: when a strategy description claims 0.85, your default expectation is 0.30 to 0.40 in your account.

### Which layer costs the most

A product is hard to split into parts, but its logarithm is not. Taking natural logarithms of [eq:haircut] turns the chain into a sum (Chapter 1A):

\[ \ln \frac{\text{SR}_{\text{user}}}{\text{SR}_{\text{paper}}} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \ln h_i \]
{#eq:loghaircut}

Since each \(\ln h_i\) is negative, its share of the total is that layer's share of the drag, which makes the layers comparable however many there are.

::: worked Splitting a drag into layers
**Step 1: the logarithms.** Take \(h_1 = 0.80\), \(h_2 = 0.85\), \(h_3 = 0.85\) and \(h_4 = 0.80\). Then \(\ln 0.80 = -0.223\) and \(\ln 0.85 = -0.163\), so by [eq:loghaircut] the sum is \(-0.223 - 0.163 - 0.163 - 0.223 = -0.772\).

**Step 2: the survivor.** The product is \(e^{-0.772} = 0.462\). A paper Sharpe ratio of 1.0 becomes 0.462, and the drag is 53.8%.

**Step 3: the shares.** Out-of-sample decay and behavior each take \(0.223/0.772 = 29\%\) of the drag, and execution and tax each take 21%.
:::

The figure draws the chain as bars, one for the Sharpe ratio left after each layer. Its sliders set the paper Sharpe ratio and the four factors. The starting values are those of the example: a paper 1.0 with factors of 0.80, 0.85, 0.85 and 0.80. ::: warn Common mistake: adding the haircuts
Three haircuts of 25% do not remove 75% of a Sharpe ratio. Each applies to what the one before it left, so together they remove \(1 - 0.75^3 = 58\%\). Adding them overstates the loss. For large haircuts it breaks down entirely: four haircuts of 30% would add up to 120%, an impossible loss, while the product keeps \(0.70^4 = 24\%\). Multiply the survivors instead of adding the cuts.
:::

## Complexity costs more

The haircut varies with the kind of strategy, as the Edge Book's general rule shows:

| Strategy type | Typical haircut |
|---|---|
| Long-only equity ETF | −5% to −15% |
| Factor ETF, such as QUAL or MTUM | −15% to −30% |
| Index put-write in ETF form | −25% to −40% |
| Self-managed put-write | −30% to −50% |
| Self-managed multi-leg options | −40% to −60% |
| Discretionary timing strategies | −50% to −80% |

The haircut grows with complexity, discretion, self-management and active timing. More legs mean more friction, more decision points mean more behavioral risk, self-management adds operational risk and active timing adds missed trades.

The operational implication is to prefer simple implementations, because every additional leg in an options structure must add enough premium or risk-management value to overcome the extra haircut it brings. Often it does not.

## Three strategies, from paper to account

### A swing setup on SPY

The Dealer Mechanics Lab's example is a trend-pullback setup of the kind Chapter 11H formalizes.

::: worked A trend-pullback swing on SPY
**Step 1: the rules.** Enter SPY when its two-day [[relative strength index]], RSI(2), falls below 10 during [[Stage 2|Weinstein stage]], the markup phase (Chapters 11C and 11E). Exit when the price reclaims its 21-day [[exponential moving average]] (21-EMA), or after 3 days if it has not. Size every trade to the same dollar risk.

**Step 2: the paper backtest.** Optimized in-sample on 2015 to 2024, the setup shows a Sharpe ratio of 1.1, an average trade of 4 days, a win rate of 67% and an average win 1.4 times the average loss.

**Step 3: out-of-sample.** Walked forward through 2025 with its parameters frozen, it shows 0.8, and the win rate drops to 60%.

**Step 4: live execution.** Limit orders at entry and exit do not always fill at the mid, and the slippage leaves 0.65.

**Step 5: after tax.** For a top-bracket US retail trader every gain is short-term, taxed at an effective rate of about 40%, which leaves 0.45.

**Step 6: after behavior.** The Sharpe ratio that reaches the account is 0.30 to 0.35.
:::

The paper 1.1 became 0.30 to 0.35, keeping 27% to 32% of itself: a haircut of 68% to 73%. This is normal. Plan for it.

The implied factors are \(0.8/1.1 = 0.73\), \(0.65/0.8 = 0.81\), \(0.45/0.65 = 0.69\), and 0.67 to 0.78 for behavior. The tax factor sits far below the typical 0.85 to 0.95 because the trade is in SPY, where every gain meets the short-term rate. Expressed through XSP instead, the same trade would pay the lower 60/40 blend of Chapter 2E. Tax lowers a Sharpe ratio only to the extent that it takes proportionally more from the average return than from the swings around it. A tax that took the same share of every gain and refunded the same share of every loss would scale both alike and leave the ratio unchanged.

The win rate's fall is worth a second look. Had the average win stayed at 1.4 times the average loss, the expected result per trade would have fallen from \(0.67 \times 1.4 - 0.33 = 0.61\) average losses to \(0.60 \times 1.4 - 0.40 = 0.44\) (Chapter 1C). That fall of 28%, from seven points of win rate, is close to the Sharpe ratio's fall of 27%, from 1.1 to 0.8.

### Put-writing on the PUT index

The Edge Book walks the whole haircut for a [[VRP harvest]], XSP cash-secured monthly puts (Chapter 10C).

::: worked The PUT index, layer by layer
**Step 1: academic.** Bondarenko's 2019 analysis of the PUT index over 1986 to 2018 gives an annual return of 9.54%, a volatility of 9.95% and a Sharpe ratio of about 0.65. Its maximum drawdown was −33%, in the global financial crisis (GFC), and its skewness was −2.09.

**Step 2: investable index.** The PUT index is already a mechanical, investable index, so this layer is small. An academic version would idealize fills further, while the PUT methodology is honest. Its monthly rebalancing and real fill prices on ATM SPX puts, puts struck [[at the money|at-the-money]], leave a Sharpe ratio of about 0.55 to 0.60. The Edge Book puts this haircut at about 5% to 10% in its detailed walk and 10% to 15% in its summary. The Sharpe ratios themselves imply 8% to 15%.

**Step 3: live ETF or self-managed.** PUTW charges an expense ratio of 0.44%, or 44 bps, which the Edge Book rounds to about 50 bps. It tracked the PUT index closely before 2023, then moved to weekly options. Its Sharpe ratio was about 0.40 to 0.50 on its 2016 to 2022 data and 0.30 to 0.45 as realized, a further haircut of about 25% to 35%. If you write XSP puts yourself instead, you skip the wrapper's expense. You pick up slightly worse fills than the index assumes, better tax treatment under Section 1256 than PUTW's distributions get, and operational risk from missed rolls and errors. A self-managed XSP put-write should track close to the PUT index, perhaps slightly worse on Sharpe ratio but better after tax: about 0.45 to 0.55 before behavior.

**Step 4: your account.** Of the four frictions that remain, slippage costs 1 to 3 volatility points per round trip. Tax drag is about 30 to 40 bps a year after Section 1256 treatment in a taxable account. Behavior costs 50 to 150 bps a year through sub-optimal entries and exits and inconsistent sizing. Forced liquidation is a low risk if the puts are cash-secured, and a significant one if they are leveraged. By the Edge Book's estimate, these take a further 15% to 30%. It ends the chain at 0.20 to 0.40, which assumes more than that cut: 15% to 30% off 0.45 to 0.55 would leave about 0.32 to 0.47.
:::

| Layer | Sharpe ratio | Cumulative haircut |
|---|---|---|
| Academic PUT index (Bondarenko) | 0.65 | 0% |
| Investable PUT methodology | 0.55 to 0.60 | −8% to −15% |
| Self-managed XSP equivalent | 0.45 to 0.55 | −15% to −31% |
| User, after tax, with realistic execution | 0.20 to 0.40 | −38% to −69% |

**Plan for a Sharpe ratio of 0.25 to 0.35 if you allocate to this, and treat anything higher as a pleasant surprise.** The haircut is real. The Edge Book's short version of the same chain gives the steps as round haircuts: 10% to 15% for the index, a further 25% to 35% for PUTW, and 10% to 25% more for your account.

### Trend following

The third example follows a strategy with far less leverage and complexity than put-writing.

::: worked Trend following, layer by layer
**Step 1: academic.** Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen's "Century of Evidence" study of trend (2017) covers 67 markets from 1880 to 2016. Its average net Sharpe ratio is about 0.4 *after* fees of 2 and 20, that is, 2% of assets a year plus 20% of profits. Idealized models without manager fees give about 0.8 to 1.2.

**Step 2: investable index.** Live composites of [[commodity trading advisors|commodity trading advisor]] (CTAs), the SG Trend Index and the BTOP50, realized Sharpe ratios of about 0.3 to 0.5 over 2003 to 2024. That is already close to the academic net figure, because Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen included fees, so live experience roughly matches paper.

**Step 3: live ETF.** Three ETFs carry trend: DBMF, a replication fund with a 0.85% expense ratio, KMLM from Mount Lucas at 0.92%, and RSST, a return-stacked fund, at 1.04%. They realized Sharpe ratios of 0.25 to 0.45 over 2018 to 2024, depending on the vehicle. Their tracking error to the SG Trend Index can be 200 to 400 bps a year. Each has its quirks: a replication lag for DBMF, a fixed signal for KMLM.

**Step 4: your account.** Trend has long droughts, such as 2011 to 2018, which test patience, and many investors capitulate. Tax is favorable, since futures-based vehicles get Section 1256 treatment. Sizing suffers too: many users under-allocate during droughts and over-allocate after winning years, for a net of 0.20 to 0.40.
:::

You might expect trend, with less leverage and less complexity, to take a smaller haircut than the VRP. In some ways it does: it carries no risk of margin expansion, gets cleaner tax treatment and has lower path dependency. But its behavioral haircut is large because of its long whipsaw periods. Investors who ride trend through droughts of 4 to 7 years before it pays in a 2008 or a 2022 are rare. Most exit during the drought and miss the payoff.

Trend's value, however, is not in its standalone Sharpe ratio but in how it behaves in crashes. Even a trend Sharpe ratio of 0.20 contributes more to a portfolio's Sharpe ratio than its standalone arithmetic suggests, because of its negative crash beta (Chapter 10D).

## The largest layer is you

Of the four layers, the behavioral one, \(h_4\), is consistently the largest single source of loss. The Edge Book goes further: the behavioral part of the user-account haircut is often larger than all the other parts combined.

### What investors actually earn

Morningstar's annual *Mind the Gap* study has measured the behavioral gap for two decades by comparing two returns on the same funds. A fund's **[[time-weighted return]]** compounds its return period by period, whatever money it held, so it measures what the fund did. Its investors' **[[dollar-weighted return]]** weights each period by the money they actually had in the fund, so it measures what the investors got. Across more than 20 years, the investors have trailed their funds consistently, by 100 to 200 bps a year.

::: worked A fund that gained while its investor lost
**Step 1: the fund.** A fund returns +30% in its first year and −20% in its second. Its time-weighted return over the two years is \(1.30 \times 0.80 - 1 = 4\%\), about 2.0% a year.

**Step 2: the investor.** An investor puts in $10,000 at the start and, impressed by the first year, adds $20,000 at the start of the second. The account holds $13,000 + $20,000 = $33,000 going into the fall and ends at \(33{,}000 \times 0.80 = \$26{,}400\), against $30,000 paid in.

**Step 3: the dollar-weighted return.** It is the single yearly rate \(x\) that grows both deposits to the ending value:

\[ 10{,}000\,(1+x)^2 + 20{,}000\,(1+x) = 26{,}400 \]

Writing \(y = 1 + x\) and dividing by 10,000 gives \(y^2 + 2y - 2.64 = 0\), whose positive root is \(y = -1 + \sqrt{3.64} = 0.908\). The investor earned −9.2% a year while the fund earned +2.0%.
:::

The toy gap is extreme, though its mechanism is the real one: money arrives after gains and leaves after losses. The gap is largest in three kinds of fund:

- **Sector funds**, where investors chase performance, buying after rallies and selling after drops.
- **Complex strategies**, which investors do not understand, so they panic at drawdowns.
- **High-volatility strategies**, which investors enter at peaks and exit at troughs.

By retail standards, premium selling and trend following both qualify as complex and highly volatile. The behavioral gap on them can easily exceed 200 bps a year.

::: familiar
The retail haircut is the gap between efficacy and effectiveness. A regimen's effect in a phase 3 trial, with selected patients, protocol dosing and close follow-up, is its efficacy. Its effect in community practice, with dose reductions, missed cycles and patients the trial would have excluded, is its effectiveness, which you do not expect to equal its efficacy. A backtest reports efficacy, and your account reports effectiveness. Within that, the fund's time-weighted return is the per-protocol analysis. Its investors' dollar-weighted return is the intention-to-treat result, which counts everyone who started, including those who stopped early.
:::

### Six ways a swing trader leaks

For swing trading, the behavioral failure modes are six:

1. **Position-size inconsistency.** Sizing larger after wins and smaller after losses, the reverse of what disciplined trading requires.
2. **[[Stop migration|stop migration]].** Moving a stop further away as the price approaches it, "to give the trade more room". This converts small losses into large ones.
3. **Premature exits on winners.** Closing winners early to "lock in profit" while letting losers run: the classic **[[disposition effect]]**.
4. **Revenge trading.** Taking marginal setups after a loss to "make it back", with predictable consequences.
5. **[[Setup creep|setup creep]].** Lowering the bar for what counts as a valid setup during slow weeks.
6. **News chasing.** Entering a position on news after the move has already happened, rather than on the geometry of a setup.

Chapter 11N gives remedies for setup creep, stop migration and inconsistent sizing among the failures it catalogs. For revenge trading, Chapter 13F's card adds a day-level defense: after taking a planned loss, stop trading for the rest of the session.

### The forced-liquidation cost

Beyond simple panic selling, the behavioral cost includes [[forced liquidation]] (Chapter 1E). When [[portfolio margin]] requirements expand in stress, undisciplined sizing means you are forced to close positions at the worst time. That is not strictly behavioral, since margin rules are structural. But it is often caused by the behavioral failure to size for stress in advance. A single forced liquidation with the VIX at 30 can wipe out 12 to 18 months of accumulated edge.

### The fix is structure

Resolving to behave better than the average investor is not a fix, because most people who make that resolution are wrong. The fix is structural: written rules, entry criteria committed in advance, mechanical position sizing, and [[kill criteria|kill criterion]] committed in advance. Discipline exists only when it binds under stress, which it does only if you wrote it down in calm conditions, before the trade.

::: practice
Six structural defenses reduce the behavioral haircut:

1. **Written rules** for entry, exit and sizing.
2. **Kill criteria** specified in advance (Chapters 13C and 13G).
3. **A behavioral kill criterion** at the portfolio level: a 25% drawdown halts all discretionary tactical sleeves for 30 days.
4. **Mechanical execution** where possible, such as automated rebalances and scheduled rolls.
5. **A strategy diary** recording what you actually did against what the rules said.
6. **A quarterly review** comparing your account's performance with the ETF or index it is supposed to track.

If your account runs consistently 200 bps a year or more behind its benchmark, the gap is behavioral, and only structural changes fix it.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "the behavioral haircut does not apply to me"
The belief is that you behave better than the average investor, so the haircut belongs to someone else. You may be right, though the burden of proof is on you. Most traders who expect to beat the median are wrong. The haircut is consistent across decades of data, including among sophisticated investors. The minority who genuinely outperform their funds do it through specific structural choices, such as automation, written rules, kill criteria and behavioral safeguards, not through self-discipline alone. Without written rules and kill criteria, you experience the average behavioral gap however disciplined you feel.

Might your particular implementation beat the typical retail approach? Maybe. But the prior probability that any given retail trader beats the haircut is low, so plan for the typical haircut and treat outperformance as a positive surprise. Suppose you have run a strategy for 3 years or more with documented kill criteria, written rules and a strategy diary. Suppose too that its realized Sharpe ratio matches or beats the paper expectation. Then you have *some* evidence of better-than-haircut execution. Until then, plan conservatively.
:::

## Plan for the haircut, not against it

The honest framing is uncomfortable. Many sophisticated retail traders spend 100 hours or more implementing a strategy with a paper Sharpe ratio of 0.6, expect to realize 0.5, and actually realize 0.15 after a year. For a swing strategy with a paper Sharpe ratio of 1.0, spending 100 hours on it and realizing 0.35 is the median outcome.

Use the haircut equation to **filter strategies before you commit capital.** If a strategy's user Sharpe ratio after tax does not beat simply holding SPX, the strategy is not worth its operational complexity. Chapter 1D makes the same point from the other side: the competitor for an active strategy is not zero but the equity risk premium you could hold passively.

The fix is not to find better strategies but to plan for the haircut explicitly and to choose the places where it bites least:

1. **Pick strategies whose value does not depend on perfect execution.**
2. **Pick instruments with favorable tax treatment:** XSP, SPX or ES over SPY, for the 60/40 advantage.
3. **Pick setups with simple, mechanical entry and exit rules,** and use ETF implementations where you can.
4. **Commit to position sizing in advance** (Chapter 13C).
5. **Build behavioral safeguards,** the written rules and kill criteria above.
6. **Let go of complex strategies** that do not survive the haircut.

::: warn When it fails
The haircut equation is a planning multiplier, not a measurement. It can mislead in three ways:

- **Your own layer can fall outside the typical range.** The swing example's tax factor was 0.69, against a typical 0.85 to 0.95, because its instrument taxed every gain as ordinary income.
- **A jump is not a haircut.** The equation shrinks a Sharpe ratio smoothly, whereas one forced liquidation can erase 12 to 18 months of edge at once.
- **It inherits the Sharpe ratio's blind spot.** For short-volatility strategies the paper ratio itself flatters, so apply the separate haircut of 30% to 50% for negative skew (Chapter 1D) before comparing them with anything else.
:::

::: rule
Before you allocate to any strategy, answer five questions in writing:

1. What is its academic or paper Sharpe ratio?
2. What is your expected user Sharpe ratio after tax? Multiply by 0.40 by default, by 0.55 to 0.70 for a clean, ETF-implementable strategy, and by 0.25 to 0.35 for a friction-heavy one.
3. Is that user Sharpe ratio still positive after fees and effort, and does it beat holding SPX?
4. Does the strategy add to the portfolio, with a positive contribution to its Sharpe ratio, or does it duplicate exposures you already hold?
5. Can you commit to written rules and kill criteria that stop the behavioral haircut from compounding?

If any answer is "no" or "I do not know", the strategy is not ready for capital.
:::

## Where this leads

Chapter 1B gave you the distributions of returns, Chapter 1G the reasons edges exist, and Chapter 1D the reasons the Sharpe ratio misleads for short volatility. This chapter adds realistic retail expectations. Chapter 13B uses all four to formalize the conviction framework, the admission test that any strategy must pass before it gets capital. Without the haircut you would over-allocate to academically attractive strategies that do not survive implementation. The haircut is the gravity that pulls paper Sharpe ratios back to reality.

::: recap
- A paper Sharpe ratio passes through layers of friction. Each layer multiplies what the last one left: \(\text{SR}_{\text{user}} = \text{SR}_{\text{paper}}\, h_1 h_2 h_3 h_4\).
- Typically 30% to 60% of the paper Sharpe ratio survives. A paper 0.8 routinely becomes 0.20 to 0.40 in a retail account: the median, not the pessimistic case.
- Behavior is the largest single layer: investors have trailed their own funds by 100 to 200 bps a year for two decades. Structure, not willpower, closes that gap.
- Keep a mechanical sleeve of 60% to 80% running while the discretionary sleeve obeys the halts of Chapter 13C.
- Filter before you commit: a strategy whose user Sharpe ratio after tax does not beat holding SPX is not worth running.
:::

::: check
?? Name the Dealer Mechanics Lab's four layers of friction, and say why each one lowers the Sharpe ratio.
!! The paper backtest fills at the mid, with no costs, no taxes and idealized entries, so it is the ceiling. Out-of-sample data removes the overfitting that in-sample optimization builds in. Live execution pays real spreads and slippage and misses clean entries. Tax and behavior take their share last: short-term gains taxed as ordinary income, and early exits, missed setups, inconsistent sizes and revenge trades.

?? A paper strategy has a Sharpe ratio of 0.8, with haircuts that keep 0.85, 0.80, 0.85 and 0.75. What does the account see?
!! By [eq:haircut], \(0.8 \times 0.85 \times 0.80 \times 0.85 \times 0.75 = 0.8 \times 0.434 = 0.35\). About 43% of the paper Sharpe ratio survives, near the middle of the typical 30% to 60%.

?? Why is the behavioral layer often the largest?
!! It acts on every decision, hardest of all when a strategy is volatile or complex. That is when investors buy after rallies, sell after drops and abandon their rules. Mind the Gap has measured the result at 100 to 200 bps a year for two decades. The Edge Book finds the behavioral part often larger than all the other parts combined.

?? Which structural choices reduce the behavioral haircut?
!! Six choices do it: written rules, kill criteria set in advance, a portfolio-level halt after a 25% drawdown, mechanical execution, a strategy diary and a quarterly comparison with a benchmark. Each one moves a decision from a stressed moment to a calm one.

?? A strategy description claims a Sharpe ratio of 1.2 for an actively timed, multi-leg options strategy. What should you expect, and is it worth running?
!! It is friction-heavy, so multiply by 0.25 to 0.35: \(1.2 \times 0.25 = 0.30\) to \(1.2 \times 0.35 = 0.42\). That straddles buy-and-hold SPX's user Sharpe ratio of about 0.40. Unless it adds something the rest of the portfolio lacks, it is not worth the complexity.

?? Why do complex multi-leg options strategies typically take larger haircuts than simple ETF allocations?
!! Every leg pays a spread and adds slippage, every discretionary decision adds behavioral risk, self-management adds operational errors, and active timing adds missed trades. A long-only equity ETF typically loses 5% to 15% of its Sharpe ratio, while self-managed multi-leg options lose 40% to 60%. An extra leg earns its place only if it adds more premium or risk control than the haircut it brings.
:::
