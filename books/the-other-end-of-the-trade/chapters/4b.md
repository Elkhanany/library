## Linear payoffs: futures and forwards

A **[[futures contract]]** is an exchange-traded contract to buy or sell an underlying at a future date for a price agreed today. Write \(S_T\) for the spot price of the underlying at expiration and \(K\) for the contract price. The payoff at expiry is linear in the price of the underlying:

\[ \text{long future at expiry} = S_T - K \]
{#eq:future}

The short side holds the mirror image, \(K - S_T\). Either way the payoff is symmetric: each $1 the underlying ends above \(K\) is $1 of profit to the long, and each $1 below is $1 of loss. Instead of paying an upfront premium, you post **[[initial margin]]** as collateral against potential losses. The position is then marked to market daily, with **[[variation margin]]** added or refunded each evening as the profit or loss is realized.

The E-mini S&P 500 future (ES) has these terms:

- A multiplier of $50 per index point, so that one ES contract at an SPX level of 6000 is $300,000 of notional.
- An initial margin of about $15,000 to $22,000 a contract, depending on the volatility regime.
- A tick, the smallest step in which the price can move, of 0.25 index points, which is worth \(0.25 \times \$50 = \$12.50\) a contract.

::: worked Daily settlement on one ES contract
**Step 1: the evenings.** You buy one ES contract at 6000 and post initial margin, but no premium changes hands. When the contract settles at 6030 on day 1, the exchange credits \(30 \times \$50 = \$1{,}500\) to your account. When it settles at 5985 on day 2, it debits \(45 \times \$50 = \$2{,}250\).

**Step 2: the total.** The two flows net to \(-\$750\), which is \((5985 - 6000) \times \$50\), the payoff of [eq:future] at a price of 5985. Daily settlement pays the straight line as you go, rather than all at the end.
:::

**[[Forwards|forward contract]]** are the over-the-counter analog: the same payoff structure, but bilateral and not standardized. Most institutional currency hedging uses forwards rather than futures, because forwards can be sized and dated precisely.

### What sets the futures price

A futures contract is equivalent to borrowing money to buy the underlying today at the spot price and holding it to the contract date. At expiry you compare the borrowed amount plus financing against the spot price. The futures price is set so that this **[[cost of carry]]** trade, also called cash-and-carry, makes nothing. It therefore builds in the cost of financing, less the expected dividend yield, or plus the storage costs for a physical commodity.

Write \(S_0\) for the spot price today, \(r\) for the risk-free interest rate, \(q\) for the underlying's dividend yield and \(T\) for the time to expiry in years. In these symbols, the price at which the future trades today is

\[ F_0 = S_0\, e^{(r - q)T} \]
{#eq:carry}

::: grind Why the futures price is spot plus carry
**Step 1: buy the underlying with borrowed money.** Dividends reinvested at the yield \(q\) turn each unit into \(e^{qT}\) units by expiry. So buy \(e^{-qT}\) units, costing \(S_0 e^{-qT}\), to end with exactly one, borrowing the whole cost at the rate \(r\).

**Step 2: sell one future at \(F_0\).** Entering a future costs nothing, so the loan still funds the whole position.

**Step 3: settle at expiry.** Your one unit is worth \(S_T\) and the short future pays \(F_0 - S_T\), so together they are worth \(F_0\) whatever \(S_T\) is. You repay the loan, which has grown to \(S_0 e^{-qT} e^{rT}\).

**Step 4: rule out free money.** Your profit is \(F_0 - S_0 e^{(r-q)T}\), fixed on the day you enter. If it were positive, anyone could lock it in with no risk. If it were negative, anyone could run the trade in reverse. Only the price in [eq:carry] leaves no such [[arbitrage]] open to anyone.
:::

The trade in the grind box makes nothing whatever the index does, not just nothing on average, because its outcome is fixed on the day it is entered.

::: worked The basis on one ES contract
**Step 1: the futures price.** Take hypothetical values of 6000 for SPX, 4% for the rate, 1.5% for the dividend yield and three months to expiry. For these, [eq:carry] gives \(6000 \times e^{0.025 \times 0.25} = 6000 \times 1.00627 = 6037.6\).

**Step 2: the basis.** The future trades 37.6 points above spot, which on one contract is \(37.6 \times \$50 = \$1{,}880\). That is close to three months' financing of $300,000 at the 2.5-point gap between the rate and the yield.
:::

This gap between the futures price and spot is the **[[basis]]**. At expiry \(T = 0\), so \(e^{0} = 1\) and the future equals spot. When interest rates are positive and above the dividend yield, ES trades at a premium to SPX that decays to zero at expiration. That decay is the implicit financing cost: the funding cost of ES is baked into the basis.

[FIGURE IDEA: The basis over a contract's life. The futures price from [eq:carry] is drawn against days to expiry, from 90 down to 0, beside a flat spot price. Sliders set the interest rate and the dividend yield. The gap narrows to zero at expiry, and turns into a discount when the yield is set above the rate, with a readout of the basis in points and in dollars per ES contract.]

For a retail trader who wants leveraged SPX exposure without the complications of options, the ES contract is the cleanest leveraged equity exposure available in the US. Combined with the Section 1256 60/40 tax treatment of Chapter 2E, it is the operationally correct vehicle.

## Swaps: linear payoffs in installments

A **[[swap]]** is a derivative that makes recurring payments based on the difference between two reference rates or prices. The most common form is the **[[interest rate swap]]**, in which one party pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate over a series of periodic payments. A standard five-year USD interest rate swap might have these terms:

- A notional of, say, $10 million, which is never exchanged but is used only to compute the payments.
- A fixed rate of, say, 4.5% a year, paid by Party A.
- A floating rate, 3-month [[SOFR]] (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate), paid by Party B.
- Payments every quarter over a term of five years.

Write \(M\) for the notional, \(c\) for the fixed rate and \(L\) for the floating rate set for the quarter. Each leg pays its rate on the notional for a quarter, counted as 90 days of a 360-day year. The net payment to Party A, which pays fixed, is

\[ \text{net}_A = (L - c)\, M\, \tfrac{90}{360} \]
{#eq:swap}

::: worked One quarter of the swap
**Step 1: the two legs.** Each quarter, Party A owes Party B the fixed leg, \(0.045 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 \times 90/360 = \$112{,}500\), whatever SOFR does. Party B owes Party A \(L \times \$10{,}000{,}000 \times 90/360\), which varies as SOFR varies: $100,000 with SOFR at 4.0%, and $125,000 with SOFR at 5.0%.

**Step 2: the net.** Only the difference between the legs changes hands. At 4.0%, [eq:swap] gives \(-\$12{,}500\), which A pays to B. At 5.0% it gives \(+\$12{,}500\), which B pays to A. The net flows from A to B when SOFR is below 4.5%, and from B to A when it is above. Over the five-year life there are 20 such quarterly settlements.
:::

Each settlement is linear in the floating rate, so a swap is a string of forward contracts on the rate, one for each quarter. That is why its payoff family is the straight line paid in installments.

The economic content is a change in the kind of interest rate a party is exposed to. Suppose Party A has borrowed at a floating rate. The SOFR it receives from the swap covers its floating interest, which leaves it paying a fixed 4.5%: it has converted floating-rate funding into a fixed rate. Party B, if it has fixed-rate funding, does the reverse. Swaps are how institutional balance sheets manage interest rate risk at scale. The interest rate swap market is the largest derivative market in the world by notional, with hundreds of trillions of dollars outstanding.

[FIGURE IDEA: A swap's cash flows on a timeline of 20 quarters. The reader draws a path for 3-month SOFR, and each quarter shows the fixed payment from A, the floating payment from B and the net as one arrow, pointing to B when SOFR is below the fixed rate and to A when above. A slider moves the fixed rate. A running total gives each party's cumulative net, and a second panel shows that A's total is the same as holding 20 forward contracts on the rate.]

Other swap families work the same way, each exchanging one stream of payments for another:

- **Currency swaps** exchange streams of cash flows in different currencies.
- **Commodity swaps** exchange a fixed price for a floating commodity price.
- **Total return swaps** have one party pay a fixed or floating rate and the other pay the total return on an underlying asset. Hedge funds use them for synthetic exposure, holding an asset's return without holding the asset.
- **[[Variance swaps|variance swap]]** have one party pay a fixed strike and the other pay the realized variance over the swap period. They are mathematically equivalent to a delta-hedged strip of options (Chapter 7A).
- **Credit default swaps** have one party pay a fixed premium and the other pay the par value of a debt if a reference entity defaults.

Swaps are almost entirely institutional: retail traders rarely encounter them directly.

## Convex payoffs: options

**Options** are derivatives whose payoff is non-linear in the underlying. The holder pays a [[premium]] upfront and receives a payoff at expiration that depends asymmetrically on the underlying. For a [[call option]] and a [[put|put option]] struck at \(K\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{long call} &= \max(S_T - K,\, 0) \\
\text{long put} &= \max(K - S_T,\, 0)
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:options}

The asymmetry is what creates everything interesting about options. The call holder has unlimited upside, because any move above \(K\) becomes payoff, but a loss bounded at the premium paid. The put holder has a large but bounded upside, up to \(K\) if the underlying goes to zero, and a loss also bounded at the premium paid.

Net of a call premium \(C\), the call holder profits at expiry once \(S_T\) passes the breakeven \(K + C\). Net of a put premium \(P\), the put holder profits once \(S_T\) falls below \(K - P\).

[FIGURE: three-payoff-overlay]

The future was one straight line through its breakeven at 100, with no premium to recover. The call was flat at \(-3\) below the strike, then rose one for one, crossing zero at 103. The put was its mirror image, crossing zero at 97. The faint lines behind each choice show that only the options bend.

The **bend** at the strike, where the slope changes from zero to one, is the mathematical source of all option dynamics. The slope of a payoff is its [[delta]], the change in value for a $1 move in the underlying. Linear instruments need no [[Greeks]] beyond delta, because their slope never changes. Convex instruments need three more: gamma for how delta changes, theta for the cost of carrying the bend through time, and vega for how the bend's value depends on expected volatility. Chapter 5C unpacks each of the three in full.

## The convexity premium

The bend in an option's payoff is the source of all option mathematics. Suppose the underlying ends at one of two prices, \(a\) or \(b\), with equal probability. The expected payoff is then \(\tfrac{1}{2}f(a) + \tfrac{1}{2}f(b)\), while the payoff at the expected price is \(f\big(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\big)\). The midpoint test of convexity (Chapter 4A) says the first is at least the second. The same holds for any distribution of \(S_T\), a result called Jensen's inequality:

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(S_T)] \ge f\big(\mathbb{E}[S_T]\big) \]
{#eq:jensen}

Here \(\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]\) is the expected value, and \(f\) is any convex payoff. For a linear payoff the two sides are equal.

::: worked Two spreads of outcomes at a strike of 100
**Step 1: a narrow spread.** Let \(S_T\) be 90 or 110 with equal probability, so its expected value is 100. The call pays 0 or 10, an expected payoff of 5, while the payoff at the expected price is 0. The future pays \(-10\) or \(+10\), an expected payoff of 0, the same as its payoff at 100.

**Step 2: a wider spread.** Let \(S_T\) be 80 or 120 instead, with the same expected value. The call's expected payoff doubles to 10, while the future's stays at 0.
:::

Widening the spread of outcomes, with no change in their average, raised the call's expected payoff and left the future's alone. A convex payoff is worth more the more the underlying may move: volatility has value to the holder of the bend, and costs its writer. That sensitivity to volatility is what vega measures.

The bend also explains why options need hedging and futures do not. A future's slope is 1 at every price, so a hedge of one unit of the underlying stays right however the price moves. An option's slope changes with the price, so a hedge set today is wrong tomorrow. **Linear instruments need no continuous hedging; convex instruments do.** Linear hedge instruments cannot replicate convexity in a single transaction. That is the mathematical engine of the whole dealer-mechanics framework: every formula in Part V, every Greek, and every dealer-hedging mechanic in Part VI.

The economic price of convexity is the option premium. The holder pays it upfront in exchange for the convex payoff. The writer collects it in exchange for assuming the concave, mirror-image liability. Under any complete-market model, of which the [[Black-Scholes model]] is one, the fair premium is exactly the amount that makes the dynamic replication of the option cost zero in expectation (Chapter 7A). Any deviation from this fair value is, in principle, arbitrageable. In practice, the deviation captures the [[variance risk premium]] and the other premia discussed throughout the book (Chapter 10C). So the toy's expected payoff of 5 is not yet a price: which probabilities to use is the subject of Chapter 7A.

## Building everything from the three

Almost every derivative you meet at retail or institutional scale combines these three primitive families and the underlying:

- A **collar** is long stock, a long put and a short call. It is equivalent to a long call spread plus cash equal to the put's strike, as the worked example below shows.
- A **straddle** is a long call and a long put at the same strike, which pay \(|S_T - K|\) together: long convexity in both directions.
- A **callable bond** is a regular bond with a short call option on its own price, so the issuer keeps the right to retire the bond early.
- A **convertible bond** is a regular bond with a long call option on the issuer's stock.
- An **autocallable note** is a complex combination of swaps and options that pays a coupon contingent on the underlying not crossing certain barriers.

Any payoff made of straight segments can be taken apart by its slopes. Start from its value at \(S_T = 0\), which is cash, and its slope there, which is units of the underlying. Then, at each kink, the change in slope is the number of calls struck there, bought if the slope rises and sold if it falls.

::: worked Taking a collar apart
**Step 1: the collar.** Hold one share bought at 100, a put struck at 95 and a short call struck at 110. At expiry the position is worth 95 below 95, the share price between 95 and 110, and 110 above.

**Step 2: read the slopes.** The value at zero is 95 and the slope there is 0. The slope rises from 0 to 1 at 95, so buy one 95 call. It falls from 1 to 0 at 110, so sell one 110 call.

**Step 3: check three prices.** The decomposition is 95 of cash plus a 95 to 110 call spread:

| \(S_T\) | Collar | Cash | 95 call | short 110 call | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | 95 | 95 | 0 | 0 | 95 |
| 100 | 100 | 95 | 5 | 0 | 100 |
| 130 | 110 | 95 | 35 | \(-20\) | 110 |

Chapter 5F derives the same equivalence from put-call parity.
:::

::: familiar
The slope rule is the linear spline of regression modeling. A regression with a knot at 95 and another at 110 fits an intercept, a slope and one hinge term, \(\max(x - \text{knot},\, 0)\), per knot, each coefficient being the change in slope there. An option payoff built from straight segments is exactly such a spline in \(S_T\). The knots are the strikes, the hinge terms are calls, and the coefficients are how many of each you hold.
:::

The taxonomy gets baroque quickly. The principle stays simple: **every derivative is composed of futures, swaps and options on some underlying**. If you can decompose a complex structured product into its linear and convex pieces, you can hedge it. If you cannot decompose it, you cannot price it, and you cannot safely sell it. This is the foundation of the structured products desks at investment banks.

::: practice
If you want leveraged SPX exposure, use ES rather than options: it has no premium, its financing is built into the basis, and it gets the 60/40 tax treatment of Chapter 2E. Size it by notional, not by margin, since one contract at 6000 moves $3,000 for a 1% move in the index whatever margin you posted (Chapters 1E and 3D). Buy an option only when you want its bend, a capped loss or protection, because the premium is the price of that bend.
:::

::: warn When it fails
A future's payoff is a straight line at expiry, but it is settled every evening. At 6000, a 5% fall is 300 points, or \(300 \times \$50 = \$15{,}000\) per contract: the whole of an initial margin at the low end of its range. A margin call can close the position before the line is ever reached, which is the path dependency of Chapter 1E.
:::

::: rule
Match the instrument to the shape you need.

- **For a linear view**, use a linear instrument, such as ES, and size it by notional.
- **For a capped loss or protection**, use an option, and treat the premium as the price of the bend.
- **If you cannot decompose a product** into futures, swaps and options, do not buy it or sell it.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "a future costs nothing"
No premium is not no cost. The financing is in the basis: in the example above, the future sat about $1,880 per contract above spot. That premium decays to zero by expiry, and you pay it whether or not the index moves.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading the payoff diagram as today's value
A payoff diagram shows value at expiry only. Before expiry, an option's price is a smooth curve rather than the hinge, one that moves with volatility and with time (Chapter 5B).
:::

::: recap
- A future or forward pays \(S_T - K\): a straight line with no premium, settled daily for a future. Its price is spot plus carry, \(S_0 e^{(r-q)T}\), so the basis decays to zero at expiry.
- A swap is a string of forwards on a rate. An interest rate swap exchanges only the net of its fixed and floating legs on a notional.
- An option bends at its strike, so by Jensen's inequality its expected payoff rises with volatility. That is why options carry a premium and need continuous hedging.
- Every derivative decomposes into futures, swaps and options. The change in slope at each kink counts the calls.
:::

::: check
?? You buy one ES contract at 6000, which settles at 6045 on day 1 and at 5970 on day 2. What variation margin moves each evening, and what is the total?
!! The exchange credits \(45 \times \$50 = \$2{,}250\) on day 1 and debits \(75 \times \$50 = \$3{,}750\) on day 2. The total, \(-\$1{,}500\), equals the payoff at 5970, \((5970 - 6000) \times \$50\).

?? With SPX at 6000, a rate of 5%, a dividend yield of 1% and six months to expiry, what should the future trade at, and what is the basis on one ES contract?
!! By [eq:carry] the future should trade at \(6000 \times e^{0.04 \times 0.5} = 6000 \times 1.0202 = 6121.2\), a basis of 121.2 points, or \(121.2 \times \$50 = \$6{,}060\) per contract.

?? In the chapter's swap, SOFR sets at 3.8% for a quarter: who pays whom, and how much?
!! Party B owes \(0.038 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 \times 90/360 = \$95{,}000\) on the floating leg, against the $112,500 that A owes on the fixed leg. By [eq:swap] the net is \(-\$17{,}500\): A pays B $17,500.

?? A long straddle at a strike of 100 costs 3 for the call and 3 for the put. Where are its breakevens, and what does Jensen's inequality say if the index ends at 90 or 110 with equal probability?
!! The straddle pays \(|S_T - 100|\) against a cost of 6, so it breaks even at 94 and 106. Its expected payoff is 10 against a payoff of 0 at the expected price of 100: the convex payoff is worth more than its value at the average.

?? A payoff is 0 below 100, rises one for one from 100 to 110, and stays at 10 above 110. Which options build it, and how many of each?
!! The value and slope at zero are both 0, so there is no cash and no underlying. The slope rises by 1 at 100 and falls by 1 at 110: buy one 100 call and sell one 110 call, a call spread.
:::
