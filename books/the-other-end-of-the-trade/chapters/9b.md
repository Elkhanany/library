## The rise of same-day options

The most consequential shift in modern equity microstructure is the rise of **zero-days-to-expiration options**, or **[[0DTE]]** options, which expire on the day they trade. From under 5% of SPX volume in 2016, 0DTE reached 62.4% of all SPX volume in August 2025 and 60% in September 2025. Its monthly averages ran between 56% and 62% across all of 2025. Average daily 0DTE volume hit 2.4 million contracts in August 2025. Total SPX options volume hit a record 4.26 million contracts a day in September 2025.

Industry-wide, 0DTE was approximately 24% of all US listed options volume by late 2024. The Dealer Mechanics Lab describes it as on track to grow further. It calls this the largest single shift in options market structure since the launch of SPX weekly options in 2005.

## Why expiry concentrates gamma

The mechanical reason 0DTE matters is the gamma formula of Chapter 6D: **gamma grows as \(1/\sqrt{T}\)**. At the money \(d_1\) is close to zero, so \(\varphi(d_1)\) is close to its peak of 0.399, and at-the-money gamma is about \(0.399/(S\sigma\sqrt{T})\). With spot and volatility held fixed, the ratio of at-the-money gamma at two times to expiry is therefore

\[ \frac{\Gamma(T_1)}{\Gamma(T_2)} \approx \sqrt{\frac{T_2}{T_1}} \]
{#eq:ratio}

::: worked Four hours against thirty days
**Step 1: put both times in hours.** Thirty days is \(30 \times 24 = 720\) hours. Only the ratio of the two times enters [eq:ratio], so the unit does not matter.

**Step 2: take the square root.** At-the-money gamma four hours before expiry is \(\sqrt{720/4} = \sqrt{180} = 13.4\) times its 30-day value. Half an hour before expiry it is \(\sqrt{1{,}440} = 37.9\) times.
:::

Computing \(T\) in years for consistency, the Dealer Mechanics Lab tabulates the divergence:

| Days to expiry | \(T\) (years) | \(\sqrt{T}\) | Gamma ratio against 30 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 days | 0.0822 | 0.2867 | 1.0× (baseline) |
| 7 days | 0.0192 | 0.1385 | 2.07× |
| 1 day | 0.00274 | 0.0523 | 5.48× |
| 4 hours | \(4.57 \times 10^{-4}\) | 0.0214 | **13.4×** |
| 30 minutes | \(5.71 \times 10^{-5}\) | 0.00756 | **37.9×** |
| 5 minutes | \(9.51 \times 10^{-6}\) | 0.00308 | **93.0×** |

These ratios apply to at-the-money gamma. Away from the strike, time works the other way: as \(T\) shrinks, gamma compresses into the at-the-money strike and out-of-the-money gamma collapses, which is the negative color of Chapter 5D.

This concentration produces the strange 0DTE consequence. When a dealer is short a slightly out-of-the-money 0DTE call and spot ticks above the strike, delta jumps from near zero to near one almost discontinuously. **The dealer must buy a large block of stock at that strike.**

::: worked One strike, one hour before the close
**Step 1: the call's delta across the strike.** Take a 0DTE call struck at 6,000 with one hour left, [[implied volatility]] at 15% and a zero rate, so \(T = 1/8{,}760\) of a year. Spot's one-sigma move over that hour is \(6{,}000 \times 0.15 \times \sqrt{1/8{,}760} = 9.6\) points. With spot at 5,988 the delta is 0.106, and with spot at 6,012 it is 0.894.

**Step 2: the dealer's buying.** A dealer short 1,000 of these calls must hedge \((0.894 - 0.106) \times 100 \times 1{,}000 = 78{,}800\) more share-equivalents as spot crosses those 24 points, a move of 0.4%. The same call with 30 days left would need only about 3,700.

**Step 3: the ratio.** The hour-before-close hedge is about 21 times the 30-day one across this range, close to the \(\sqrt{720} = 26.8\) of [eq:ratio] at the strike itself.
:::

When many dealers face this near the same level, the Dealer Mechanics Lab calls the result the "magnet": price snapping to a strike where hedging flow concentrates. Which way that flow pushes depends on the sign of the dealers' gamma. Dealers short the calls buy as spot rises through the strike, which presses the move on. Dealers long options at the strike sell above it and buy below it, which holds price near it, the pinning of Chapter 9C.

## Did 0DTE break the market?

Given the gamma maths above, a reasonable concern is whether 0DTE has destabilized markets. The empirical answer, repeatedly confirmed by Cboe's own data, by Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024), and by the 2025 successor paper of Adams and colleagues, is **no: on average it has reduced [[realized volatility]].** The reasoning is subtle and worth unpacking.

### How big the hedging flow is

Cboe's analysis, "Much Ado About 0DTEs" by Mandy Xu (September 2023), found that **net market-maker gamma in 0DTE positions has a median of about +$173 million per percentage point**. Its 25th to 75th percentile range runs from −$1.1 billion to +$2.4 billion. "Per percentage point" means that for each 1% move in SPX, the median day's 0DTE hedging trades about $173 million of the index. For context, S&P futures trade about $400 billion in daily liquidity.

::: worked The flow as a share of the market
**Step 1: the median day.** A 1% move asks for $173 million of hedging against $400 billion of daily futures liquidity, which is \(173/400{,}000 = 0.043\%\).

**Step 2: a busier day.** Even at the 75th percentile, $2.4 billion per point, a 1% move asks for \(2{,}400/400{,}000 = 0.6\%\) of a day's futures liquidity.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: The hedging flow against the market. The reader picks a point in Cboe's range of net 0DTE market-maker gamma, from the 25th percentile's −$1.1 billion through the median's +$173 million to the 75th percentile's +$2.4 billion per point, and a move in SPX from 0.5% to 3%. A bar gives the hedging flow in dollars and as a share of $400 billion of daily futures liquidity, and its color shows whether the flow damps or pushes the move. It would show how small the median day's flow is, and how the sign rather than the size decides its effect.]

The Dealer Mechanics Lab puts the 0DTE hedging flow at about 0.04% to 0.17% of futures liquidity at the median: **meaningful but not market-moving on a typical day**. The Edge Book cites a 2025 Cboe estimate of the same order, 0DTE market-maker hedging at about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity. It draws from that a more skeptical conclusion about trading on dealer gamma (Chapters 1G and 10H).

::: familiar
A 0DTE contract's gamma is like a large hazard ratio on a rare event. The relative effect is enormous: 13 times the 30-day gamma four hours out. Yet the absolute flow it produces is a small fraction of the market. A treatment that triples the risk of an event with a 0.1% baseline adds 0.2 percentage points of absolute risk. You judge its clinical weight by that absolute number, and 0DTE hedging by its share of the day's liquidity.
:::

### The academic evidence

Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024), which the Dealer Mechanics Lab calls the academic gold standard on this topic, found:

> "... Market Makers' inventory, as measured by the net gamma of their positions, is on average positive and negatively related to future intraday volatility."
>
> Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024)

The authors add that their evidence is "consistent with delta-hedging but inconsistent with information-based trading." The Dealer Mechanics Lab's translation runs as follows. Most 0DTE flow is retail and small institutions *selling* premium, through credit spreads, [[iron condors|iron condor]] and [[cash-secured puts|cash-secured put]]. The [[dealers|market maker]] *buy* it, so they are **long gamma in aggregate in 0DTE**. Their hedging, which sells rallies and buys dips, is therefore *stabilizing*. That is the opposite sign from the original SqueezeMetrics convention (Chapter 8A). It explains why intraday volatility has compressed even as headline option volume has exploded.

Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025), posted December 3, 2025, extend this finding causally and identify the mechanism more precisely. They exploit a natural experiment: Tuesday and Thursday [[SPXW]] expirations were not available for trading until April 26 and May 19, 2022 respectively. Using this discontinuity, they show:

> "The presence of 0DTEs dampens market volatility through a shift in market makers' hedging needs. Importantly, this shift occurs not due to trading 0DTEs on their expiration day but due to positions in longer-dated options accumulated earlier and becoming 0DTEs."
>
> Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025)

This is critical. The dampening works through *longer-dated positions becoming 0DTEs* as time passes, not through trading on the expiration day. Once Tuesday and Thursday joined Monday, Wednesday and Friday, every weekday had an expiration. The structural existence of more 0DTE expirations changes the dynamics of dealer hedging across the whole life cycle of an option, not just on the day it expires.

Brogaard, Han and Won (2024), a University of Utah working paper, independently confirmed that the introduction of 0DTE did not increase market volatility. They used an event-study methodology around the staggered introduction of the Tuesday and Thursday SPXW expirations.

::: familiar
The two new expiration days arrived on two dates, 23 days apart, which makes them a stepped rollout of a single intervention. It is the logic of a stepped-wedge design, in which the same market is observed before and after each step. The second step gives a second chance to see the effect. The steps were not randomized, so anything else that changed around those dates is a candidate confounder. That is why an independent event study pointing the same way adds real weight.
:::

The academic consensus, as of late 2025 and early 2026, is that **0DTE has stabilized rather than destabilized SPX intraday markets on average**. It still allows episodic destabilization when dealer positioning flips negative.

The reader steps through the two dates. It would show why the papers can compare the same market with and without an extra expiry, and why the effect lives in positions opened days earlier.]

## Quiet most days, sharp on event days

The same data show that 0DTE can be episodically destabilizing, and the right tail is real. The dangerous days are event days: the Federal Reserve's rate decisions (FOMC), consumer price releases (CPI) and the monthly jobs report (nonfarm payrolls, NFP). When net dealer 0DTE gamma flips short on such a day, the small [[notional]] combines with the enormous per-contract gamma. The result is concentrated flow that can amplify intraday moves. The character of the modern market is therefore "**quiet most days, sharp on event days**": punctuated equilibria, driven by which side of gamma dealers sit on at the moment.

The April 2025 "Liberation Day" tariff-related sell-off and the October 10, 2025 follow-through both showed this dynamic. The second of these set the single-day options volume record of 110 million contracts. On those days, dealer gamma in 0DTE was likely net short, because retail was buying directional protection rather than selling premium. The hedging flow then amplified the directional move.

## The intraday charm clock

Charm, the drift of delta with time, scales with \(1/T\) (Chapter 5D). For a 0DTE option that factor builds aggressively through the day. Its delta decays smoothly through the morning, then accelerates dramatically after lunch. By 3:30 PM Eastern Time (ET), charm has typically delivered most of its mechanical flow.

::: worked A call struck 0.2% above spot, from the open to the close
Take a 0DTE call struck at 6,012 with spot held at 6,000, implied volatility at 15% and a zero rate. The option expires at the 4:00 PM close, so at 9:30 it has 6.5 hours left.

| Time (ET) | Hours left | Delta |
|---|---|---|
| 9:30 AM | 6.5 | 0.313 |
| 11:30 AM | 4.5 | 0.279 |
| 1:30 PM | 2.5 | 0.216 |
| 3:00 PM | 1.0 | 0.106 |
| 3:30 PM | 0.5 | 0.039 |

**Step 1: the morning.** From 9:30 to 11:30 the delta falls by 0.034, with spot unchanged.

**Step 2: the afternoon.** From 1:30 to 3:30 it falls by 0.177, more than five times the morning's pace. By 3:30 it has lost 0.274 of its 0.313, 88% of the day's decay.

**Step 3: the dealer.** A dealer long 1,000 of these calls, bought from a premium seller, opens with a short hedge of 31,300 share-equivalents. With spot never moving, charm alone makes it buy back 17,700 of them between 1:30 and 3:30.
:::

This is one reason the **last 30 minutes** of the day have become even more mechanical in the 0DTE era. That window was already concentrated for end-of-day rebalancing, as Baltussen et al. (2021) documented (Chapter 6C). Three flows now arrive in the same 30 minutes:

- Charm-driven dealer rebalancing in 0DTE options.
- End-of-day delta rebalancing of longer-dated dealer positions.
- Mechanical flow from passive funds in the [[closing auction]].

For retail traders the implication is that **the last 30 minutes are not a normal trading window**. Strategies that work in the middle of the day may not work in the close. Strategies that explicitly target the close, such as [[market-on-close orders|market-on-close order]] (MOC) into rebalancing flow, work differently from strategies that try to fade the close with mean reversion against mechanical flow.

A shaded area marks the last 30 minutes, and a readout gives the share of the day's decay done by 3:30 and the hedge a 1,000-contract dealer trades in each hour. It would show the slow morning, the steep afternoon and the near-flat last half hour for strikes a little out of the money.]

## Who trades 0DTE

Cboe's late-2025 disclosures, through Mandy Xu's monthly volatility digest, show that **retail trading makes up roughly 50% to 54% of total SPX 0DTE volume** as of mid-2025. The retail share has grown structurally as Robinhood, in January 2025, and other retail brokers expanded access to index options.

Importantly, retail 0DTE flow is mostly *premium selling*, credit spreads and iron condors, not directional purchases. This is what keeps dealers net long gamma in 0DTE on most days. Suppose retail flow flipped to net premium buying, for example during a sustained bull market with aggressive retail call buying. The regime would then flip, with dealers net short gamma in 0DTE. At that point the same product that has been stabilizing markets could become destabilizing.

The character of 0DTE markets, in other words, depends critically on the *direction* of retail demand. The Cboe data show that this direction is selling premium, which is stabilizing. Nothing ensures that it stays so. Chapter 8A's warning applies in full: every sign here is an assumption about who holds what.

A bar shows the dealers' net 0DTE gamma, green when long and red when short, with the flip point marked. It would show how far the mix of the 50% to 54% retail share has to swing before the stabilizing regime turns over.]

::: practice
If you sell 0DTE premium, you are short the gamma this chapter describes. An hour before the close, a 0.4% move across your short strike can swing its delta from 0.1 to 0.9, about 21 times what the same move does to a 30-day option. A defined-risk spread caps the loss, but not the speed at which you reach it. The Edge Book also counts 0DTE premium selling as one more short-volatility sleeve, which in a liquidity event moves with every other one (Chapter 10H).

If you trade the index rather than its options, the chapter still reaches your account twice. Event days can turn the usual damping into amplification. The last 30 minutes run on flows that have nothing to do with the news.
:::

::: warn When it fails
The stabilizing result is an average over days. It says nothing about the event days in the right tail, when dealer 0DTE gamma can turn short, as it likely did in April and October 2025. It also rests on the direction of retail flow, which Cboe measures but which can change. Every public estimate of dealer gamma assumes who holds what (Chapter 8A), so a vendor's reading can have the wrong sign on exactly the day it matters.
:::

::: rule
- **On FOMC, CPI and NFP days**, assume dealer 0DTE gamma may be short. Do not sell 0DTE premium into the release at your usual size.
- **Size a short 0DTE option for its afternoon gamma**, not its morning delta: the size you could hold through a 0.4% move across the strike in the last hour.
- **Treat the last 30 minutes as a separate window.** Decide in advance whether a trade there goes with the closing flow, as an MOC order does, or against it. Do not carry a midday strategy into it by default.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading 0DTE volume as dealer risk
Volume says how much traded, not who holds what. Half of 0DTE volume being retail is consistent with dealers net long gamma, and so a calmer market, as long as that retail flow sells premium. A day of heavy 0DTE volume is dangerous only when the flow has turned to buying.
:::

::: recap
- At-the-money gamma grows as \(1/\sqrt{T}\): 13.4 times its 30-day value four hours before expiry, 37.9 times at 30 minutes and 93 times at 5 minutes.
- Near expiry a dealer's hedge jumps from almost nothing to almost everything as spot crosses the strike.
- On average 0DTE has reduced SPX realized volatility, because dealers are net long 0DTE gamma, and Adams et al. (2025) trace the effect to longer-dated positions becoming 0DTEs.
- Event days are the exception: "quiet most days, sharp on event days".
- Charm delivers most of a 0DTE option's delta decay by 3:30 PM, which makes the last 30 minutes a mechanical window.
:::
