## A right, not an obligation

An option is a contract that gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a set quantity of an [[underlying]] asset at a price fixed in advance. That price is the **[[strike price]]**, also called the exercise price. The right lasts until a set date, the **[[expiry]]** or expiration, and using it is called **[[exercising|exercise]]** the option.

To acquire the right, the holder pays a fee that is never refunded, called the **[[premium]]**. The seller of the option, called its **writer**, keeps the premium whatever happens at expiry. In return the writer accepts a *conditional obligation*: if the holder exercises, the writer must honor the other side of the contract.

This asymmetry, with rights on one side and obligations on the other, is the cornerstone of every option's value. The premium is the price of that asymmetric right.

::: plain
An option is an insurance policy written on a price. A put works like fire insurance on a house. You pay a premium up front, in return for which the writer makes up the difference if the index falls below the strike. If the index does not fall that far, the policy lapses and the writer keeps the premium. A call is the same kind of policy written on a rise.
:::

### Calls and puts

A **[[call option]]** gives its holder the right to *buy* the underlying at the strike. The holder profits when the underlying ends above the strike by more than the premium paid. The maximum loss is the premium, while the maximum gain is unbounded in theory: every dollar above the strike adds a dollar to the payoff.

A **[[put option]]** gives its holder the right to *sell* the underlying at the strike. The holder profits when the underlying ends below the strike by more than the premium paid. The maximum gain is bounded but large: the strike minus the premium, reached if the underlying goes to zero. The maximum loss is again the premium paid.

Either type can be bought, which makes you [[long|long position]] the option, or sold (also called written), which makes you [[short|short position]] it. Two types and two sides make the four primitive positions: long call, short call, long put and short put. Every more complex strategy in Part XII is built from these four.

### In, at and out of the money

**[[Moneyness]]** describes where the strike \(K\) sits relative to the underlying's price \(S\), which tells you whether exercising right now would pay:

- A call is **[[in-the-money]]** (ITM) when \(K < S\), and a put when \(K > S\), so that exercising now would pay something.
- An option is **[[at-the-money]]** (ATM) when its strike is at, or very close to, the price \(S\).
- A call is **[[out-of-the-money]]** (OTM) when \(K > S\), and a put when \(K < S\), so that exercising now would pay nothing.

With the index at 6,000, a 5,900 call is in the money and a 5,900 put is out of the money. Chapter 5B shows how moneyness shapes an option's price.

### European and American exercise

Options are also classified by when they can be exercised. A **[[European-style option]]** can be exercised only at expiry, and an **[[American-style option]]** at any time up to expiry. The right to exercise early cannot have negative value, so an American option is worth at least as much as its European counterpart. The difference can be material when dividends or interest rates create a reason to exercise early.

US-listed options on single stocks and [[exchange-traded funds|exchange-traded fund]], such as SPY, AAPL and NVDA, are American-style. US-listed options on broad-based indexes, such as [[SPX]], [[XSP]], NDX, RUT and VIX, are European-style. They are also settled in cash: under **[[cash settlement]]**, exercise pays the in-the-money amount in dollars rather than delivering anything. The European style is a structural consequence of that cash settlement, because with no underlying to deliver, the early-exercise mechanism does not apply.

Most academic option pricing assumes European exercise because it is tractable. The practical adjustments for American exercise are well understood but add complexity. Pricing an American option means solving a free-boundary partial differential equation (PDE), in which the price at which early exercise pays must be found along with the option's value. The Black-Scholes formula of Chapter 5C is the cleaner European answer.

For the rest of this book, "option" means a European-style, cash-settled index option unless the text says otherwise: the laboratory specimen for SPX dealer mechanics. Chapter 5F takes up American-style exercise and [[physical settlement]], where shares change hands.

## The four positions at expiry

At expiry an option is worth exactly what exercising it pays. Write \(S_T\) for the underlying's price at expiry, \(K\) for the strike and \(P\) for the premium, each per share or per point of the index. A call pays the amount by which \(S_T\) exceeds \(K\), or nothing:

\[ \text{call payoff} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0) \]
{#eq:call}

Here \(\max(a, b)\) means the larger of the two numbers, so a payoff is never below zero. A put pays the amount by which \(S_T\) falls short of \(K\), or nothing:

\[ \text{put payoff} = \max(K - S_T,\, 0) \]
{#eq:put}

The buyer's profit or loss (P&L) at expiry is the payoff minus the premium. The writer's P&L is the premium minus the payoff, which is the buyer's P&L with its sign flipped. Before costs, whatever the holder gains the writer loses, which gives the four positions in this table:

| Position | P&L at expiry | Breakeven | Max profit | Max loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long call | \(\max(S_T - K, 0) - P\) | \(K + P\) | Unbounded | \(P\) |
| Short call | \(P - \max(S_T - K, 0)\) | \(K + P\) | \(P\) | Unbounded |
| Long put | \(\max(K - S_T, 0) - P\) | \(K - P\) | \(K - P\) (as \(S_T \to 0\)) | \(P\) |
| Short put | \(P - \max(K - S_T, 0)\) | \(K - P\) | \(P\) | \(K - P\) (as \(S_T \to 0\)) |

The **[[breakeven]]** is the price at expiry where the P&L is zero. For a long call above the strike, set \(S_T - K - P = 0\) and solve to get \(S_T = K + P\). The short call's P&L is the same expression negated, so it crosses zero at the same price. A short position breaks even exactly where the long one does, because one side's gain is the other side's loss.

The short call's maximum loss is unbounded in theory and, in practice, bounded only by how high the underlying can go. The short put's maximum loss, \(K - P\), is substantial, because the put can be exercised against you with the underlying near zero.

::: worked One strike, four positions
**Step 1: set up.** Take a strike of 100 and a premium of $3, the starting values of the figure below, with one contract covering 100 shares.

**Step 2: the long call.** With the underlying at 110 at expiry, the call pays \(110 - 100 = 10\) by [eq:call], so the P&L is \(10 - 3 = +7\) a share, or +$700 a contract. At 95 the call pays nothing, and you lose the $3 premium, or $300 a contract. The breakeven is \(100 + 3 = 103\), where the payoff just repays the premium.

**Step 3: the short call.** The writer's P&L is the mirror image, −$700 a contract at 110 and +$300 at 95, with the breakeven still at 103.

**Step 4: the puts.** The long put breaks even at \(100 - 3 = 97\) and, with the underlying at 90, pays 10 by [eq:put] for a P&L of +$7 a share. Its writer keeps at most $3 a share and loses at most \(100 - 3 = 97\) a share, which is $9,700 a contract, if the underlying goes to zero.
:::

## What each position is a bet on

Each position suits a different view of the market, its natural thesis. The thesis involves both direction and **[[implied volatility]]** (IV), the volatility that an option's price implies (Chapter 5E). The loss of a [[naked|naked option]] short call, one written without holding the underlying to cover it, is unbounded.

| Position | Thesis |
|---|---|
| Long call | Strong up move expected; IV cheap relative to the expected move |
| Short call | Stock flat or modestly down; IV rich relative to your forecast |
| Long put | Strong down move expected; or a hedge for long stock |
| Short put | Stock flat or modestly up; a willing buyer at K; IV rich |

The asymmetry between the two sides is *the* fundamental feature of options trading. The long sides have defined risk and theoretically unlimited reward, while the short sides have limited reward and large or unlimited risk.

Every "income strategy" you meet is some structured version of selling premium. It accepts the asymmetric risk in exchange for collecting positive expected value through the **[[variance risk premium]]**, the excess of what options charge over the volatility that follows. Chapter 1C showed why a high win rate cannot prove that expected value is positive, and Chapter 10C weighs the evidence for the premium. Every "lottery ticket" strategy is the opposite, paying premium up front for a chance at the convex upside. The two are mirror images, traded at different population frequencies for different reasons.

### Why these four matter

Every options strategy you meet is a specific combination of these four primitives, with the underlying itself added in some. That covers a [[covered call]], an [[iron condor]], a jade lizard, a [[calendar spread]] and a broken-wing butterfly. Knowing the four cold, including their P&L asymmetry and natural thesis, is the difference between memorizing strategy names and understanding what you are trading. Part XII uses this composition principle to take apart fifty-odd named strategies, a deconstruction that is trivial once you have internalized the four primitives.

## Contract size: the multiplier

Option prices are quoted per share or per index point, but you trade whole contracts. The **[[contract multiplier]]** converts one into the other:

- **US equity and ETF options:** a multiplier of 100, so one contract controls 100 shares and a premium of $3.00 on the chain is $300 of cash per contract.
- **SPX index options:** $100 times the index *level*, since there are no shares to count.
- **ES futures options:** $50 per index point, on the E-mini S&P 500 [[futures contract]].
- **VIX options:** $100 per point of the VIX index.

Converting any quoted amount into dollars takes one multiplication, with \(m\) the multiplier and \(n\) the number of contracts:

\[ \text{dollars} = \text{quote} \times m \times n \]
{#eq:dollars}

The multiplier is the most common source of "I thought I bet smaller than that" mistakes in retail accounts.

::: worked Selling 10 XSP puts
**Step 1: the order.** An at-the-money XSP put trades at a premium of $4.50, and you sell 10 contracts. A trader who reads the chain's "$4.50" as the risk and thinks of it as $45 of risk is off by a factor of 100.

**Step 2: the premium.** By [eq:dollars], you receive \(4.50 \times 100 \times 10\), which is $4,500 of premium.

**Step 3: the obligation.** At the strike of 580, the potential assignment liability is \(580 \times 100 \times 10\), or $580,000. That is what a cash-secured version of the trade must hold as collateral.

**Step 4: the same trade on SPX.** SPX is 10 times larger than XSP, so ten SPX puts at the matching strike of 5,800 would bring in $45,000 against an obligation of $5,800,000.
:::

The XSP multiplier saves you from sizing errors that would be catastrophic on SPX. It does not save you from misunderstanding what you have sold.

The discipline is to multiply the per-contract values by the multiplier and the number of contracts every time you place an options order, and to do it *before* clicking submit. The two-second pause has saved more retail accounts than any indicator stack.

::: practice
- **Index options behave like the book's default.** SPX and XSP options cannot be exercised before expiry and settle in cash at expiry. A short SPX put held to expiry costs its in-the-money amount times $100, and no shares change hands.
- **Stock and ETF options add early assignment.** A short American-style put on SPY or a single stock can be assigned on any day, delivering 100 shares per contract (Chapter 12H covers exercise and assignment).
- **The payoff is fixed only at expiry.** Until then the option's market price moves every day, carrying your margin with it (Chapter 1E).
:::

::: warn When it fails
The expiry table describes where a position ends, not how it gets there. Before expiry an option's price also depends on time and volatility (Chapter 5B), so a short put can show a large loss while the index is still above its strike. The table says nothing about how likely each ending price is, either (Chapter 1C). American-style options add early assignment, which the European picture leaves out.
:::

::: rule
Before any options order, work through three steps:

- **Name the position** as one of the four primitives, or as a sum of them.
- **Write down its breakeven and its maximum loss in dollars**, with [eq:dollars]. The maximum loss is unbounded for a short call and, for a short put, the strike less the premium, times the multiplier and the number of contracts.
- **Do not place the order** if that dollar loss is one the account could not survive. Reduce the size until it is survivable, sizing for the path as well as for expiry (Chapter 1E).
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading a quote as the price of a contract
A premium of $4.50 is quoted per share or per index point. The contract costs 100 times that, and a short put's obligation is 100 times the strike for each contract. Most "I bet bigger than I meant" errors are this one.
:::

::: recap
- An option gives its holder a right and its writer an obligation, for a premium that prices the asymmetry.
- Two types and two sides make four primitive positions. At expiry a call pays \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\) and a put pays \(\max(K - S_T, 0)\), less the premium for the buyer and plus it for the writer.
- Breakeven is \(K + P\) for either call and \(K - P\) for either put, long or short.
- Long positions risk the premium for a large or unbounded gain, while short positions earn the premium for a large or unbounded risk.
- Multiply every quote by the multiplier and the number of contracts before you trade.
:::

::: check
?? You buy one SPX 5,800 put for 40 index points. Where is your breakeven, and what are your maximum loss and maximum gain in dollars?
!! Breakeven is \(5{,}800 - 40 = 5{,}760\). The most you can lose is the premium, \(40 \times \$100 = \$4{,}000\). The most you can gain is \((5{,}800 - 40) \times \$100 = \$576{,}000\), if the index went to zero.

?? You write a 100-strike call for $3 and hold it to expiry, when the underlying is at 108. What is your P&L per contract, and where was your breakeven?
!! The call pays \(108 - 100 = 8\) a share, so your P&L is \(3 - 8 = -5\) a share, or −$500 a contract. Your breakeven was \(100 + 3 = 103\), the same price as the buyer's.

?? You hold one SPX 5,800 put at expiry, and SPX settles at 5,750. What do you receive, and in what form?
!! The put is cash-settled, so you receive the in-the-money amount of \((5{,}800 - 5{,}750) \times \$100 = \$5{,}000\) in cash, with no shares changing hands. The put could not have been exercised earlier, because SPX options are European-style.

?? You sell a 100-strike put for $3 and buy a 90-strike put for $1, both with the same expiry. What is the most you can lose per contract?
!! The net premium is \(3 - 1 = \$2\) a share. Below 90 the long put pays back every dollar that the short put loses, so the loss stops growing at \(100 - 90 = 10\) a share. The maximum loss is \(10 - 2 = \$8\) a share, or $800 a contract, against $9,700 for the short put alone. This pair is a [[put spread]], one of the compositions of Part XII.
:::
