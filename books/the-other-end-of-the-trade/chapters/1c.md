::: where
Chapter 1B described the shape of returns: a strategy that sells options wins most of the time and loses rarely but heavily. This chapter asks the question that shape raises, which is whether the strategy makes money at all. The answer is a single number, the expected value, and this book calls that number edge.
:::

::: tools
- Expected value as a probability-weighted average, and negative skew (Chapter 1B).
- Log returns, and the downward bend of the logarithm (Chapter 1A).
:::

Edge is not a feeling, it is not a story, and it is not "I really think this stock is going up". Edge is a number: the expected value of a trade or a strategy, net of all costs, after appropriate discounting for risk. Taking expected value seriously has one consequence more important than any other, previewed in Chapter 1B. A strategy that wins most of the time can still lose money, and a strategy that wins less than half the time can make money.

Win rate is correlated with edge but is not the same thing. The retail bias toward strategies with a high "probability of profit" is a bias toward shapes that *feel* like edge but may not contain any.

::: plain
Imagine two bets, the first of which pays you a little almost every time and, once in a long while, takes back far more than it ever paid. The second costs you a little almost every time and, once in a long while, pays out a fortune. The first feels like winning and the second feels like losing. Which one actually makes money depends on only one thing, the expected value: each outcome's size multiplied by its probability, added up. How often you win is part of it, but only a part.
:::

## The definition of edge

For a trade with a list of possible outcomes, the expected value is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes, as in Chapter 1B:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i\, x_i \]
{#eq:ev}

Here \(x_i\) is the profit or loss of outcome \(i\) and \(p_i\) its probability. Most of the bets in this chapter have two outcomes. A **binary bet** wins an amount \(G\) with probability \(p\), loses an amount \(L\) with probability \(1 - p\), and costs \(c\) up front. Weighting each outcome by its probability and subtracting the cost gives its expected value:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = pG - (1-p)L - c \]
{#eq:binary}

The trade has **[[edge]]** if and only if \(\mathbb{E}[X] > 0\). If \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\) the trade is fair, and if \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 0\) you are paying for the privilege of playing.

Setting the expected value to zero and solving for \(p\) gives the **[[breakeven win rate]]**, the win rate a bet must beat to have edge:

\[ p^{*} = \frac{L + c}{G + L} \]
{#eq:breakeven}

The breakeven win rate depends only on the sizes of the win, the loss and the cost. A bet whose loss is large relative to its win needs a very high win rate just to break even.

## Two famous coin flips

These two examples are foundational in quantitative trading curricula, and they are worth committing to memory.

::: worked Coin flip 1: the high win rate that loses
**Step 1: write the bet.** You win $1 with probability 0.55 and lose $1.25 with probability 0.45, with no cost to play.

**Step 2: apply [eq:binary].** The expected value per round is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[X] &= 0.55 \times 1 - 0.45 \times 1.25 \\
&= 0.55 - 0.5625 \\
&= -\$0.0125
\end{aligned}
\]

**Step 3: check against the breakeven.** By [eq:breakeven], the bet needs a win rate of \(1.25/2.25 = 55.6\%\), and 55% falls just short of it.

The win rate is 55%, better than even, yet the expected value is negative: you lose about a penny and a quarter per round. Play this game enough times and you go broke for sure. The high win rate masks the asymmetric payoff that makes the bet unprofitable.
:::

::: worked Coin flip 2: the low win rate that wins
**Step 1: write the bet.** This time you win $35 with probability 0.25 and lose $1 with probability 0.75, again with no cost to play.

**Step 2: apply [eq:binary].** The expected value per round is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[X] &= 0.25 \times 35 - 0.75 \times 1 \\
&= 8.75 - 0.75 \\
&= +\$8.00
\end{aligned}
\]

**Step 3: check against the breakeven.** The breakeven win rate is \(1/36 = 2.8\%\), far below the 25% the bet achieves.

The win rate is 25%, three losses in every four bets, yet the expected value is massively positive. This is the shape of a long [[out-of-the-money]] option, of a venture capital portfolio, and of a discretionary trader who takes many small losses chasing rare large wins. It looks awful on a day-to-day basis, and it is an excellent bet.
:::

## Win rate is not edge

Consider two coin flips with the same lopsided odds but opposite roles.

**Scenario A: the insurance buyer.** With probability 95% you pay $1, and with probability 5% you receive $25. Your win rate is 5%, because you "win" only when the rare event hits, and your expected value is \(0.95 \times (-1) + 0.05 \times 25 = -0.95 + 1.25 = +\$0.30\) a round. This is the structural shape of a tail-hedge buyer, who pays small premiums most of the time and collects a large payoff when the rare event arrives. It has positive expected value, a low win rate and positive skew.

**Scenario B: the premium seller.** Now flip Scenario A around, so that with probability 95% you receive $1, and with probability 5% you pay $25. Your win rate is now 95%, and the expected value is \(0.95 \times 1 - 0.05 \times 25 = +0.95 - 1.25 = -\$0.30\) a round. By [eq:breakeven], this bet needs a win rate of \(25/26 = 96.2\%\) to break even.

You win 19 times out of 20 and still lose 30 cents a round on average. **This is the trap.** It shows, as plainly as anything can, why the "feel" of trading ("I won 19 trades in a row") can systematically diverge from the actual profit and loss.

The same shape, 95% small wins and 5% catastrophic losses, describes every undisciplined premium-selling strategy in existence. Sell a deep out-of-the-money put, and in 95% of months the premium expires worthless and you keep it. In the other 5% the underlying blows through your strike and erases a year of accumulated premium. The structural shape is identical to the coin flip, and only the magnitudes change. **Win rate is not edge; expected value is.**

The trap is Scenario B, not premium selling in general. The [[CBOE PUT Index]] of Chapter 1B, which writes at-the-money cash-secured puts on SPX mechanically each month from 1986 forward, has produced *positive* long-run risk-adjusted returns over four decades. Premium selling can have positive or negative expected value, depending on strike selection, sizing, roll discipline, and whether the premium collected actually compensates for the tail risk taken. The point is not that selling premium is bad. The point is that a 95% win rate tells you nothing about which kind of premium selling you are doing.

### Why the trap feels safe

This is also why the brokerage statements of new premium sellers look magnificent for their first six to twelve months. The first batch of options expires worthless, and so does the next. The trader concludes that they have discovered a free money machine.

The 5% catastrophe has not happened yet, because a 5% event happens about once every twenty trials. On a monthly cadence that means roughly twenty months between hits. The chance of getting through \(n\) months with no hit is \(0.95^n\), which after 12 months is still \(0.95^{12} = 54\%\). More often than not, a new seller's whole first year is clean. The trader is sampling from a distribution whose left tail they have not yet seen, and concluding that the left tail does not exist.

A slider moves a vertical "today" line across the months, and a readout gives the share of traders who are still ahead. Early on, most lines are rising and most traders are ahead; the share falls as the months pass and the rare losses arrive, until the lines cluster around the downward-sloping expected value.]

### Ask what the losers lose

Anyone who tells you that their options-selling strategy has "an 85% win rate" or "a 92% probability of profit" is telling you about the *shape*, not the *edge*. Always ask what the average losing trade loses, in dollars, relative to what the average winning trade wins. Set that ratio against the win rate and you know the sign of the expected value.

The breakeven formula makes this a one-line check: at a win rate \(p\), a bet with no costs breaks even when the average loss is \(p/(1-p)\) times the average win. An 85% win rate is profitable only if the average loss is less than \(0.85/0.15 = 5.7\) times the average win. A 92% win rate is profitable only if it is less than \(0.92/0.08 = 11.5\) times.

## A more realistic version: selling a put

Real trades have more than two outcomes, and the losses vary in size. Here is a simplified short put, with the average loss standing in for the whole loss side of the distribution.

::: worked Selling a put that expires worthless 90% of the time
**Step 1: set up the trade.** Sell a put for $0.50 of premium, with a strike far enough below the price that it expires worthless about 90% of the time. In the language of Chapter 5C that is roughly a 10-delta put. The maximum possible loss is $4.50, which happens if the stock goes to zero (a simplification).

**Step 2: the wins.** Averaged over all trades, the wins contribute \(0.90 \times \$0.50 = +\$0.45\) a trade.

**Step 3: the losses.** When the put loses, it rarely loses the maximum. Suppose the average loss, given a loss, is about $2. The losses then cost \(0.10 \times \$2 = \$0.20\) a trade on average.

**Step 4: the expected value.** The mean is \(+\$0.45 - \$0.20 = +\$0.25\) a trade, so this one *does* have positive expected value, and its breakeven win rate is \(2/2.50 = 80\%\), comfortably below the 90% it achieves.
:::

Now change only the loss side, and suppose the realized average loss is $3 instead of $2, because losses cluster in stress, when [[implied volatility]] expands and puts get dearer. The mean falls to \(+\$0.45 - \$0.30 = +\$0.15\). Suppose next that you cannot hold to expiry because of [[margin]]. A broker's margin call can force you to close at the worst [[mark-to-market]] loss along the way. The realized loss can then be $4 to $4.50 even when the loss at expiry would have been $2, and the mean falls to \(+\$0.45 - \$0.40 = +\$0.05\), and to zero at $4.50.

| average loss, given a loss | expected value per trade | breakeven win rate |
|---|---|---|
| $2.00 | +$0.25 | 80.0% |
| $3.00 | +$0.15 | 85.7% |
| $4.00 | +$0.05 | 88.9% |
| $4.50 | $0.00 | 90.0% |

The win rate is 90% in every row, yet the realized edge depends heavily on the loss distribution and on the path the trade takes, not just on the win rate. Chapter 1E develops the path, and the way margin turns a paper loss into a realized one.

## Edge comes before everything

Risk management, position sizing and discipline, the subjects of Part XIII, keep you in the game, but they cannot manufacture edge. **If your underlying expected value is negative, perfect discipline just means losing money more methodically.** It is the single most important sentence in this book, and later chapters repeat it in different forms.

The machinery of later chapters, from dealer mechanics and regime classification to conviction scoring and kill rules, assumes that you have a process with positive expected value. It exists to keep an edge from being destroyed by behavior, fees, taxes and bad sizing, and it does not create edge where none exists.

### The two claims inside a short put

When you sell a deep out-of-the-money index put, you have placed a structural bet. You are betting that the equity skew and the [[variance risk premium]] together compensate you enough for the rare tail outcome. The equity skew is the richer pricing of index puts than of calls, which Chapter 5G describes. You are also betting that you can size and roll the position so that the tail outcome does not end the game. Notice which of these assumptions is doing the work.

- **The premium has to be enough.** This is an empirical claim about the market, sometimes true and sometimes not, and Chapter 10C examines the evidence for it.
- **Your sizing has to survive the tail.** This is a discipline claim about you, and it is almost always the binding constraint.

The slide from a profitable premium seller into Scenario B is rarely about a sudden change in market behavior. It is about a sudden change in *the trader's* behavior, such as:

- a doubled position size after a winning streak;
- a strike moved one band closer to at-the-money "because vol is high right now";
- a stop level that was never pre-committed in writing.

Part XIII (Discipline) returns to this in detail, because it is the central practical issue, while Part V (Options) is the technical preamble.

## Expected utility: why dollars are not enough

The plain expected value calculation assumes that you value money linearly: that gaining $1,000 and losing $1,000 have equal and opposite impact. This is approximately true for bets that are small relative to your wealth and approximately false for large ones.

Real people have *concave* utility, which bends downward the way the logarithm does in Chapter 1A. A $1,000 gain when you have $50,000 in the bank matters more than a $1,000 gain when you have $5 million. Equivalently, a $1,000 loss hurts more than a $1,000 gain feels good. That is just the concavity of utility expressed in symmetric form.

The mathematical formalization is **[[expected utility]]** theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944), which says that a rational decision-maker maximizes the probability-weighted average of the value placed on each outcome:

\[ \mathbb{E}[U(W)] = \sum_i p_i\, U(W_i) \]
{#eq:eu}

where \(W_i\) is your wealth in outcome \(i\) and \(U\) is some concave **utility function**, the value you place on each level of wealth. The most-used utility function in finance is the logarithm:

\[ U(W) = \ln W \]
{#eq:logu}

With log utility the $1,000 example becomes arithmetic: on $50,000, a gain is worth \(\ln(51{,}000/50{,}000) = +0.0198\) and a loss costs \(\ln(49{,}000/50{,}000) = -0.0202\), slightly more. On $5 million, the same gain is worth only \(\ln(5{,}001{,}000/5{,}000{,}000) = +0.0002\).

[FIGURE IDEA: The curve U(W) = ln W for wealth from $10,000 to $200,000, with a slider for current wealth and a second for the size of a symmetric gain or loss. The figure marks the utility gained and lost as two vertical bars and reports their ratio. It would show the loss bar outgrowing the gain bar as the stake grows relative to wealth, and both shrinking toward equality as wealth grows: concavity made visible.]

Log utility has a special property: the bet size that maximizes expected log utility is the bet size of the **[[Kelly criterion]]**, which Chapter 3D develops. Log utility is also the natural utility function for an investor who compounds, because maximizing expected log return is equivalent to maximizing the expected compound growth rate. For an investor with a multi-decade horizon and no need to spend the money soon, log utility is approximately the right utility function.

::: grind Why the expected log return is the growth rate
**Step 1: compound many rounds.** Take \(n\) independent rounds of the same bet, with simple returns \(R_1, \ldots, R_n\) on the account, so that wealth after \(n\) rounds is \(W_n = W_0 (1+R_1)(1+R_2)\cdots(1+R_n)\).

**Step 2: take logarithms.** The product becomes a sum, as in Chapter 1A:

\[ \ln\frac{W_n}{W_0} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \ln(1 + R_t) \]

**Step 3: average.** Divide both sides by \(n\). By the law of large numbers, the average of many independent draws approaches their expected value:

\[ \frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{W_n}{W_0} \to \mathbb{E}[\ln(1+R)] \]

**Step 4: read the result.** For large \(n\), wealth is about \(W_n \approx W_0\, e^{\,n\,\mathbb{E}[\ln(1+R)]}\), so the account grows at a rate of \(\mathbb{E}[\ln(1+R)]\) per round. Maximizing that expectation maximizes the long-run growth rate, and if it is negative the account shrinks toward zero, whatever the dollar expected value.
:::

### Compute expected log return, not expected dollars

The practical implication is direct: when you compute expected value for sizing purposes, compute the expected *log* return on the portfolio, not the expected dollar return. Log utility automatically penalizes catastrophic outcomes, and dollar expected value does not.

::: worked Two trades with the same $10,000 expected value
**Step 1: set up trade 1.** On a $100,000 portfolio, a trade has an expected value of +$10,000 but a 5% chance of losing $80,000. For the mean to be +$10,000, the other 95% must gain \((10{,}000 + 0.05 \times 80{,}000)/0.95 = \$14{,}737\).

**Step 2: its expected log return.** Weight the log of each outcome's wealth ratio by its probability:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&0.95 \ln(1.14737) \\
&\quad + 0.05 \ln(0.20) \\
&= 0.1306 - 0.0805 \\
&= +0.0501
\end{aligned}
\]

That is positive, but it is worth the same as a sure gain of \(100{,}000 \times (e^{0.0501} - 1) = \$5{,}140\), about half the dollar expectation, because the 5% branch costs that much.

**Step 3: set up trade 2.** Keep the +$10,000 expected value but deepen the bad branch to a loss of $95,000. The good branch must now gain \((10{,}000 + 0.05 \times 95{,}000)/0.95 = \$15{,}526\).

**Step 4: its expected log return.** The same calculation now gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
&0.95 \ln(1.15526) \\
&\quad + 0.05 \ln(0.05) \\
&= 0.1371 - 0.1498 \\
&= -0.0127
\end{aligned}
\]

The expected log return is now *negative*, although the dollar expected value has not changed. Losing 95% of the portfolio crushes the logarithm of the remaining wealth far more than the gains help it. Repeated at this size, the trade shrinks the account over time.
:::

The curve rises, peaks at the Kelly fraction, then falls through zero, while a straight line shows dollar expected value rising without limit. ## Where this connects to options

Every options strategy can, and must, be evaluated by its expected value, decomposed by component. A short at-the-money put has an expected value of the gross premium, minus the expected payout at exercise, minus transaction costs, minus the expected cost of financing its margin. A long [[calendar spread]] has an expected gain from the re-pricing of forward volatility, minus the cost of setting it up, minus the expected loss from the underlying moving against the position. Every line in a brokerage statement is a sample drawn from a distribution, and the question is always the same: is the mean of that distribution positive after costs?

This is also why most options education content fails. It teaches you the *shape* of a strategy, its **[[payoff diagram]]**, without teaching you how to evaluate whether the strategy has positive expected value in the current market. On its own, a payoff diagram tells you nothing about edge.

Take a short [[iron condor]] and its mirror image, the long iron condor. Both have limited risk and limited reward, in opposing geometries: one wins small and often, the other wins large and rarely. Whether the short or the long one has positive expected value depends on whether implied volatility is over-pricing or under-pricing the volatility that actually occurs. Edge is what determines the outcome, while geometry tells you about the path, not about the edge.

::: practice
- **Net out every cost before you call it edge.** Commissions, the bid-ask spread (Chapter 2D), taxes (Chapter 2E) and margin financing all come off the expected value. A put with a gross edge of $0.25 a trade and round-trip costs of $0.10 keeps $0.15; the same put with costs of $0.30 has none.
- **Compute the breakeven win rate for every strategy you consider.** Put its average win and loss into [eq:breakeven] and compare the result with the win rate it actually achieves. A realized win rate only a point or two above breakeven is not a margin of safety.
- **Size by expected log return.** Before adding a position, check the expected log return of the whole portfolio with the position in it, not the dollar expected value of the position alone.
- **Expect your first year of premium selling to flatter you.** At a 5% monthly chance of a large loss, a clean first year is more likely than not, so a clean first year is weak evidence of edge.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Expected value is only as good as the probabilities and sizes that go into it, and those are estimated from samples. For a negatively skewed bet, most short samples overstate the mean. Scenario B over 12 months shows a perfect record with probability 54%, and in those samples its average is +$1 a round against a true −$0.30.

Expected value also says nothing about the path or about size. A bet with positive expected value can still ruin an account if it is sized so large that one loss is fatal. The expected log return catches that danger, and the dollar expectation misses it.
:::

::: rule
- **Act only on positive expected value after all costs.** If you cannot estimate the expected value, you do not have edge, and discipline cannot supply it.
- **Demand a margin above breakeven.** Require the win rate you can defend to beat the breakeven win rate by more than your uncertainty about it.
- **Size so that the portfolio's expected log return stays positive with room to spare.** If adding a position would push it toward zero, the position is too large, however good its dollar expected value.
- **Never accept a win rate or a probability of profit on its own.** Ask for the average win and the average loss, then run the breakeven check.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading "probability of profit" as edge
Brokerage platforms and sales pitches quote the probability that a trade ends profitable. For a short option that number is high by construction, because it describes the shape, not the edge. An 85% probability of profit says nothing about edge until you know the average win and the average loss: it breaks even when the average loss is 5.7 times the average win.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: choosing a strategy from its payoff diagram
Two strategies with the same geometry, or mirror-image geometries, can have opposite expected values. The diagram shows what happens at each final price. It does not show how likely each price is, or what the market charged you for the position. Only the expected value, computed from both, shows edge.
:::

::: familiar
You know the gap between a surrogate endpoint and the one that matters. A regimen can shrink tumors in most patients and still fail to extend overall survival, if the responses are brief or the harm to the other patients is severe. The response rate counts how often something good happens; overall survival weighs every outcome by how large it is. Win rate is the response rate of a trading strategy, and expected value is its overall survival.
:::

::: recap
- Edge is the expected value of a trade, net of all costs: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = pG - (1-p)L - c\) for a binary bet, and positive expected value is the only definition of edge.
- A bet has edge only if its win rate beats the breakeven win rate \((L + c)/(G + L)\). A high win rate with a large average loss can easily fall short of it.
- Scenario B, winning $1 95% of the time and losing $25 otherwise, loses 30 cents a round. Premium selling can be profitable, but a high win rate never shows that it is.
- Discipline keeps an edge alive but cannot create one.
- For sizing, maximize the expected log return of the portfolio, which is its long-run growth rate. It penalizes catastrophic outcomes that dollar expected value ignores.
:::

::: check
?? A bet wins $2 with probability 0.7, loses $5 otherwise, and costs $0.10 to place. What is its expected value, and what win rate would it need to break even?
!! By [eq:binary], the expected value is \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.7 \times 2 - 0.3 \times 5 - 0.10 = 1.40 - 1.50 - 0.10 = -\$0.20\). By [eq:breakeven], the breakeven win rate is \((5 + 0.10)/(2 + 5) = 72.9\%\), so the bet's 70% falls short.

?? A newsletter advertises an options strategy with "a 92% probability of profit". What must you know before you can say whether it has edge, and what is the threshold?
!! You need the average win and the average loss. At a 92% win rate, the strategy breaks even when the average loss is \(0.92/0.08 = 11.5\) times the average win. It has edge only if its average loss is smaller than that, after costs.

?? Scenario A, the insurance buyer, wins only 5% of the time. Why is it the better bet of the two scenarios?
!! Its expected value is \(-0.95 + 1.25 = +\$0.30\) a round, because its rare win of $25 more than pays for its frequent $1 losses. The low win rate is the shape of the bet, not its edge.

?? On a $100,000 account, a bet gains $30,000 or loses $20,000 with equal probability. Should a log-utility investor take it, and should they if the stakes double to +$60,000 or −$40,000?
!! At the first size, the dollar expected value is +$5,000 and the expected log return is \(0.5 \ln 1.3 + 0.5 \ln 0.8 = 0.1312 - 0.1116 = +0.0196\), so the investor takes it. At double size, the dollar expected value doubles to +$10,000, but the expected log return is \(0.5 \ln 1.6 + 0.5 \ln 0.6 = 0.2350 - 0.2554 = -0.0204\). The investor declines: repeated at that size, the bet would shrink the account.

?? A new premium seller runs a strategy with a 5% monthly chance of a large loss and has had 12 clean months. How much should that record raise their confidence in the strategy's edge?
!! Very little. A strategy with that risk goes 12 months without a loss with probability \(0.95^{12} = 54\%\), whatever its expected value. A clean year is what both a good and a bad version of the strategy usually produce.
:::
