## Why the sign is an assumption

Gamma is identical, and positive, for a call and a put at the same strike and expiry (Chapter 5C). So an option's type, strike and expiry say nothing about which way its line counts in GEX. Only the side the dealer holds does, and that side is not in the public data. A working paper by Chilingarian (2026) puts it in one sentence:

> Because Black-Scholes gamma is identical and positive for calls and puts at the same strike, the sign in any GEX aggregate is an inventory assumption, and when left implicit, a mislabeled term can silently invert the entire regime.
> Chilingarian (2026)

Open interest is the public count, and it is built to be sideless. The OCC computes it once a day, overnight, from end-of-day records that its clearing members submit and that it then reconciles. It shows the count on its own site the next morning and distributes it to the industry the next business day. Open interest rises by one when a trade opens a new contract and falls by one when a trade closes one. It does not change when a contract passes from one holder to another. Nothing in it says whether a dealer, a customer or neither holds either side.

So every GEX model fills in the sign \(s_i\) of [eq:8a/line] with a **[[sign convention]]**. That is a rule, or a model, that decides for each line whether dealers are long or short. The rest of this chapter is about how good those rules are.

## The vendor formula and its two conventions

The public formula for GEX came from a vendor. SqueezeMetrics first published its "Gamma Exposure (GEX)" white paper in March 2016 and revised it in December 2017. Its per-line formula is \(\Gamma \cdot \text{OI} \cdot 100\) for calls and \(\Gamma \cdot \text{OI} \cdot (-100)\) for puts, in shares per $1 of index move. Multiplying by \(S^2 \times 0.01\) gives the per-1% form most vendors quote. SpotGamma calls the 0.01 scaling "the industry convention":

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GEX} &= \textstyle\sum_i \Gamma_i\,\text{OI}_i \times 100 \\ &\quad \times S^2 \times 0.01 \times \text{sign}_i \end{aligned} \]
{#eq:vendor}

Here \(\text{sign}_i = +1\) for calls and −1 for puts. It is [eq:8a/gex] written out, with \(\text{OI}_i = N_i\), \(M = 100\), and one sign convention built in. Each term is the dollar gamma per 1% move of the open interest on one line. The sign is the whole game, because it encodes the assumption about which side of each option type the dealer holds. The white paper's own claim was that high GEX goes with low volatility the next day. Chapter 8G sets that claim against later tests, including a vendor's test of its own product that finds most of the signal already in the VIX and at-the-money implied volatility.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab says that every later vendor adopted this formula in some form, and names SpotGamma, Tier1Alpha and Cboe's own derivative dashboards. Of the three, only SpotGamma documents a formula of this form, and it applies its own sign rules. Tier1 Alpha's method is unpublished. Cboe's published research does not assume a sign at all: it measures positions from its own trade records, as the sections below describe.

Two conventions are in wide use, and both are assumptions about who holds what, not observations:

| Convention | \(s_i\) for calls | \(s_i\) for puts | Assumed positioning |
|---|---|---|---|
| JPM-era (pre-2022) | +1 | −1 | Dealers long calls, bought from asset managers who sell them; short puts, sold to hedgers who buy them |
| 0DTE-era (post-2022) | −1 | +1 | Dealers short calls, sold to customers who buy them; long puts, bought from retail premium sellers who write them |

The 0DTE-era row needs customers to be net sellers of puts, as in [[cash-secured puts|cash-secured put]] and [[put credit spreads|put credit spread]]. At the same time it needs them to be net buyers of calls. A customer who sells a call, in a [[call credit spread]] or an [[iron condor]], leaves the dealer long that call, which pushes the call sign back toward +1. Which of those flows dominates is exactly what the convention assumes.

This is not a minor calibration issue but the dominant source of model risk in every public GEX product. Model risk is the risk of being wrong because a model's assumption is wrong. Receive a claim that "GEX flipped negative today" with skepticism. Ask first which convention the publisher uses, and whether it matches the actual flow in the product you trade.

## The JPM era: where the first sign came from

SqueezeMetrics built its method on the assumption that **dealers are net long calls and net short puts**. The reasoning came from the dominant flow at the time. Institutional asset managers were the main sellers of calls, through [[covered calls|covered call]] and the famous "[[JPM collar]]" rolled by JPMorgan's Hedged Equity Fund. Institutional hedgers such as pension funds and endowments were the main buyers of puts. Dealers stood on the other side of both: long the calls they bought from yield-hunting asset managers, and short the puts they sold to hedgers. The white paper gives the reasoning in its own words. Calls are "sold by investors and bought by market makers (reflecting covered call strategies)". Puts "are mostly purchased by investors for downside protection and sold by market makers."

Under that assumption, a rise in SPX makes the dealers' long calls gain delta, so dealers sell stock to stay flat, which damps the rally. A fall makes the puts' deltas more negative. That leaves the dealers who are short them longer in delta, so dealers sell stock again, which amplifies the fall. The result is the familiar pattern of slow grinds up and fast crashes down. The Dealer Mechanics Lab attributes the asymmetric character of equity volatility cycles directly to this asymmetric dealer positioning.

### What position data say about that era

The put half of the assumption has the strongest support of anything in this chapter, because several studies could see who held the puts:

- **Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009)** used CBOE data that identify the aggregate long and short open interest of public customers and of firms' own traders, from before weekly options existed. End users were net long index options, most of all out-of-the-money puts, and net short single-stock options. So dealers were net short index puts.
- **Chen, Joslin and Ni (2019)** measured public investors' net buying of deep out-of-the-money SPX puts, January 1991 to December 2012. On average the public bought this crash insurance from intermediaries, so dealers were net short it. When the public's net buying fell, which the authors read as intermediaries' constraints tightening, options grew more expensive. Risk premia rose across many assets, funding liquidity deteriorated and broker-dealers deleveraged.
- **Constantinides and Lian (2021)** model and test the same market: [[market makers|market maker]] are net sellers and customers net buyers of index puts. As disaster risk rises, customers want more puts while market makers grow more credit-constrained in writing them, so the net amount bought falls just as prices rise.

The last two carry a warning for every convention. The dealers' short put position is not fixed. It is a quantity they choose, and it shrinks exactly when their constraints bind, which is when stress arrives.

The call half has weaker support. Covered-call writing and collars do leave dealers long calls, when dealers take the other side. By Cboe's count, assets in derivative-income funds, which sell options, grew about sixfold from 2019, from about $20 billion to over $120 billion. Yet Cboe's own note on those funds found that buying of upside calls for leverage, not call selling, dominated the flows it examined. The position study in the next section finds dealers' net call position near zero.

### The one position whose holder is known

One large index-option position is public by construction. The JPM collar of JPMorgan's Hedged Equity Fund is reset every quarter, with about 35,000 SPX contracts per leg by vendors' counts. The fund holds stocks and, as vendor commentary describes it, "buys a put spread below the market for downside protection and sells calls above the market to finance those puts". The fund is long the upper put of the spread, so whoever sold that put, typically dealers, is short it. At that one strike the short side is short gamma by construction, not by assumption. Its hedging joins a move through the strike instead of opposing it. Chapter 8C returns to what that strike looks like on a map.

## The 0DTE era: a story and the data

SqueezeMetrics' assumption dates from 2016 and 2017. After Cboe added Tuesday and Thursday expirations to SPX weekly options in 2022, every weekday had an expiry, and same-day options took over the volume. Cboe's figures put [[0DTE]] options at 5% of SPX volume in 2016, over 40% after the new expirations, about 50% in August 2023, and a record 62% in August 2025. Cboe also estimates that retail makes up about 50% to 60% of SPX 0DTE trading.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab's account of this era runs as follows. Retail and small institutional accounts sell premium, through credit spreads, iron condors and cash-secured puts, with dealers buying from them. So the new net positioning is plausibly the opposite of the JPM-era assumption: dealers net long puts, bought from customers who write them, and net short calls, sold to customers who buy them. That is the 0DTE-era convention.

The flow data do not support its premise that customers overwhelmingly sell premium:

- **Cboe's own data** describe customer 0DTE activity as "very balanced" in 2023, spread across hedging, yield harvesting, tactical leverage and systematic trading. The 2025 update calls it "extremely balanced", and adds that "over 95% of all 0DTE trades are done in a limited risk format (either long options outright or short via spreads)".
- **A tabulation of uncertain attribution**, for January 2020 to June 2023, puts customers' 0DTE purchases from market makers at 14.95% of all SPX and [[SPXW]] volume, and their sales to market makers at 14.06%.
- **Beckmeyer, Branger and Gayda (2023)**, an SSRN working paper, identified retail SPX trades through Cboe's price-improvement auctions, February 2021 to September 2023. More than 75% of those retail trades were in 0DTE contracts. That retail slice *bought* more 0DTE options than it sold, leaving market makers net short. The paper also reports the market makers' aggregate net short 0DTE position growing. It is a narrow slice, about 6% of 0DTE trading by the paper's identification, against Cboe's estimate of 50% to 60% retail.

Studies that measure market makers' positions rather than assume them find something more specific:

- **Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2023)**, "0DTEs: Trading, Gamma Risk and Volatility Propagation", is an SSRN working paper (SSRN 4692190), first posted in November 2023 and circulated in 2024. It rebuilt market makers' inventory from Cboe's records of end users' buying and selling. They found that "Market Makers' inventory, as measured by the net gamma of their positions, is on average positive and negatively related to future intraday volatility". The paper infers positions from flow; it does not attribute them to retail premium selling, which is the Lab's gloss.
- **Adams, Fontaine and Ornthanalai (2024)**, an SSRN working paper, found that "Market makers match most 0DTE order flows, but absorb end-users' trades in long-dated options that eventually become 0DTE positions."
- **Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025)**, "Do S&P500 Options Increase Market Volatility? Evidence from 0DTEs" (SSRN 5641974), answer the question in their title with "On the contrary", and continue:

> ... the presence of 0DTEs dampens market volatility through a shift in market makers' hedging needs. Importantly, this shift occurs not due to trading 0DTEs on their expiration day but due to positions in longer-dated options accumulated earlier and becoming 0DTEs.
> Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025)

- **Amaya, Garcia-Ares, Pearson and Vasquez (2025)**, a working paper, used proprietary records of every SPX and SPXW trade from January 2020 to June 2023. They found that the gamma of the aggregate market-maker position, "while typically positive, is often negative".
- **Cboe's own measurement** (Xu, 2023) put market makers' net 0DTE gamma at about $170 million to $670 million through the day. At 3:30 PM its median was +$173 million, with a typical range reaching down to −$1.1 billion.

Put together, the measured sign of dealer gamma in SPX same-day options is positive on average, and not always. It comes mostly from positions in longer-dated options that age into expiry, not from same-day premium selling, which market makers largely pass through. The 0DTE-era convention can land on the right average sign for a put-heavy book, but for a reason the data contradict. Chapter 8G gives the effect sizes these studies report.

## What position data show

The most direct evidence on the sign for all SPX options, 2011 to 2023, is a working paper by Terstegge (2025). It builds intermediaries' aggregate positions from Cboe's daily open and close volume files:

- **Calls:** intermediaries' buy and sell volumes are roughly balanced, and their net call position hovers near zero.
- **Puts:** sell volumes consistently exceed buy volumes, leaving a persistent net short put position of about 19 million contracts, concentrated in deep out-of-the-money puts.
- **Risk:** without hedge adjustments, a 10% fall in the S&P 500 would cost roughly 200% of the dealers' option inventory value. Even a dealer perfectly hedged at the close cannot re-hedge a large overnight or weekend move, because equity liquidity is thin then.

By contracts, then, dealers look like the put half of the JPM-era convention with flat calls, and nothing like the 0DTE-era convention's "dealers long puts". Two cautions apply. Cboe's files miss hedges done off the exchange or in futures options. And contracts are not gamma: a deep out-of-the-money put carries little gamma until a crash brings spot toward it.

The second caution is how "dealers short puts" and "dealers long gamma on average" can both be true. The worked example shows one way, with a made-up book.

::: worked Contracts against gamma
**Step 1: the book.** SPX is at 6,000. Dealers are long 100,000 contracts at the 6,000 strike with 7 days to expiry, options their customers sold them, at 15% implied volatility. They are short 1,000,000 puts at 4,800, 20% below spot, with 90 days to expiry. Those carry 28% implied volatility, the higher level a skewed smile gives far out-of-the-money puts (Chapter 5E). By count, dealers are net short 900,000 contracts.

**Step 2: dollar gamma per contract.** By [eq:8a/line], with [[Black-Scholes|Black-Scholes model]] gamma at a 4% rate, one near-the-money contract carries about $115,100 per 1% move and one far put about $3,750. The near contract carries about 31 times as much.

**Step 3: GEX at 6,000.** The long block gives \(100{,}000 \times \$115{,}100 \approx +\$11.51\) billion and the short puts \(1{,}000{,}000 \times \$3{,}750 \approx -\$3.75\) billion. The total is about +$7.76 billion per 1%: dealers are long gamma and their hedging damps moves.

**Step 4: after a fall.** Hold the volatilities fixed and move spot. At 5,800 the long block gives +$3.17 billion and the short puts −$5.39 billion, a total of −$2.22 billion. At 5,400, 10% down, the 7-day options carry almost no gamma and the puts give −$9.51 billion. The book turned from damping to amplifying on the way down, between 5,850 and 5,800.
:::

That pattern, damping on normal days and amplifying in a sell-off, is what Chapters 8D and 8H describe. It also means that a single sign per option type is wrong for SPX. The JPM-era convention gets the puts' direction right by count but misses the long gamma near the money. The 0DTE-era convention can match the average gamma sign but signs the puts the wrong way, and it would miss the turn in Step 4.

### Single stocks follow different rules

Positions in single-stock options look different again. Lakonishok and co-authors (2004, published in 2007) used CBOE open interest split by investor type, 1990 through 2001. Investors other than market makers held about four times more long call than long put open interest, and more short than long open interest in both calls and puts. Market makers were therefore net long both. Christoffersen and co-authors (2018) find illiquidity premia in equity options consistent with evidence that market makers there "hold large and risky net long positions", which the premia compensate. Kanne, Korn and Uhrig-Homburg (2023) find market makers net long options on some underlyings and net short on others.

Vendors disagree with those data and with each other. SpotGamma states that its "working assumption" for equities "is that dealers are short options for both puts and calls". It also models market makers "to be short puts and long calls on index products, due to the prevalence of option strategies like collars and covered calls". Barchart's free pages sign calls positive and puts negative for every ticker. Episodes of heavy retail call buying can leave dealers short gamma in a single name, the setting of Chapter 8I. Lipson, Tomio and Zhang (2023), a working paper, find that retail option trading after commissions fell to zero raised the volatility of the underlying stocks. The channel was dealers hedging higher-gamma contracts in the direction of price moves.

So no single convention fits every underlying. The Dealer Mechanics Lab says that for SPY and individual stocks the appropriate convention may be regime-dependent, and the single-stock evidence supports that.

## Demand-based option pricing

A foundational paper sits under all of this: Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009), "Demand-Based Option Pricing", *Review of Financial Studies*, Vol. 22, No. 10, pages 4259 to 4299. In their model, dealers who cannot hedge perfectly charge for the risk left in the positions they absorb. **[[Demand-based option pricing]]** follows: demand pressure raises an option's price in proportion to the variance of its unhedgeable part, and raises other options' prices in proportion to covariances. The Dealer Mechanics Lab summarizes the paper as showing that net end-user demand for index options is the primary driver of the deviation of option prices from no-arbitrage benchmarks. The deeper the dealers' positioning imbalance, the larger the deviation, and the larger the hedging activity needed to manage it.

The Garleanu-Pedersen-Poteshman framework establishes that option prices are not pure forecasts of future variance plus a risk-free rate. They also depend on *who is buying and who is selling*, beyond the model fundamentals. The Dealer Mechanics Lab takes this as the structural justification for the [[variance risk premium]] (Chapter 10C) and as the academic foundation for the GEX framework as a whole.

The Edge Book reads the same paper more narrowly (Chapter 10I). In its reading, demand pressure affects option prices at the level of option pricing, not the price of the underlying, and its effect per unit of demand is small. Nor does the paper validate specific level-trading rules. Both readings are defensible, because the paper tests prices, not what dealer gamma does to the index.

Later work fills in the mechanism. Bollen and Whaley (2004) found that net buying pressure for index puts moves the implied volatility of S&P 500 options most strongly. For stock options, call demand dominates. This is the causation Chapter 8A mentioned: heavy demand for index puts steepens the skew. Muravyev (2016) split the price impact of option trades into an inventory-risk part and an information part, and found the inventory part larger. Fournier and Jacobs (2020) model an index-option market maker who demands a larger variance risk premium after it incurs losses. The inventory GEX tries to measure is the same inventory that sets option prices, and dealers are paid to carry it.

## How positions are estimated

Between two of OCC's daily counts, every intraday GEX number is imputed from the trade tape rather than observed. Vendors do this with varying sophistication, and the imputation errors can be substantial intraday, especially in high-volume sessions. Four sources feed the estimates:

| Source | What it records | Coverage and timing |
|---|---|---|
| OCC open interest | Contracts outstanding per series, with no sides | All U.S. listed options; once a day, the next morning |
| Cboe [[Open-Close data]] | Volume by origin (customer, professional customer, broker-dealer, market maker), by buy or sell, and by open or close | Cboe's exchanges: all SPX, but only Cboe's share of multi-listed products; end of day, or intraday in 10-minute snapshots delivered within five minutes |
| ISE Trade Outline | Opening and closing volume by origin, with market makers added as an origin in a later update | ISE and GEMX, which do not list SPX |
| Signed volume (for example OptionMetrics) | Buyer- or seller-initiated volume, inferred with the Lee-Ready algorithm | Over 10,000 underlyings from 2016; nightly, with 5- and 30-minute snapshots |

Cboe's end-of-day Open-Close subscription is listed at $500 a month, the 10-minute intraday product at $1,000 and a one-minute product at $5,000, with history from January 2018. So the side of an SPX trade is observable within minutes for a few thousand dollars a month. The side of an SPY trade is split across exchanges and, for retail orders that wholesalers internalize, partly off the exchanges altogether (Chapter 2B). That is why the one vendor method that claims to make no assumptions covers SPX only, and why most retail maps assume a sign.

### Volume is not position

Trading volume says little about who is left holding what. Cboe's 2023 note gives an example from August 15 of that year. Over 100,000 contracts of a 4440-strike put traded in the day. Yet market makers "were left short only about 3,000 contracts by the end of the day, or just 3% of the gross volume". Its 2025 update states the principle: "what matters for determining the potential impact of market maker gamma hedging activity is the balance of the volume between buys vs. sells, not the notional size".

### Trade classification and its errors

Where the data do not record sides, vendors infer them. Three rules are common. The quote rule calls a trade above the [[mid-quote]] a buy and one below it a sell, and cannot classify one at the midpoint. The tick rule compares the price with the previous trade's. The Lee-Ready algorithm combines the two. This is **[[trade classification]]**, and its accuracy has been measured. Savickas and Wilson (2003) tested the rules against a proprietary CBOE data set that records the true direction of each option trade. The quote rule, Lee-Ready, the Ellis-Michaely-O'Hara rule and the tick rule signed correctly 83%, 80%, 77% and 59% of the trades each could classify. That is an error rate of 17% to 41%.

The errors are not spread evenly. Trades outside the quotes and trades against reversed quotes were highly misclassified by all four rules. Components of index-option complex trades, executed away from the retail automatic system, were misclassified almost 50% of the time by any method. Dropping them, 15% of the sample, raised the quote rule's success to over 87%. A second problem is who the "customer" is. A study led by Muravyev (2020) found that most option trades originate from investors who time their executions, such as proprietary traders and institutions with execution algorithms. So the liquidity taker is often not a customer facing a hedging dealer. And the midpoint can be a poor guide to value at the moment of a trade.

::: familiar
This is non-differential misclassification, and it behaves as it does in a trial. If a binary outcome is recorded with sensitivity \(Se\) and specificity \(Sp\), the same in both arms, the observed risk difference is the true one times \(Se + Sp - 1\). With both equal to \(1 - e\), that factor is \(1 - 2e\), the figure's shrinkage. The bias runs toward the null on average. But any single study, like any single draw, can land far from it, and a small true effect can change sign.
:::

Two findings bound how well any of this can work. Ardia and Vaudescal (2026), an SSRN working paper, built net gamma from public open interest over five years of half-hour SPX data. It tracked a dealer-inventory benchmark at a correlation of 0.99 for options other than 0DTE, but only 0.53 for 0DTE. The benchmark shares its open-interest starting point with the proxy, so 0.99 is an upper bound on agreement. And market makers match most 0DTE flow, as Adams, Fontaine and Ornthanalai found, so their same-day gamma comes mostly from older positions that sit in yesterday's open interest. For longer expiries, yesterday's open interest is most of the story. For same-day options, the flow intraday maps chase is mostly offset, and the part that is not is the part public data measure worst.

A few statements in that material deserve their labels. The "no assumptions" of the Unusual Whales method holds only as far as Cboe's own flags are complete: a dealer trading as a broker-dealer, or hedging in SPY or futures options, falls outside them. Volland claims "over 90% accuracy over all expirations, with 99% accuracy in 0DTE options" for its classification, without a disclosed benchmark. Its creator, a competitor of the others, says the open-interest convention is "right maybe ±75% of the time", a figure with no support offered. Skylit says only that its positioning is "derived by Skylit from that data, not reported by the exchanges". Its calculations run on "custom inference models and proprietary intelligence built from years of research into dealer microstructure" (Chapter 8C). A working paper by Sen (2026) sums up the market: commercial services "cost roughly US$99 to US$1,999 per month, cover mainly U.S. equity indices, and disclose little of their methodology".

SpotGamma, to its credit, published a sensitivity test of its own model. On data from February 7, 2020, it assumed the model "is only 50% correct and the remaining 50% open interest is randomized to be long or short", and repeated the run at higher shares. The total moved with the share it got right, which is the sign map's lesson in a vendor's hands.

## What no public data show

Every public GEX model sees mainly the standard listed chains: contracts that trade on Cboe and the other exchanges, cleared through the OCC, with strikes on a fixed grid. That dataset is comprehensive, but it captures only one side of the market, the listed side. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls the rest the **invisible book**, and of all the limits it names this as the one that bites hardest in practice. "Absent from the chains most vendors use" is the more accurate phrase, because some of it does reach OCC.

**FLEX options** sit alongside the listed market, trading on Cboe but with a customizable strike, expiration, exercise style and settlement (Chapters 2B and 4A). OCC clears them and publishes FLEX open interest reports, but they sit outside the standard chains vendors ingest. The Dealer Mechanics Lab cites Cboe's figures: FLEX volume averaging 1.3 to 1.4 million contracts a day in 2025, more than ten times the 2019 level. Total FLEX open interest is 35 million contracts, up from 2 million in 2019, a 17.5× increase. Buffer ETFs, the [[defined-outcome ETF]] products built from FLEX, held more than $70 billion by mid-2025. Issuer literature says that their market makers "hedge through the broader listed options complex". So part of the FLEX book's gamma reappears in listed open interest, with a sign no convention assigns correctly.

**Over-the-counter (OTC) books** are harder still. OCC has cleared some [[over-the-counter]] S&P 500 index options since 2014, but bilateral books leave no public record. Foreign exchange shows what records make possible. Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette (2022) rebuilt aggregate dealer gamma in EURUSD and USDJPY options from trade-repository data, October 2017 to June 2018, and found it negative. No equivalent exists for U.S. equity-index options traded off the exchange.

**[[Structured products|structured product]]** are the largest unseen layer: autocallables, principal-protected notes and range accruals, which retail and private-wealth channels sell to clients around the world. An **[[autocallable]]** pays a coupon and can be redeemed early when the index is above a set level. It exposes its holder to losses if the index falls through a lower barrier. The issuing bank's hedge replicates the structure's option exposure dynamically. The Dealer Mechanics Lab says the autocallable book alone is measured globally in hundreds of billions of [[notional]], dominated by retail flow in Europe and Asia. The evidence on these hedges comes from other markets. In Korea, issuers of step-down autocallables are long vega, and their hedging went with lower implied volatility (Yoon 2014; Ji and co-authors 2015). In January 2024 China's CSI 500 and CSI 1000 indexes fell through the knock-in levels of "snowball" autocallables, set at 70% to 80% of initial levels, and a case study (Yin, 2026) lists institutional hedging pressure among the effects. For the S&P 500, a BIS box by Todorov and Vilkov (2024) proposed that option dealers "effectively dampen volatility when they hedge structured products", without measuring it.

The Lab's central claim is that **the invisible book matters most precisely when GEX matters most**. On a quiet day, FLEX and over-the-counter hedging is small and self-cancelling. On a stress day it can dominate everything the listed GEX number shows. That happens when a barrier is approached, when an autocallable book nears a knock-in level, or when an institutional buyer suddenly rolls a multi-billion-dollar collar. The claim is plausible, and nothing public quantifies it. The Lab adds that Volmageddon's VIX call wing was in FLEX, and that the March 2020 vanna cascade ran through structured-product hedges as much as through listed puts. Those are its interpretations. The documented accounts of February 2018 attribute the amplification to the rebalancing of leveraged and inverse VIX products (Chapter 8H), and no published estimate tests the March 2020 claim. The Lab expects the next stress event to feature a flow that surprises every published GEX model, and calls that almost certain.

Theory supports the worry, if not its size. Gennotte and Leland (1990) built a model in which hedging demand is not fully observed, and found that crashes "can occur even with relatively little hedging". The usual reading is that unseen hedging sales are mistaken for informed selling. Grossman (1988) argued that a traded put reveals the hedging demand behind it and a replicated one does not. Read together, they give listed open interest its value, since it is visible, and they make the unseen hedges the dangerous part.

::: insight
Published GEX is a useful prior on typical days. On atypical days, treat it as one of several inputs, not a forecast.
:::

## Units

Four units are in use, and vendors often do not say which. For one line with gamma \(\Gamma\), open interest OI and spot \(S\):

| Unit | Per line | To dollars per 1%, multiply by | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index units (shares) per $1 | \(\Gamma \cdot \text{OI} \cdot 100\) | \(S^2 \times 0.01\) | SqueezeMetrics' white paper |
| Dollars per index point | \(\Gamma \cdot \text{OI} \cdot 100 \cdot S\) | \(0.01\,S\) | SqueezeMetrics' index monitor; Tier1 Alpha |
| Dollars per 1% move | \(\Gamma \cdot \text{OI} \cdot 100 \cdot S^2 \cdot 0.01\) | 1 | SpotGamma, Barchart, this book |
| ES contracts per 1% | dollars per 1%, divided by \(50\,S\) | \(50\,S\) | Desk usage |

Skylit and GEXBot state no unit for their values, so their numbers cannot be converted at all.

::: worked One number in four units
**Step 1: per point.** A GEX of $10 billion per 1% at SPX 6,000 is \(10{,}000{,}000{,}000/60 \approx \$167\) million per index point, since a 1% move is 60 points.

**Step 2: index units and contracts.** In the white paper's unit it is \(10{,}000{,}000{,}000/(6{,}000^2 \times 0.01) \approx 27{,}800\) index units per $1 of index move. Per 1% move it is \(10{,}000{,}000{,}000/6{,}000 \approx 1.67\) million index units, or about \(10{,}000{,}000{,}000/300{,}000 \approx 33{,}000\) ES contracts.

**Step 3: a vendor's example.** SqueezeMetrics' monitor material explains a reading of 64,521,235: for every point the S&P 500 moves, dealers would need to trade "$64,521,235 worth of S&P 500 index exposure". If SPX stood at 3,000, that would be \(64.5 \text{ million} \times 30 \approx \$1.9\) billion per 1%. Another summary of the same material speaks of thousands of dollars per point, so even this unit needs checking.

**Step 4: a unit to check.** A Tier1 Alpha thread of October 2023, as a search summary shows it, had dealers "excessively short-gamma with around $1.7 billion to hedge per index point". At about 4,100 that would be \(1.7 \times 41 \approx \$70\) billion per 1%, far above SqueezeMetrics' example. Either the summary garbled the unit or the measure is defined differently, and a number like that should not be compared until the unit is known.
:::

An SPY or XSP contract carries about one tenth of an SPX contract's dollar gamma at the same moneyness, because its underlying is about one tenth the size (Chapter 6D).

## Six limits of the framework

The model has serious limits, and you should know them before you read any GEX chart with conviction. The first four are the subject of the sections above.

1. **The counterparty assumption:** as the profile builder's toggle made plain, the sign is fragile. The standard SqueezeMetrics convention assumes dealers are long calls and short puts, and the position data fit neither that nor its opposite for SPX. The right convention depends on which counterparty actually holds each option in aggregate, which is hard to observe.
2. **Open interest publishes with a lag:** the OCC publishes open interest T+1, that is, on the next business day. Intraday GEX is imputed by inference from the trade tape, with the classification errors described above.
3. **FLEX, over-the-counter and structured-product books are outside the chains:** their hedges are real, and public models cannot size them.
4. **Black-Scholes gamma is wrong under skew:** GEX uses Black-Scholes gamma at one volatility, as if the volatility surface were flat. The Dealer Mechanics Lab says that real put-wing gammas under steep skew can be several times the Black-Scholes number. It adds that the error does not reverse: it consistently understates put-side gamma in equity markets. Measured convexity departs from it in another way. Willeboordse (2026), across five underlyings from 2020 to 2025, found the correlation between empirically measured and Black-Scholes gamma significantly negative at a daily horizon, −0.25 to −0.47. The author attributes it to implied volatility moving against spot. Hull and White (2017) showed that the delta minimizing a hedge's variance folds in the expected change in volatility. A dealer using it trades differently from the Black-Scholes hedge GEX assumes.
5. **GEX is positioning, not outcome:** it tells you what dealers would do *given* a move, not whether a move happens. The effect sizes in the academic literature are real but modest. Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (2005) find that on expiration dates the returns of optionable single stocks are altered by an average of at least 16.5 basis points, 1996 to 2002. The paper does not study the index. At the index level, Golez and Jackwerth (2012) find S&P 500 futures pulled toward the at-the-money strike when options on the futures expire. Just before SPX options expire, the futures are pushed away from it. The notional shift is at least $115 million per expiration day. Barbon and Buraschi (2021), first posted in 2020, measure dealers' gamma imbalance. In one version of the paper, one standard deviation of it goes with a fall of about 12 basis points in a stock's absolute daily return, against an average of about 109. Another version gives 5 to 25 basis points for stocks, against an average of 180. For the indexes tested, one standard deviation goes with a fall of more than 20 basis points in the absolute daily return. Chapters 8E and 8G give these results in full.
6. **Dealers may not hedge the way the formula assumes:** Hu, Kirilova, Muravyev and Ryu (2023), an SSRN working paper, studied account-level data for Korean index options. Market makers reduced risk mainly by rebalancing their inventory within minutes, trading the options back out, rather than by delta hedging. Only a minority consistently delta hedged. In S&P 500 options, daily trading volume exceeds net position changes by a factor of 32. The authors conclude that "delta hedging is less universal than commonly assumed". Dong (2025) found that an option trade moves the underlying stock by 0.56 basis points over five minutes, about one sixth of a stock trade's effect. Multi-leg trades had negligible effects. Open-interest gamma can therefore overstate the hedge flow of [eq:8a/flow].

## An auditable recipe

Two working papers show what a disclosed method looks like. Chilingarian (2026) sets out "a three-model taxonomy" and ratifies one model, the SqueezeMetrics open-interest proxy, "as a written contract". It fixes the aggregation formula and "its unit scale", gives "a correctness rule" for the gamma flip, and shows on "a real 10,223-contract SPX snapshot" how switching conventions changes GEX. Sen (2026) publishes a complete engine for S&P 500 and gold futures options. It prices with Black-76 at each strike's own implied volatility and uses the standard dealer-sign convention. It solves for the zero-gamma level numerically and adds walls, pinning strikes, and vanna and charm overlays, and it runs for US$42.80 a month.

Neither paper makes the sign right. What they make is the sign visible, so that a reader can see the assumption, change it and watch the answer move. That is the standard to hold any GEX number to.

::: practice
Before you use a vendor's GEX number, find six things in its method:

- the sign rule, and whether the vendor has tested it;
- the data, and how old they are;
- whether intraday values are imputed from the tape, and how;
- which expiries are included;
- the volatility input, one flat number or each strike's own;
- the unit.

A number built from SPY open interest is not the same as one built from SPX, because wholesalers internalize much of the SPY flow (Chapter 2B). Then run the check this chapter's figures run: flip the convention, or shift the assumed shares, and see whether the sign survives. If it does not, the sign carries no information for you.
:::

The two traditions this book draws on agree on the method and differ on the weight. The Dealer Mechanics Lab reads the 0DTE-era evidence as putting SPX dealers net long gamma on most days. It makes the sign of GEX a central input to the regime (Chapter 8D). The Edge Book gives three reasons for caution: vendor models disagree, the 0DTE era changed who holds what, and a Cboe study of 2025 put 0DTE market-maker hedging at about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity. It keeps only the sign, as low-confidence regime context worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight, and never sizes from the magnitude (Chapter 10H). This chapter's evidence cuts both ways. The measured average sign for SPX same-day options is positive, as the Lab says. Yet the conventions behind most public numbers do not describe the positions, which supports the Edge Book's low confidence in any vendor's reading.

::: warn When it fails
GEX fails when it is most wanted. On a stress day the books outside the chains can dominate the listed number, the intraday figure is an imputation, and Black-Scholes misstates the put-wing gamma. The dealers' short put position itself shrinks when their constraints bind, which is when stress arrives. And a convention that fitted one period's flows can go stale when the dominant flow changes, as it did between 2017 and the 0DTE era.
:::

::: rule
Before you act on any GEX reading:

- **Name the convention.** If you cannot find the vendor's sign rule, do not use the sign.
- **Run the sensitivity.** Flip the convention, or read the sign map. If the sign flips under a modest change in who holds what, treat it as unknown.
- **Prefer measured signs.** For SPX, a sign built from exchange data on who traded with whom outranks one assumed by option type.
- **Use the sign, not the size or the levels.** Treat positive or negative GEX as regime context for sizing other trades (Chapters 8D and 10H). Never size from the magnitude, and read Chapter 8C before trading any level a map draws.
- **Discount the atypical day.** On a stress day, treat published GEX as one input among several, never as a forecast.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "GEX flipped negative today"
The claim is read as news about dealers, but it may be news about a vendor's convention. The same open interest gives opposite signs under the two conventions, and the flip stays where it is while the side that amplifies changes. Ask which convention before you ask what the number means.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: one convention for every product
A rule that signs calls positive and puts negative for every ticker treats SPX, SPY and single stocks alike. The position data do not: SPX dealers are short far puts and near-flat in calls, single-stock market makers have historically been long both, and SPY's flow is shaped by internalization. Check that the convention was built for the product you trade.
:::

::: recap
- Gamma is positive for calls and puts alike, so the sign of GEX is an assumption about inventory. Open interest, counted once a day without sides, cannot settle it.
- The JPM-era convention (calls +1, puts −1) and the 0DTE-era convention (the reverse) give opposite signs from the same book. Position data support the JPM era's short puts, not its long calls, and contradict the 0DTE era's premise that customers overwhelmingly sell premium.
- Measured SPX positions show dealers short about 19 million puts, mostly far out of the money, with calls near flat. Near the money, their same-day gamma is positive on average. Books like that damp on normal days and amplify in a sell-off, so no per-type convention fits.
- Intraday maps rest on inferred trade sides, which standard rules get wrong 17% to 41% of the time; errors shrink each strike toward zero and can flip small ones.
- FLEX, over-the-counter and structured-product books lie outside the standard chains, Black-Scholes gamma misstates the put wing, and dealers often trade options back out instead of hedging. Published GEX is a prior on typical days, not a forecast.
:::

::: check
?? The same open interest gives GEX of +$7.5 billion under one convention and −$7.5 billion under the other. What information would settle which is right?
!! Who holds each side: whether dealers are long or short each line in aggregate. Open interest counts contracts but not their holders. Without position data the sign is an assumption. That is why direct measurements of market-maker positions carry weight, such as those rebuilt from Cboe's Open-Close data by Dim, Eraker and Vilkov and by Terstegge.

?? Terstegge finds SPX dealers net short about 19 million puts, while Dim, Eraker and Vilkov find market makers' same-day gamma positive on average. How can both hold?
!! Contracts are not gamma. Far out-of-the-money puts carry little gamma at the current spot, while near-the-money short-dated options carry a great deal. A dealer long a modest block near the money and short a large block of far puts is net short by count and long by gamma. The worked example's book is +$7.76 billion by gamma. In a sell-off the far puts' gamma grows and the near block's fades, and the total turns negative.

?? A vendor's intraday map signs trades with a rule that misclassifies 20% of them, without bias. On average, how large does a strike with a true net of +1,000 contracts look, and what happens to a strike with a true net of +50?
!! On average each strike appears at \(1 - 2e = 0.6\) of its true net, so +1,000 looks like about +600. The +50 strike averages +30, but single draws scatter around that. A small net can easily come out negative, as the two flipped strikes in the figure's first draw did.

?? Why does the invisible book make published GEX less reliable on stress days than on quiet ones?
!! Public models see FLEX outside the standard chains and bilateral over-the-counter books not at all. On quiet days those hedges are small and, in the Dealer Mechanics Lab's account, self-cancelling. On stress days barrier levels, knock-in levels and large collar rolls can make them dominate the listed number. Theory adds that hedging the market cannot see is the kind that moves prices most.

?? One vendor quotes dealer gamma of $1.2 billion per index point and another $55 billion per 1% move, both with SPX at 5,000. Which is larger?
!! Convert the first: a 1% move at 5,000 is 50 points, so $1.2 billion per point is \(1.2 \times 50 = \$60\) billion per 1%, slightly larger than $55 billion. Only after converting, and after checking that both use the same sign rule and expiries, can the two be compared at all.
:::
