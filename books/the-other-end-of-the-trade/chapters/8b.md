::: where
Chapter 8A added every dealer's gamma into one number, gamma exposure, whose sign says whether dealers as a group are long or short gamma. This chapter shows what each sign does to prices: calm, mean-reverting markets on one side, trending and volatile ones on the other. It gives a simple model of the feedback, the measured effect sizes, and the decision rule each source draws from them.
:::

Everything in Parts I to VII compresses to a single regime question: **is the dealer book net long gamma or net short gamma?** The answer sets the sign of the feedback loop between hedging and prices, and with it the whole character of intraday price action.

::: plain
A dealer who is long gamma behaves like a thermostat, cooling the room when it warms and heating it when it cools. A dealer who is short gamma behaves like a microphone held too close to its speaker, so that every sound comes back louder. The news that moves prices is the same in both cases. What differs is whether the dealers' hedging pushes back against each move or joins it.
:::

## Two regimes, one mechanism

The two regimes are mirror images of each other, row by row:

| | Dealers long gamma: positive GEX | Dealers short gamma: negative GEX |
|---|---|---|
| Hedging | Sells strength, buys weakness | Buys strength, sells weakness |
| Intraday behavior | **[[Mean reversion]]** | **[[Momentum]]** |
| Volatility | **Compressed** relative to its unhedged level | **Expanded** relative to its unhedged level |
| Favored style | Range trading | [[Trend following]] |
| Premium | Selling it is favored, because dealer hedging suppresses the realized volatility you are betting against | Buying it is favored, because realized volatility is amplified |
| What fails | Breakouts often fail, because the dealer flow fades them | Mean reversion is dangerous: fades against the move get crushed |

A toy example shows the mechanism. Suppose GEX is +$5 billion per 1% move. By Chapter 8A's hedge-flow formula, a 1% rally then has dealers selling about $5 billion of index exposure and a 1% fall has them buying about $5 billion, so each move meets a flow against it. If GEX is −$5 billion instead, the same rally has dealers buying about $5 billion and the same fall has them selling about $5 billion. The news, and the first 1% move it caused, are identical in both cases. Only the sign of the response differs.

## The mathematics of feedback

Published GEX is positive when hedging is stabilizing, but the feedback maths reads more naturally with the opposite sign. So define the **[[destabilizing gamma]]** \(G\) as the negation of published GEX, in whatever units the model uses. It is positive when dealers are net short gamma, so that their pro-trend hedging amplifies moves. It is negative when they are net long gamma, so that their contra-trend hedging damps moves. With that sign convention, the Dealer Mechanics Lab writes

\[ \sigma_\text{eff}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{(1 - \kappa G)^2} \]
{#eq:eff}

in which each symbol has a plain meaning, and the feedback coefficient \(\kappa\) carries the state of the market:

| symbol | meaning |
|---|---|
| \(\sigma\) | the information volatility: how volatile prices would be from news alone, with no hedging feedback |
| \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) | the **[[effective volatility]]**: the volatility prices show once dealer hedging feeds back into them |
| \(G\) | the destabilizing gamma, the negation of published GEX |
| \(\kappa\) | the feedback coefficient: how far prices move per unit of hedging flow, larger when market depth is thin and when dealers hedge aggressively |

Only the product \(\kappa G\) matters, so the units of \(G\) and \(\kappa\) can be anything that makes it a pure number. Taking square roots, and keeping \(\kappa G\) below 1, gives \(\sigma_\text{eff} = \sigma / (1 - \kappa G)\), and the denominator sorts the regimes:

| \(G\) | Published GEX | Dealers | \(1 - \kappa G\) | Effective volatility | Regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| below 0 | positive | long gamma | above 1 | below \(\sigma\) | **Stabilized** |
| 0 | zero | flat | 1 | equal to \(\sigma\) | **Neutral** |
| above 0 | negative | short gamma | below 1 | above \(\sigma\) | **Amplified** |
| toward \(+1/\kappa\) | strongly negative | deeply short gamma, in extreme stress | toward 0 | toward infinity | **Edge of feedback explosion** |

That divergence is, mathematically, what February 5, 2018 (Volmageddon), March 16, 2020 (the Great Liquidity Crisis) and August 5, 2024 (the yen carry unwind) looked like, the cases Chapter 8C takes in detail.

::: grind Where the formula comes from: a feedback multiplier
One simple model produces [eq:eff] exactly. It is a toy rather than the Dealer Mechanics Lab's derivation, but it shows what \(\kappa\) and \(G\) do.

**Step 1: the first round.** News moves the price by a return \(\varepsilon\), with standard deviation \(\sigma\). Dealers hedge that move, and their trade moves the price by a further \(\kappa G\,\varepsilon\), with the first move if \(G > 0\) and against it if \(G < 0\).

**Step 2: the rounds that follow.** The extra move needs hedging too, which adds \(\kappa G\) times it, \((\kappa G)^2 \varepsilon\), and that needs hedging in turn. When \(\kappa G\) lies between −1 and 1, the rounds shrink and the total is a geometric series:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{move} &= \varepsilon\,\big(1 + \kappa G + (\kappa G)^2 + \cdots\big) \\ &= \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \kappa G} \end{aligned} \]

**Step 3: the volatility.** Dividing a random quantity by a constant divides its standard deviation by the same constant. The total move therefore has standard deviation \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\), and squaring it gives [eq:eff].

**Step 4: over time.** The regime figure spreads the same feedback over successive steps, \(r_t = w\,r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\). A shock then reaches the price as \(\varepsilon_t\) in its own step, \(w\,\varepsilon_t\) in the next and \(w^2\varepsilon_t\) in the one after, for a total of \(\varepsilon_t/(1 - w)\) once the echoes die out. The paths' volatility over many steps follows the same formula, with \(w = \kappa G\).
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: The feedback multiplier as a cascade. One news shock is drawn as a bar, and each round of hedging adds a smaller bar after it: in the same direction when κG is positive, alternating when it is negative. The reader sets κG from −0.9 to +0.95 and watches the bars add up to the total, ε/(1 − κG). ::: worked The regime figure, in the formula
**Step 1: the weights.** The regime figure's feedback of 0.45 is \(\kappa G = -0.45\) under positive GEX and \(\kappa G = +0.45\) under negative GEX.

**Step 2: the multipliers.** Under positive GEX the multiplier is \(1/(1 + 0.45) = 0.69\), so hedging removes about 31% of the volatility. Under negative GEX it is \(1/(1 - 0.45) = 1.82\), so hedging adds about 82%.

**Step 3: the comparison.** The ratio of the two multipliers is \(1.82/0.69 = 2.64\). The figure's six paths span about 3.6 points under negative GEX against 1.5 under positive, a ratio of about 2.4. Six paths over 60 steps are too small a sample for a closer match.
:::

::: familiar
This is the design effect of a cluster-randomized trial. When outcomes within a cluster are positively correlated, the variance of an estimate is inflated by a factor that grows with the correlation, while a negative correlation would deflate it. Returns work the same way: positive feedback correlates each step with the one before and inflates the variance of the cumulative move, while negative feedback deflates it. The variance ratio test of Chapter 3B measures exactly this inflation.
:::

### The divergence is nonlinear

A linear reading, that at \(G = 0\) effective volatility equals \(\sigma\), understates what makes the formula matter in practice. Fix \(\sigma = 0.15\), a baseline information volatility of 15%, and a small illustrative feedback coefficient \(\kappa = 0.0010\), then sweep \(G\) across the regimes:

| G (positioning regime) | \(1 - \kappa G\) | \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| −800 (deeply stabilizing) | 1.80 | 8.3% |
| −400 (mildly stabilizing) | 1.40 | 10.7% |
| 0 (neutral) | 1.00 | 15.0% |
| +400 (mildly destabilizing) | 0.60 | 25.0% |
| +600 (moderate stress) | 0.40 | 37.5% |
| +800 (acute stress) | 0.20 | **75%** |
| +900 (near-critical) | 0.10 | **150%** |
| +950 | 0.05 | 300% |
| +990 (edge of explosion) | 0.01 | 1,500% |

From \(G = +800\) to \(G = +900\), a roughly 12% increase in destabilizing positioning, \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) doubles from 75% to 150%. **That is the operational signal: small changes in aggregate positioning produce outsized realized-volatility consequences in the stress regime.** It is also why the [[gamma flip]] matters as a level even though no single trade is responsible for it.

The slope of the curve makes this exact: differentiating \(\sigma / (1 - \kappa G)\) with respect to \(G\) gives

\[ \frac{d\sigma_\text{eff}}{dG} = \frac{\kappa\,\sigma}{(1 - \kappa G)^2} \]
{#eq:slope}

::: worked How steep the curve gets
**Step 1: at neutral.** At \(G = 0\), [eq:slope] gives \(0.0010 \times 0.15 = 0.00015\) per unit of \(G\), so a hundred more units of \(G\) add about \(100 \times 0.00015 = 0.015\), or 1.5 points of volatility.

**Step 2: in acute stress.** At \(G = +800\) the denominator is \(0.2^2 = 0.04\), so the slope is \(0.00015/0.04 = 0.00375\), which is 25 times as steep. A straight line from there predicts 37.5 more points for the next 100 units of \(G\).

**Step 3: the curve.** The table's actual rise from \(G = +800\) to \(+900\) is 75 points, double even that, because the slope keeps growing along the way. The local sensitivity of \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) to \(G\) is unbounded as \(G\) approaches \(1/\kappa\).
:::

The value \(\kappa = 0.0010\) is only illustrative, since in practice \(\kappa\) is fitted to the regime and varies with depth, with dealer aggressiveness and with how concentrated positioning is. The qualitative shape, slow at the wings and explosive near the critical point, is the universal feature.

**A note on signs.** \(G\) is the *negation* of standard published GEX. Suppose a vendor reports "GEX = +$2.3B" under the JPM-era convention, where positive means stabilizing; the corresponding \(G\) in [eq:eff] is −$2.3B. Both ways of signing the number coexist in practice, so check which one your model or vendor uses before you read the sign.

## Empirical effect sizes

The dealer-feedback story is real and quantifiable, but its practical effect is modest on a typical day. Several academic papers, some peer-reviewed and some working papers, have measured it:

- **Barbon and Buraschi (2021)**, in "Gamma Fragility" (SSRN 3725454, revised March 18, 2021), document a link between large aggregate dealer gamma imbalances and intraday momentum and reversal in stock returns. They find supporting evidence that intraday momentum is explained by the interaction of negative ex-ante gamma imbalance with illiquidity, and reversal by the interaction of positive imbalance with illiquidity. The effect is stronger for the least liquid underlying securities. That finding matters, because it suggests that SPX itself, which is highly liquid, has smaller gamma-feedback effects than less liquid single stocks.
- **Ni, Pearson, Poteshman and White (2021)** extend the original 2005 pinning paper. They find that hedge rebalancing alters the probability of large daily moves, over 300 bps, by 5% to 14% historically, rising to 13% to 40% in their later sample.
- **Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024)** find, for the 0DTE regime, that net market-maker gamma is **on average positive** and **negatively related to future intraday volatility**. The hedging pattern they find is consistent with delta hedging but inconsistent with information-based trading. It is the academic foundation for the claim that 0DTE options have, on net, *reduced* intraday volatility in SPX.
- **Adams, Dim, Eraker, Fontaine, Ornthanalai and Vilkov (2025)**, the successor paper, establish causally that the presence of 0DTE options dampens market volatility. The dampening works through longer-dated positions becoming 0DTEs over time, not through trading on the expiration day itself.

The episodes that everyone remembers are February 2018, March 2020, the GME squeeze and the August 5, 2024 unwind. They are the **right tail** of this distribution, where feedback briefly approached the unstable threshold. On most days, GEX is a probabilistic prior you can use to bias your strategy selection, not a deterministic forecast.

## Regime as a decision boundary

For practical trading, the regime works as a decision boundary on strategy selection, and the shape of the VIX term structure joins the GEX sign in drawing it. The [[VIX]] term structure is in [[contango]] when longer-dated implied volatility sits above shorter-dated, the usual calm shape. It is in [[backwardation]] when that order inverts under stress (Chapter 5E).

- **Positive GEX, VIX in contango, no event imminent:** premium-selling and mean-reversion strategies are favored. Realized volatility is likely to undershoot implied, range bets pay, and tight stops work.
- **Negative GEX, VIX in backwardation, an event imminent or just past:** directional and long-volatility strategies are favored. Realized volatility is likely to exceed implied, trend bets pay, and wider stops are needed.
- **Transition regimes, with GEX near zero and the VIX term structure in transition:** reduce size, take only the highest-conviction setups, and expect choppy intraday action.

Chapter 11G formalizes this into the composite regime scorecard. For now, the principle is: **strategy selection should be conditioned on regime, and regime is most concisely characterized by the sign of net dealer gamma combined with the shape of the VIX term structure.**

### How the Edge Book weighs the regime

The Edge Book agrees on the direction of the effect: negative-GEX days tend to have higher realized volatility, and positive-GEX days compressed realized volatility (Chapter 10H). It gives the sign far less weight, treating GEX as a low-confidence filter: a soft input worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight. If GEX disagrees with the VIX term structure or with the realized-volatility filters, the Edge Book ignores it, and if it agrees, it adds slight confidence.

In the Edge Book's use, the sign adjusts the size of other strategies, for example by trimming put-writing modestly when GEX is negative. It never sizes from the magnitude of GEX, which it calls too model-dependent. It also rejects trading at specific GEX levels for retail accounts (Chapters 1G and 10I). Its reasons are the model risk of Chapter 8A, the change in structure as 0DTE options grew after 2022, and a Cboe study from 2025. That study put 0DTE market-maker hedging at about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity. The two sources agree that the sign is regime context and differ on how much it should move a decision.

::: practice
In an account, the regime changes size and stops before it changes which trades you take. A stop set at two standard deviations of a day's move sits at about 1.9% when effective volatility is 15%, because a day's standard deviation is then \(15\%/\sqrt{252} = 0.95\%\) (Chapter 5E). At 29%, the top of the figure's default stress run, the same rule puts the stop at about 3.7%, and a stop left at 1.9% is hit by ordinary noise. Size falls for the same reason: with the stop nearly twice as far away, the same dollar risk buys about half the position.
:::

::: warn When it fails
The regime read fails in two ways. The sign itself can be wrong, since it rests on a convention about who holds what (Chapter 8A). And \(\kappa\) is not a constant: when depth vanishes, the same destabilizing gamma that meant mild amplification can approach the singular point, where a linear intuition badly understates the move (Chapter 8C).
:::

::: rule
Condition size and style on the regime, and let the sign of GEX nudge rather than decide:

- **Positive GEX, contango, no event near:** premium selling and mean reversion may run at normal size, with tight stops.
- **Negative GEX with backwardation or an event:** favor directional and long-volatility trades, widen stops, and cut short-premium size.
- **GEX near zero or the term structure in transition:** reduce size and take only the highest-conviction setups.
- **Always:** use the sign, never the magnitude, and give it less weight than the term structure (Chapter 10H).
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading the regime as a forecast
Positive GEX does not mean the market cannot move: on most days the regime is only a probabilistic prior. The episodes that did damage were the right tail, when the feedback briefly approached its unstable threshold. A sizing rule has to survive those episodes, not only the typical day.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: mixing up G and GEX
The feedback formula uses \(G\), the negation of published GEX. Put a vendor's "+$2.3B" into it unconverted, and a stabilizing regime reads as an amplifying one. Convert the sign first, and then check the vendor's convention from Chapter 8A as well.
:::

::: recap
- Long dealer gamma (positive GEX) makes hedging trade against moves: mean reversion and compressed volatility. Short dealer gamma (negative GEX) makes it trade with them: momentum and expanded volatility.
- With destabilizing gamma \(G = -\text{GEX}\), effective volatility is \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\): damped for \(G < 0\), amplified for \(G > 0\), and unbounded as \(G\) nears \(1/\kappa\). Its slope grows as \(1/(1 - \kappa G)^2\), so small changes in positioning matter most in stress.
- Measured effects are modest on typical days and large only in the right tail.
- Both sources condition strategy on the regime. The Dealer Mechanics Lab gives the GEX sign a central place beside the VIX term structure, while the Edge Book gives it a low-confidence one.
:::

::: check
?? With \(\sigma = 20\%\) and \(\kappa = 0.5\), what is effective volatility at \(G = +0.8\), and what does that \(G\) say about dealers?
!! Since \(1 - 0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.6\), effective volatility is \(0.20/0.6 = 33\%\). A positive \(G\) is negative published GEX: dealers are net short gamma, and their hedging amplifies moves.

?? With \(\sigma = 0.15\) and \(\kappa = 0.0010\), how much does effective volatility rise when \(G\) moves from +400 to +600, and when it moves from −600 to −400?
!! From +400 to +600 it rises from 25% to 37.5%, which is 12.5 points. From −600 to −400 it rises from \(0.15/1.6 = 9.4\%\) to \(0.15/1.4 = 10.7\%\), about 1.3 points. The same 200 units of positioning do about ten times as much on the destabilizing side.

?? Why do the regime figure's negative-GEX paths trend, although the random shocks have no drift?
!! Each return carries +0.45 times the one before, so a move tends to be followed by another in the same direction, which is positive autocorrelation, or momentum. Over many steps each shock's effect on the price is multiplied by \(1/(1 - 0.45) = 1.82\).

?? Barbon and Buraschi find the effect stronger for less liquid securities. What does that imply for SPX against a single stock?
!! SPX is highly liquid, so its gamma-feedback effects should be smaller than those of less liquid single stocks. In the terms of [eq:eff], a deeper market means a smaller \(\kappa\) and so a smaller amplification for the same positioning.

?? A vendor reports GEX of −$3 billion, the VIX term structure is in contango, and no event is near. How does each source treat this?
!! The signals disagree, so the Dealer Mechanics Lab's boundary fits neither clean case and its transition rule applies: reduce size and take only the highest-conviction setups. The Edge Book ignores GEX when it disagrees with the term structure, so it sizes as contango suggests (Chapter 10H).
:::
