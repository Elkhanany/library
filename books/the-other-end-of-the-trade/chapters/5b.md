## Two parts of a premium

An option's premium is the sum of two distinct components: **[[intrinsic value]]**, what the option would be worth if exercised this instant, and **[[extrinsic value]]**, also called time value, which is everything else. Extrinsic value is the market's price for the remaining uncertainty and time. The decomposition is exact, and the intuition behind it is foundational:

\[ \text{premium} = \text{intrinsic} + \text{extrinsic} \]
{#eq:split}

Intrinsic value is the payoff of Chapter 5A with today's price \(S\) in place of the price at expiry:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{call intrinsic} &= \max(S - K,\, 0) \\
\text{put intrinsic} &= \max(K - S,\, 0)
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:intrinsic}

Take a call with a strike of $100 and the stock at $103. Exercising it would let you buy at $100 and sell at once at $103, capturing $3 a share, so the intrinsic value is exactly $3. With the stock at $97 instead, exercising would mean paying $100 for something worth $97. Rational holders do not exercise at a loss, so the intrinsic value is zero.

Extrinsic value is whatever the premium holds beyond the intrinsic value:

\[ \text{extrinsic} = \text{premium} - \text{intrinsic} \]
{#eq:extrinsic}

It captures the time remaining, the market's expectation of volatility, and the chance of a favorable move before expiry. For an option that can be exercised at once, extrinsic value is never negative, since exercising would always capture the intrinsic value. A deep in-the-money European put can trade slightly below its intrinsic value, because its holder must wait until expiry to receive the strike, and waiting costs interest.

How the premium divides between the two parts depends on moneyness, the position of the strike relative to spot:

- **Deep [[in-the-money]] (ITM) options**, with spot well above the strike for a call or well below it for a put, have a small extrinsic part. Such an option is almost all intrinsic and behaves like a delta-1 position in the underlying (Chapter 5C).
- **[[Out-of-the-money]] (OTM) options** have no intrinsic part, so all their value is extrinsic, resting on the possibility of a favorable move. The further out of the money, the higher the extrinsic value relative to the intrinsic. A deep OTM option is a pure bet on a tail move.
- **[[At-the-money]] (ATM) options have the maximum extrinsic value**, because they carry the maximum uncertainty about whether they finish in or out of the money.

::: plain
Extrinsic value is what you pay for the chance that things change before expiry. Deep in the money, an option is almost sure to be exercised, so it is nearly the underlying itself, bought at the strike. Far out of the money, it is almost sure to expire worthless, so it is cheap and every cent of it is a bet. At the money nobody knows which way it finishes, and that uncertainty costs the most.
:::

::: worked Extrinsic value in a coin-flip market
**Step 1: set up.** Suppose a stock stands at 100 today and ends at either 90 or 110 at expiry, with equal probability. Ignore interest, and price each call at its expected payoff, the probability-weighted average of Chapter 1C.

**Step 2: the at-the-money call.** A call struck at 100 pays 10 or nothing, so it is worth \(\tfrac12 \times 10 + \tfrac12 \times 0 = 5\), all of it extrinsic because its intrinsic value is zero.

**Step 3: in the money.** A call struck at 95 pays 15 or nothing, which is worth 7.50, of which 5 is intrinsic and 2.50 extrinsic. A call struck at 90 pays 20 or nothing, worth 10, all of it intrinsic. Its payoff equals \(S_T - 90\) in both outcomes, so its floor at zero is never used, and the option is worth no more than exercising it now.

**Step 4: out of the money.** A call struck at 105 pays 5 or nothing, worth 2.50, all of it extrinsic. A call struck at 110 can never pay, so it is worth nothing.

| Strike | Pays if 90 | Pays if 110 | Premium | Intrinsic | Extrinsic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90 | 0 | 20 | 10.00 | 10 | 0 |
| 95 | 0 | 15 | 7.50 | 5 | 2.50 |
| 100 | 0 | 10 | 5.00 | 0 | 5.00 |
| 105 | 0 | 5 | 2.50 | 0 | 2.50 |
| 110 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

**Step 5: the pattern.** Extrinsic value peaks at the money and falls away on both sides, reaching zero where the floor at zero can no longer matter.
:::

## The option chain

The **[[option chain]]** is the central interface for options trading: calls on the left, puts on the right, and strikes down the middle, with each row one strike at the chosen expiry. The critical columns are the bid, the ask, the volume and the open interest, not the "last" price, which can be stale and misleading.

Here is a synthetic chain for a stock at $150 with 32 **[[days to expiry]]** (DTE). Its prices come from the Black-Scholes model of Chapter 5C at a single implied volatility of about 14%, with interest ignored, so that they obey put-call parity (Chapter 5F).

| CallVol | CallOI | CallBid | CallAsk | Strike | PutBid | PutAsk | PutOI | PutVol |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 776 | 3,581 | 10.08 | 10.18 | 140 | 0.08 | 0.18 | 2,097 | 381 |
| 613 | 2,246 | 5.68 | 5.78 | 145 | 0.68 | 0.78 | 4,622 | 214 |
| 125 | 1,441 | 3.56 | 3.66 | 148 | 1.56 | 1.66 | 2,973 | 514 |
| 826 | 1,408 | 2.45 | 2.55 | **150** | 2.45 | 2.55 | 2,889 | 359 |
| 653 | 3,392 | 1.59 | 1.69 | 152 | 3.59 | 3.69 | 2,646 | 632 |
| 557 | 2,048 | 0.74 | 0.84 | 155 | 5.74 | 5.84 | 1,531 | 410 |
| 730 | 2,065 | 0.12 | 0.22 | 160 | 10.12 | 10.22 | 1,150 | 440 |

The 140-strike call is deep in the money, with $10 of intrinsic value, so its premium near $10.13 is almost all intrinsic. The small extrinsic part, 13 cents, remains because at 32 DTE there is still some uncertainty. The 140-strike put is deep out of the money, so its premium is near zero. The 150-strike call and put, at the money, are nearly identical in price ($2.45 to $2.55) because put-call parity (Chapter 5F) ties them together. The deep out-of-the-money 160-strike call has a near-zero premium because a 7% move in 32 days is unlikely.

Subtract each option's intrinsic value from its mid-quote and the coin-flip pattern appears. The extrinsic value runs $0.13, $0.73, $1.61, $2.50, $1.64, $0.79 and $0.17 from the 140 strike to the 160, peaking at the money. A call and a put at the same strike carry the same extrinsic value here, which is put-call parity at work in a chain without interest.

The bars form a hump centered on the money that flattens and narrows as expiry nears, and the call and put bars at each strike stay equal while interest is set to zero.]

### What each column means

- **Bid and ask:** the highest price a buyer is offering right now, and the lowest price a seller is asking. The [[bid-ask spread]] between them is your immediate execution cost. SPY options often have $0.01 spreads on liquid ATM strikes, SPX can have $0.10 to $0.30, and XSP sits somewhere in between. Always use [[limit orders|limit order]] on options, which name the worst price you accept, and never [[market orders|market order]], which accept whatever price is there.
- **Last:** the price of the most recent trade, which can be hours or even days old in an illiquid strike. Treat it as history rather than as a price you can act on, and read the bid and the ask instead.
- **Volume:** the number of contracts traded today, a measure of current interest that resets at the start of each trading session.
- **Open interest (OI):** the total number of contracts at this strike that have been opened but not yet closed or expired. **[[Open interest]]** is the single best liquidity indicator. High open interest means a deep market with narrow spreads. Low open interest means wide spreads, potential [[slippage]] and, in the worst case, a strike where you cannot exit at any reasonable price.
- **ITM shading:** most brokerage platforms shade the in-the-money rows. Calls are in the money below spot (strike below spot) and puts above it (strike above spot), so the shading flips at the at-the-money row. In the chain above, the calls from 140 to 148 and the puts from 152 to 160 would be shaded.

Every trade has a buyer and a seller, and each of them is either opening a position or closing one. That decides how the trade moves open interest:

| Buyer | Seller | Open interest |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Opening | Rises by the contracts traded |
| Opening | Closing | Unchanged: an existing position changes hands |
| Closing | Opening | Unchanged: an existing position changes hands |
| Closing | Closing | Falls by the contracts traded |

::: familiar
Volume and open interest are the incidence and prevalence of a market. Open interest is a stock, like prevalence: the number of positions alive at one moment. It rises as positions open and falls as they close or expire, as prevalence rises with new cases and falls with recovery and death. Volume is a flow over a day, but it counts every trade, opening or closing, so it resembles admissions and discharges together rather than new cases alone. A 0DTE strike is an illness that begins and ends within the day.
:::

## The bid-ask trap

Two options can look identically priced, both showing $3.00 as the last trade, and still cost very different amounts to trade. Suppose the first is quoted $2.95 bid, $3.05 ask and the second $2.50 bid, $3.50 ask. Both have a **[[mid-quote]]**, the average of bid and ask, of $3.00, but the second is dramatically more expensive to trade.

You either lift the ask at $3.50, paying $0.50 over the midpoint, or sit on the bid at $2.50 and possibly never fill. That $0.50 of extra cost is \(0.50/3.00 = 16.7\%\) of the option's value, which is the entire [[variance risk premium]] edge you would expect to harvest. Two trades at this spread, one to enter and one to exit, can wipe out three months of theta collection (theta is the daily decay of Chapter 5C).

Measure every spread as a share of the mid:

\[ \text{spread share} = \frac{\text{ask} - \text{bid}}{\text{mid}} \]
{#eq:spread}

For the tight quote, [eq:spread] gives \(0.10/3.00 = 3.3\%\). A [[round trip]], buying at the ask and later selling at the bid, costs the full $0.10, or $10 a contract. The wide quote gives \(1.00/3.00 = 33\%\), with a round trip costing $100 a contract, ten times as much for an option of the same value. The tight quote passes a 5% limit but not a 2% one, while the wide quote fails both by a long way.

**Bid-ask discipline is not a detail: it is the floor under everything else.** The recommended retail discipline is never to trade an option unless its bid-ask spread is less than 5% of the midpoint, and ideally less than 2%. For ATM SPY options this is trivially met. For deep out-of-the-money, low-OI single-name options it often is not, and the right response is to not trade that option.

A related practice is **mid-price filling**: most professional options strategies enter at the bid-ask midpoint or better, using limit orders that work the midpoint rather than crossing the spread. If you place a limit order at the midpoint and the market does not fill you, the trade probably was not worth doing at the price you wanted. Patience is the cost of getting better fills. Impatience is the cost of paying the full spread on every trade.

## Open interest through expiry

Open interest changes daily as contracts open and close. For most strikes it rises through the early life of the contract as positions are built, then falls in the final week as positions are closed before expiry. The 0DTE phenomenon in SPX has changed this dramatically: for a [[0DTE]] strike, open interest is functionally zero, since the strike did not exist yesterday, and volume tells the whole story.

Watching open interest change can be informative, as when open interest at one strike jumps by 50,000 contracts on a day when volume there was also 50,000 contracts. By the table above, every contract that traded opened a new position on both sides, which tells you a single large player took a new position at that strike. Smaller and more frequent changes in open interest suggest broader market activity.

For dealer mechanics (Part VIII), aggregate open interest across all strikes is the input to the **[[gamma exposure]]** (GEX) calculation of Chapter 8A. The *shape* of open interest across strikes determines the gamma flip level and the regime characterization of Chapter 8B.

[FIGURE IDEA: The life of one strike's open interest and volume, day by day from listing to expiry, for a monthly SPX option beside a 0DTE one. The reader moves a day slider and sees open interest build, plateau and drain in the final week for the monthly, while the 0DTE strike shows a single day of heavy volume on zero open interest.]

::: practice
- **Read the quote before the price:** check the bid, the ask and the open interest first, then compute the spread's share of the mid with [eq:spread]. At SPX spreads of $0.10 to $0.30, each round trip costs $10 to $30 a contract before commissions.
- **Enter at the mid:** place a limit order at the midpoint and work it, moving it a little at a time if it does not fill, or letting the trade go.
- **Judge same-day options by volume:** a 0DTE strike has no open interest, so its day's volume is the only measure of how easily you can get out.
:::

::: warn When it fails
The spread rule assumes that the spread you see on entry is the one you pay on exit. Under stress, spreads widen (Chapter 1F), so a position opened at a 2% spread may close only at a far wider one. Open interest is also a count as of the previous day, so it says nothing about a 0DTE strike and can lag a busy session.
:::

::: rule
- **Trade an option only if its spread is below 5% of the mid**, and prefer below 2%.
- **Use limit orders at the mid**, and never market orders.
- **If the mid does not fill**, reprice patiently or skip the trade rather than chase the ask.
- **Prefer strikes with high open interest**, and for 0DTE options, where open interest is zero, look at volume instead.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: trusting the last price
The last price can be hours or days old in an illiquid strike. Two options with the same last price can have spreads ten times apart, so price every trade off the current bid and ask.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: buying far out of the money because it is cheap
A deep out-of-the-money option is all extrinsic value, a pure bet on a tail move. Its spread is usually the widest on the chain as a share of its price. In the chain above, the 160 call is quoted $0.12 bid and $0.22 ask, a spread of \(0.10/0.17 = 59\%\) of the mid, which fails the 5% rule many times over.
:::

::: recap
- A premium is intrinsic value, what exercise would pay now, plus extrinsic value, the price of the time and uncertainty left.
- Extrinsic value peaks at the money. Deep in the money the option is nearly all intrinsic; far out of the money it is all extrinsic.
- On a chain, read the bid, the ask and the open interest, not the last price. Open interest counts the positions still open, and volume counts today's trades.
- Trade only when the spread is below 5% of the mid, ideally 2%, with limit orders at the midpoint.
:::

::: check
?? A call struck at 150 costs $4.10 with the stock at $153.40. How much of the premium is intrinsic value and how much is extrinsic?
!! By [eq:intrinsic] the intrinsic value is \(153.40 - 150 = \$3.40\), which by [eq:extrinsic] leaves \(4.10 - 3.40 = \$0.70\) of extrinsic value.

?? A put struck at 95 costs $1.20 with the stock at $100. What is its extrinsic value, and what does the premium pay for?
!! The put is out of the money, so its intrinsic value is zero and all $1.20 is extrinsic. It pays only for the chance that the stock falls below 95 before expiry.

?? An option is quoted $1.80 bid and $2.20 ask. Does it pass the spread rule, and what does lifting the ask on 10 contracts cost over the mid?
!! The mid is $2.00, so by [eq:spread] the spread is \(0.40/2.00 = 20\%\), which fails the 5% rule. Lifting the ask pays $0.20 over the mid, which on 10 contracts is \(0.20 \times 100 \times 10 = \$200\).

?? Open interest at a strike stood at 12,000 yesterday and at 15,000 today, after 8,000 contracts traded there. What can you say about today's trades?
!! Open interest rose by 3,000, and only trades in which both sides open a position raise it. So at least 3,000 of today's 8,000 contracts were opened on both sides. The rest could be positions changing hands, or closings offset by further openings.

?? In the coin-flip market, why is the at-the-money call's extrinsic value larger than the extrinsic value of the call struck at 95?
!! The call struck at 100 pays 10 or nothing, so its whole value of 5 depends on which way the coin lands. The call struck at 95 already has 5 of intrinsic value, and only 2.50 of its 7.50 depends on the coin. The more the outcome is in doubt, the more extrinsic value the option carries.
:::
