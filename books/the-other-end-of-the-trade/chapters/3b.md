::: plain
Momentum means a move tends to be followed by another in the same direction. Mean reversion means it tends to be followed by a move back, and a random walk means the last move says nothing about the next. One number, the weight a model puts on the last return, tells the three apart. In real markets that number is small, it depends on the horizon you look at, and it changes sign between calm markets and stressed ones.

## The AR(1) model

The most basic useful model is an autoregressive linear regression of order one, or **[[AR(1) model]]**. It is autoregressive because the series is regressed on its own past, and of order one because it uses one lag. It captures the two most fundamental trading behaviors, mean reversion and momentum, by predicting the next [[log return]] as a linear function of the previous one:

\[ \hat{r}_t = w\,r_{t-1} + b \]
{#eq:ar1}

Here \(r_{t-1}\) is the last period's log return and \(\hat{r}_t\) the prediction for the current period. The weight \(w\) and the bias \(b\) are the model's only two parameters, and the sign of \(w\) determines everything.

The model describes a process in which each return is the prediction plus a fresh shock:

\[ r_t = w\,r_{t-1} + b + \varepsilon_t \]
{#eq:process}

The **[[innovation]]** \(\varepsilon_t\) is a random shock with mean zero and standard deviation \(\sigma\), independent of everything before it. Chapter 1G wrote the same equation for the decay of a pricing error, with \(\phi\) (phi) in place of \(w\). Statisticians usually write \(\phi\), and the first figure below does too.

A worked prediction: with \(w = -0.05\) and \(b = 0\), a day that rose 1% predicts \(-0.05 \times 1\% = -0.05\%\) for the next. With a hypothetical bias of \(b = 0.03\%\), a daily drift, the prediction becomes \(-0.05\% + 0.03\% = -0.02\%\).

## Three regimes

**\(w < 0\): [[mean reversion]].** A negative weight flips the sign of the lag, so a positive return predicts a negative one and a negative return predicts a positive one: "what goes up must come down". This regime is common at second-to-minute timescales in liquid markets, where dealer mean reversion (from gamma hedging) and microstructure noise (the bid-ask bounce) dominate. It is also common at multi-week timescales for individual equities and indices, where overreaction followed by correction recurs.

**\(w > 0\): [[momentum]].** A positive weight reinforces the sign: "what goes up keeps going up". This regime is most common at intermediate timescales, weeks to months, across asset classes. It is the empirical foundation of decades of [[trend following|trend following]] research. It is also the basis of factor strategies such as Jegadeesh-Titman 12-1 momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993, *Journal of Finance*), which ranks stocks by their past 12-month return excluding the latest month (Chapter 10F). Cross-asset trend has been documented in 140+ years of data by Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen (2017, *Journal of Portfolio Management*).

**\(w \approx 0\): [[random walk]].** The next return is independent of the previous one, so the model has no predictive content. This is the **[[efficient market hypothesis]]** in its weakest form, and it is a good approximation at most timescales for most liquid markets. Trade fees, slippage and [[adverse selection]] eat any strategy that operates in this regime.

### How far the paths spread

A process is **[[stationary]]** when its statistical behavior does not drift over time: its variance stays bounded, its distribution stays the same, and forecasts far ahead converge to the long-run mean. Taking the variance of both sides of [eq:process] shows when the AR(1) qualifies. Write \(V\) for the variance of each return. Because \(\varepsilon_t\) is independent of \(r_{t-1}\), their variances add, and a stationary \(V\) must satisfy \(V = w^2 V + \sigma^2\), so

\[ V = \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - w^2} \]
{#eq:var}

With \(\sigma = 1\%\) and \(w = 0.5\), that is \(1/0.75 = 1.33\) in squared percent, a standard deviation of 1.15% a period. The formula has a positive answer only when \(|w| < 1\), which is the condition for stationarity. As \(w\) approaches either +1 or −1, \(V\) grows without limit.

What a position lives through, though, is the accumulated return: the price path. Here the two ends behave in opposite ways. Over \(n\) periods, the standard deviation of the summed returns is approximately

\[ \text{sd}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n} r_t\Big) \approx \frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{1 - w} \]
{#eq:spread}

A random walk (\(w = 0\)) gives the square-root-of-time rule of Chapter 1A, \(\sigma\sqrt{n}\). As \(w\) approaches +1, the denominator shrinks toward zero and the paths fan out without limit. As \(w\) approaches −1, the denominator approaches 2 and the paths draw in: each return is largely undone by the next, so the price stays nearer its start than a random walk does.

::: grind Where the spread of the paths comes from
**Step 1: add up the process.** Sum [eq:process] over \(t = 1\) to \(n\), with \(b = 0\) and \(S_n = r_1 + \cdots + r_n\):

\[ S_n = w\sum_{t=1}^{n} r_{t-1} + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \varepsilon_t \]

**Step 2: rewrite the lagged sum.** The sum of \(r_0\) to \(r_{n-1}\) is \(S_n - r_n + r_0\). Substituting and collecting \(S_n\) on the left:

\[ (1 - w)\,S_n = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \varepsilon_t + w\,(r_0 - r_n) \]

**Step 3: drop the small term.** For a stationary process, \(r_0\) and \(r_n\) are single returns of fixed size, while the sum of \(n\) shocks grows with \(n\). Over a long horizon, \(S_n \approx \sum \varepsilon_t / (1 - w)\).

**Step 4: take the standard deviation.** The \(n\) independent shocks sum to a standard deviation of \(\sigma\sqrt{n}\). Dividing by \(1 - w\) gives [eq:spread].
:::

::: worked Three weights over a year of steps
**Step 1: the random walk.** With \(\sigma = 1\%\) and \(n = 250\), \(w = 0\) gives \(1\% \times \sqrt{250} = 15.8\%\).

**Step 2: momentum near +1.** At \(w = 0.9\), the denominator is 0.1 and the spread is \(15.8\%/0.1 = 158\%\), ten times the random walk.

**Step 3: mean reversion near −1.** At \(w = -0.9\), the denominator is 1.9 and the spread is \(15.8\%/1.9 = 8.3\%\), about half the random walk. The paths fan out toward +1 and draw in toward −1.
:::

## Reading a fitted weight

The weight in a fitted AR(1) is interpretable as the first-order **[[autocorrelation]]** of returns, the [[correlation]] between each return and the one before it:

\[ w \approx \text{Corr}(r_t, r_{t-1}) \]

The fitted weight is a regression slope, which is the covariance of \(r_t\) and \(r_{t-1}\) divided by the variance of \(r_{t-1}\) (Chapter 3C derives this). For a stationary series, \(r_t\) and \(r_{t-1}\) have the same variance, so the slope equals the correlation.

A weight of −0.05 means returns are weakly mean-reverting: a 1% up move predicts roughly a 0.05% down move on average. A weight of +0.10 means returns are weakly momentum-driven. The magnitude tells you the predictive content, and the magnitudes in financial markets are *small*. Typical first-order autocorrelations of daily SPX returns lie between −0.05 and +0.05, with most of the absolute value coming from microstructure noise rather than fundamental signal.

::: worked How many days it takes to see an autocorrelation of 0.05
**Step 1: the standard error.** For a series with no true autocorrelation, the estimate from \(n\) returns has a standard error of about \(1/\sqrt{n}\). One year of 250 daily returns gives \(1/\sqrt{250} = 0.063\), larger than the 0.05 you are trying to detect.

**Step 2: a two-standard-error estimate.** For 0.05 to sit two standard errors from zero, you need \(2/\sqrt{n} = 0.05\), so \(n = 1{,}600\) days, about six years of trading.

**Step 3: adequate power.** To detect a true 0.05 with 80% power at a two-sided 5% level, you need \(n = (1.96 + 0.84)^2/0.05^2 = 3{,}136\) days, about 12 years.
:::

That is the sample size calculation you would do before a trial, and it shows why a weight fitted to a year or two of daily data is mostly noise.

### Where the noise comes from: the bid-ask bounce

Much of the small negative autocorrelation in short-horizon returns is manufactured by how prices are recorded. Trades print at the bid or at the ask, so a recorded price jumps between the two even when the value between them has not moved (Chapter 2C).

::: worked A price that bounces without moving
**Step 1: the setup.** Suppose a stock's true value sits at $100.00 with a bid of $99.95 and an ask of $100.05. Each trade prints at one or the other at random, so the recorded price is the true value plus an error \(e_t\) of ±$0.05.

**Step 2: the changes.** The recorded change is \(d_t = e_t - e_{t-1}\). With \(s^2\) the variance of each error, \(\operatorname{Var}(d_t) = 2s^2\). Consecutive changes share one error with opposite signs, so \(\operatorname{Cov}(d_t, d_{t-1}) = -s^2\).

**Step 3: the autocorrelation.** Dividing gives \(-s^2/2s^2 = -0.5\). A price that never moved shows strong apparent mean reversion.

**Step 4: add real moves.** If the true value also moves with variance \(\sigma^2\) a period, the autocorrelation is \(-s^2/(\sigma^2 + 2s^2)\). With a half-spread of 0.05% and a hypothetical true volatility of 0.03% a minute, it is \(-0.0025/(0.0009 + 0.005) = -0.42\). With a daily volatility of 1%, it is \(-0.0025/1.005 = -0.0025\).
:::

The same bounce that dominates minute returns is invisible in daily ones. You cannot trade it either, because capturing it means buying at the bid and selling at the ask, which is the market maker's business, not the taker's.

::: familiar
The bid-ask bounce is regression to the mean from measurement error. Measure a stable tumor marker with independent assay error and chart the changes between visits. A falsely high reading makes one change look like a rise and the next like a fall, so successive changes correlate at exactly −0.5 while the true level never moves. Before you treat a falling marker as a response, or a reversal in prices as an edge, ask how much of it is the instrument.
:::

## The Hurst exponent and the variance ratio test

A more powerful way to detect mean reversion or momentum than just fitting an AR(1) is to compute the **[[Hurst exponent]]** \(H\) of the return series. It measures how variance scales with the time horizon. Over \(k\) periods:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{k} r_t\Big) \propto k^{2H} \]

where \(\propto\) means "proportional to". For a random walk, \(H = 0.5\) and variance scales linearly with time, because \(2H = 1\). For mean-reverting series \(H < 0.5\): variance grows more slowly than linearly, because reversals shrink the cumulative move. For momentum-driven (persistent) series \(H > 0.5\): variance grows faster than linearly, because trends compound.

The related **[[variance ratio test]]** (Lo and MacKinlay, 1988, *Review of Financial Studies*) formalizes this into a statistical test of the random-walk null hypothesis. The variance ratio at lag \(k\) compares the variance of \(k\)-period returns with \(k\) times the one-period variance:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{VR}(k) &= \frac{\operatorname{Var}(r_t + \cdots + r_{t+k-1})}{k \operatorname{Var}(r_t)}
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:vr}

Under the random-walk null, \(\text{VR}(k) = 1\) for all \(k\). A ratio above 1 indicates momentum, and a ratio below 1 mean reversion. It also tells you how wrong the square-root-of-time rule of Chapter 1A is: the true \(k\)-period standard deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{k}\) multiplied by \(\sqrt{\text{VR}(k)}\).

::: worked The variance ratio at lag 2
**Step 1: expand.** For any stationary series with variance \(V\) and lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho_1\), the variance of a two-period sum is \(V + V + 2\rho_1 V\).

**Step 2: divide.** Dividing by \(2V\) gives \(\text{VR}(2) = 1 + \rho_1\). A weight of −0.05 gives 0.95, and a weight of +0.10 gives 1.10.

**Step 3: the Hurst exponent.** If variance scales as \(k^{2H}\), then \(\text{VR}(k) = k^{2H-1}\), so \(H = \tfrac{1}{2} + \ln \text{VR}(k)/(2\ln k)\). At lag 2, a ratio of 0.95 gives \(H = 0.5 - 0.0513/1.386 = 0.46\), and a ratio of 1.10 gives \(H = 0.5 + 0.0953/1.386 = 0.57\).
:::

For an AR(1), the ratio settles at \((1 + w)/(1 - w)\) at long lags. That is 0.905 for \(w = -0.05\) and 1.22 for \(w = +0.10\).

Lo and MacKinlay's 1988 paper found significant momentum in weekly US stock returns, with variance ratios above 1 at lags of 1 to 4 weeks, which challenged the random-walk hypothesis on those horizons. Tools like these are how the academic literature has built robust statistical support for both mean reversion, at very short and very long horizons, and momentum, at intermediate ones. The retail trader who claims "I'm a mean reversion guy" is making a horizon-specific assumption that they may not realize they are making.

## Why AR(1) is the right starting point

You will not get rich on AR(1) alone. The reason to learn it is that it is the most basic non-trivial model that captures the essential question every trading system must answer: do you expect the next move to continue the current move (momentum) or to reverse it (mean reversion)? Every more sophisticated model is a refinement of this question:

- **ARMA models** add moving-average terms, which carry past shocks forward.
- **ARIMA models** handle non-stationarity.
- **GARCH models** add time-varying volatility.
- **Kalman filters** add inference about a hidden state.
- **Hidden Markov models** add switching between discrete regimes.
- **LSTMs and transformers**, two kinds of neural network, add nonlinear interactions across time.

All of them, in their cleanest formulation, answer some version of "given what has happened, what comes next?" The most basic answer is a weighted combination of recent values. Start with AR(1), and add complexity only when the residuals demand it.

## The regime problem

The most important practical issue with AR(1) and its descendants is that **the sign of \(w\) changes over time**. SPX intraday autocorrelation in calm regimes is mildly negative. That is mean reversion, courtesy of positive-gamma dealer hedging, in which dealers sell into rallies and buy into falls (Part VI shows why). In stress regimes, intraday autocorrelation flips positive. That is momentum, courtesy of negative-gamma dealer hedging, which amplifies moves. A static AR(1) fitted to all available data averages across these regimes and produces an estimated \(w\) near zero: neither regime, and not predictive.

::: worked Two regimes that cancel
**Step 1: the regimes.** Suppose 80% of days are calm, with \(w = -0.2\) and return variance \(V\). The other 20% are stressed, with \(w = +0.2\) and a variance of \(4V\).

**Step 2: the pooled slope.** A single regression divides the pooled covariance by the pooled variance. The covariance is \(0.8 \times (-0.2V) + 0.2 \times (0.2 \times 4V) = -0.16V + 0.16V = 0\), so the pooled weight is zero.

**Step 3: the result.** Two real, opposite effects produce a fitted weight of exactly zero, and the model concludes that the market is a random walk.
:::

The fix is **regime-conditional modeling**: fit one AR(1) for calm regimes and another for stress regimes, with a classifier that identifies which regime is currently active. This is the essence of **[[regime-switching models|regime-switching model]]** and the foundation of every serious quant trading framework. The book returns to it in Chapter 8B, where positive and negative [[gamma exposure]] (GEX) serve as the regime indicator, and in Chapter 11G, the composite regime scorecard.

::: familiar
The regime problem is a qualitative interaction. A drug that helps one biomarker-defined subgroup and harms another can show a null effect in the whole trial, just as the pooled weight shows zero. The remedy is the same in both fields. The subgroup, or the regime classifier, has to be defined in advance and tested out of sample, because a classifier tuned until the two fitted weights look different is a subgroup analysis run until one comes out significant.
:::

::: practice
- **Fit the AR(1) on log returns at the horizon you intend to trade**, since the weight at one minute says nothing about the weight at one month.
- **Report the weight with its standard error.** At about \(1/\sqrt{n}\), a year of daily data cannot distinguish a weight of −0.05 from zero.
- **Discount short-horizon mean reversion** for the bid-ask bounce: the part that comes from prints alternating between bid and ask cannot be captured by a trader who crosses the spread.
- **Check the variance ratio at several lags**, and remember that each lag you look at is another comparison.
:::

::: warn When it fails
An AR(1) fitted to one regime fails when the regime changes, and the sign of the weight can flip exactly when the stakes are highest, as stress arrives. A weight near zero may mean either a random walk or two regimes cancelling, and the fit alone cannot tell you which. A short-horizon negative weight may also be the bid-ask bounce, which looks like edge on paper and costs money in practice.
:::

::: rule
- **Trade a weight only if** it is several standard errors from zero out of sample, at the horizon you trade, and large enough that the predicted move exceeds the round-trip cost.
- **Condition on the regime** before trusting any fitted weight on SPX. Fit calm and stress separately, with a regime classifier fixed in advance.
- **If the weight is within noise of zero, treat the market as a random walk** at that horizon and look for a risk premium instead of a pattern.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "I'm a mean reversion trader"
Mean reversion and momentum are properties of a horizon, not of a market or a personality. The same index can revert over minutes, trend over months and revert again over years. Name the horizon, measure the weight or the variance ratio there, and check it again when the regime changes.
:::

::: recap
- The AR(1) model predicts \(\hat{r}_t = w\,r_{t-1} + b\). A negative weight means mean reversion, a positive one momentum, and a weight near zero a random walk.
- Returns are stationary only for \(|w| < 1\). Price paths fan out as \(w\) approaches +1 and draw in as it approaches −1, with a spread of about \(\sigma\sqrt{n}/(1-w)\).
- The fitted weight is the lag-1 autocorrelation. On daily SPX it is small, and its standard error of about \(1/\sqrt{n}\) makes a year of data too little to measure it.
- The variance ratio and the Hurst exponent measure how variance scales with the horizon, and they show that each regime belongs to a horizon.
- The sign of the weight changes between calm and stress, so a pooled fit can show zero where two real effects cancel. Model the regime.
:::

::: check
?? A fitted AR(1) on 1,260 daily returns gives \(w = -0.08\) and \(b = 0\). What does it predict after a 2% rise, and is the weight distinguishable from zero?
!! It predicts \(-0.08 \times 2\% = -0.16\%\). The standard error is about \(1/\sqrt{1{,}260} = 0.028\), so the weight is about 2.9 standard errors from zero. It is distinguishable in sample, but a 0.16% expected move must still beat the round-trip cost, and the weight must survive walk-forward testing.

?? With \(\sigma = 1\%\) a day, what is the standard deviation of a single return when \(w = 0.6\), and of the price path over 100 days?
!! By [eq:var], \(V = 1/(1 - 0.36) = 1.5625\), a standard deviation of 1.25% a day. By [eq:spread], the 100-day spread is about \(1\% \times 10/0.4 = 25\%\), against 10% for a random walk.

?? A two-day variance ratio comes out at 0.90. What lag-1 autocorrelation does that imply, and what regime?
!! Since \(\text{VR}(2) = 1 + \rho_1\), the autocorrelation is −0.10: mean reversion at a one-day horizon.

?? A pooled AR(1) on five years of SPX intraday returns gives a weight of almost exactly zero. Name two different explanations.
!! The market may be close to a random walk at that horizon. Or calm periods with a negative weight and stressed periods with a positive weight, and more variance, may cancel, as in the two-regime example. Fitting the regimes separately distinguishes the two.
:::
