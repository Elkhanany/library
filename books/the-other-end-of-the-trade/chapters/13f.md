## What a setup card is

A **[[setup card]]** compresses one strategy into the form needed for live execution: its edge source, the regime it requires, its size, the rule that ends it and the ways it commonly fails. The cards are designed to be printed, taped next to your monitor or copied into a trading journal. Their value lies in being *binding* at the moment you are tempted to override them.

::: plain
A strategy you understand in a calm evening can still be traded badly at 10 in the morning, when the price is moving and a story is forming in your head. A card moves every decision you can make in advance to the evening. At the screen you check conditions and follow rules, and anything the card does not allow waits until you can think about it calmly.
:::

This chapter merges two sets of cards. The Dealer Mechanics Lab wrote cards for its four swing setups, two risk overlays and seven patterns to avoid. The Edge Book wrote cards for ten allocatable strategies, four regime filters and seven strategies it rejects. Both sets share one principle, **discretion at observation, mechanism at execution.** You use judgment to decide what you are seeing. Once you decide to act, the card constrains how.

::: familiar
A setup card is a treatment protocol. The protocol states the eligibility criteria, the dose, the schedule, the dose modifications for toxicity and the stopping rules, and it is written before any patient is in the room. At the bedside you judge whether the patient meets the criteria. You do not rewrite the dose because this patient seems different. The card's regime row is the eligibility, its sizing rule the dose, its kill rule the stopping rule, and its "skip when" row the contraindications.
:::

Every card in the chapter lights each of its rows green or red, and a column of verdicts reads trade, reduce, halt or skip. It shows how one change in the regime, such as the VIX crossing 22, switches several cards off at once.]

## How to read a card

The two sources use different fields, but the fields answer the same questions. The table sets them side by side.

| The question | Swing setup card | Allocation card |
|---|---|---|
| Why does it pay? | Edge source | Edge source and category |
| How good is the evidence? | Evidence tier, A to C | Evidence tier, T1 to T3 |
| When may you trade it? | Regime | Regime required |
| In what? | Vehicle | Instrument |
| When do you get in? | Entry trigger | Entry |
| How big? | Sizing rule | Sizing cap |
| When do you get out? | Profit target and time stop | Exit |
| What ends it? | Invalidation | Kill rule |
| What goes wrong? | Common failure | Hidden exposure and stress loss |
| When do you stand aside? | Skip when | Not to trade when |
| What should it earn, and how sure are you? | Not on the card | Expected return and confidence |

The two evidence scales differ. The Edge Book's tiers, T1 to T3, run from peer-reviewed, replicated, multi-decade evidence to thin or decayed evidence (Chapter 10A). The Lab's tiers, A to C, grade the kind of source (Chapter 11B). Tier A is peer-reviewed work, replicated over decades, or official exchange data. Tier B is vendor statistics or backtests with credible methods but no peer review, and Tier C is practitioner pattern language that you must formalize yourself.

Swing cards state their targets in units of **R**, the dollar loss you take if the stop is hit. A target of 2R is a gain of twice that, and a trade's [[R-multiple]] is its profit or loss divided by R (Chapter 11H). A [[time stop]] closes the trade after a set number of trading days, whatever its profit or loss.

## Swing setup cards

These four cards come from the Dealer Mechanics Lab and summarize the setups of Chapter 11H. Each is a long trade for a positive regime, held for days, in the [[discretionary sleeve]] of the portfolio.

### Trend pullback and reclaim

| Field | Rule |

### Where the cards and the chapters disagree

The cards summarize Chapter 11H, but in four places they give different numbers from that chapter or from the kill rules of Chapter 13C:

| Setup | The card | Chapter 11H | Chapter 13C's kill rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trend Pullback | Skip above a VIX of 25; evidence Tier A | Requires a VIX below 22 and [[contango]]; evidence Tier B | A close below the entry day's low, or 5 days |
| Compression Breakout | Skip above a VIX of 22; time stop 8 days | Requires a VIX below 18; time stop 8 days | A close back inside the range, or 7 days |
| Undercut and Reclaim | Skip above a VIX of 22 | No VIX condition | A close below the undercut low, or 3 days |
| IBS and RSI Mean Reversion | Time stop 2 days | Requires a VIX below 22; time stop 2 days | A reclaim of the 21-EMA, or 3 days |

Where they differ, take the stricter reading. Chapter 11H's regime conditions define the setup, so a trade that fails one of them is not the setup at all, and the card's skip row adds vetoes on top. For the time stop, the shorter one ends a trade that has not paid within its usual horizon.

::: worked A Trend Pullback in XSP calls
**Step 1: check the card.** The composite reads +42, SPX is in Stage 2 with ADX at 24, GEX is positive, the VIX is 16 in contango, and RSI(2) closed at 7 on a pullback to the 21-EMA. Every regime condition holds on the card and in Chapter 11H, and no skip condition applies.

**Step 2: set the risk.** A $500,000 portfolio risking 1% has a budget of $5,000. XSP is at 600, and the stop below the entry day's low is at 595, $5 away.

**Step 3: size the calls.** You buy XSP calls with a delta of 0.60, so at the stop each loses about \(0.60 \times 5 = \$3.00\), or $300 a contract with the multiplier of 100. For a move this small, delta is a close guide, and for a long call it errs on the safe side, since gamma slows the call's fall. The rule allows \(\lfloor 5{,}000/300 \rfloor = 16\) contracts, which carry \(16 \times 100 \times 0.60 \times 600 = \$576{,}000\) of delta exposure, 115% of the portfolio.

**Step 4: write the exits.** R is \(16 \times 300 = \$4{,}800\). A target of 1.5R needs the calls to gain $4.50 each, about \(4.50/0.60 = 7.50\) points on XSP, so 607.50; 2R needs 610. Close at whichever comes first of the target, a close above the prior 5-day high, a close below the entry day's low, or 5 trading days.
:::

## Risk overlay cards

Two overlay cards from the Dealer Mechanics Lab change how the swing cards are applied around scheduled events.

| Overlay | Active | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| FOMC week | From 2 trading days before the meeting to 1 day after it (T−2 to T+1) | Reduce all new entries to 50% of their normal size, and widen their stops by 50%. Skip any new entry whose widened stop, 1.5 times the normal distance, would put more than twice the normal portfolio risk on the trade. Look for opportunities in the vanna unwind, 60 to 90 minutes after the announcement |
| Earnings | From 2 trading days before the report to 2 days after it (T−2 to T+2), for any position in the reporting company | Close single-stock positions before the report, or convert them to a defined-risk options structure. Long call spreads and long put spreads are permitted; never hold naked shares or naked options through earnings. Size the position so that the implied move would not produce a loss of more than 2% of the portfolio |

The FOMC rules keep the risk of a new entry below its normal level. Half the size with a stop 1.5 times as far puts \(0.5 \times 1.5 = 0.75\) of the normal risk on the trade. The third rule vetoes any setup whose natural stop is already so wide that even this overshoots. The [[vanna]] unwind is dealers buying back hedges as implied volatility collapses after the announcement (Chapters 5D and 7B). The card looks for it 60 to 90 minutes after the announcement. Chapter 11K opens the window earlier, at 30 to 90 minutes, and enters only once the VIX has fallen more than 2 points within 15 minutes. The card and that chapter also differ before the meeting: the card allows new entries at half size, while the worked FOMC week of Chapter 11K takes none on the eve of the release.

::: worked Sizing through an earnings report
**Step 1: the limit.** A $300,000 portfolio may lose at most 2% on the report, $6,000.

**Step 2: the implied move.** The options price a move of 8% for the report, read from the at-the-money straddle (Chapter 11A).

**Step 3: the size.** A move of 8% costs $6,000 on a position of \(6{,}000/0.08 = \$75{,}000\), so that is the largest position you may hold through the report, 25% of the portfolio. Held as a long call spread, the most you can lose is the spread's cost, so size it so that its cost is no more than $6,000.
:::

The figure applies the overlay's rules, halving the size, widening the stop, testing the veto and capping the position so that the implied move costs no more than 2% of the portfolio, and draws the position before and after. It shows how much smaller a trade becomes inside an event window.]

### Card 01: long equity

| Field | Rule |

| Edge source | Risk premium, Source 1 of Chapter 1G |
| Category | Foundation premium |
| Evidence tier | T1: Mehra and Prescott; Dimson, Marsh and Staunton, across 35 markets |

| Hidden exposure | A [[crash beta|tail beta]] of 1.0 by definition. A negative real return over several decades is possible, as in Japan from 1989 to 2010 |
| Stress loss | S1: −10% of the allocation. S2: −20%. S3: −35%. S4, a 1987-style gap: −22% overnight |
| Sizing cap | 50% to 65% of the portfolio |
| Kill rule | When 1/CAPE minus the 10-year Treasury yield stays below −1% and 12-month earnings growth is negative, reduce by 25%, not a full exit |
| Confidence | High |
| Not to trade when | You cannot tolerate a drawdown of 35% to 50%, or you need the capital within 5 years |

Dollar-cost averaging means buying a fixed amount at regular intervals rather than all at once. The kill rule compares the equity market's cyclically adjusted [[earnings yield]], one over the [[CAPE ratio]], with the yield on 10-year Treasuries (Chapter 10B). Suppose, hypothetically, that the CAPE ratio is 32 and the 10-year yield 4.5%. The earnings yield is \(1/32 = 3.1\%\), and the gap is \(3.1\% - 4.5\% = -1.4\%\), below −1%. If that gap persists and earnings growth over 12 months turns negative, a $550,000 equity sleeve is cut by 25%, to $412,500. Either condition alone does nothing.

::: worked Checking the card's S1 loss
**Step 1: the floor.** In S1 the index falls 10%, so XSP goes from 580 to 522 and the put is $58 in the money. A premium of 2% to 3% of the strike is $11.60 to $17.40. The put is worth at least its $58 of [[intrinsic value]], so the loss is at least \(58 - 17.40 = \$40.60\), or $4,060 a contract, and up to $4,640 at the lower premium.

**Step 2: where $3,500 comes from.** A first-order estimate adds delta and vega. Delta, about −0.5, gives \(0.5 \times 58 \times 100 = \$2{,}900\). A one-month at-the-money put on 580 has a vega of about \(0.4 \times 580 \times 0.289/100 = 0.67\) per volatility point, so the VIX's rise of 10 points adds about $670. The total, about $3,570, is close to the card's figure. It leaves out gamma, which is the lesson of Chapter 13E: price a large fall from the option's value at the stressed level.

**Step 3: the count.** With \(B_\text{exp}\) at 5% of a $1 million portfolio, $50,000, and \(L_\text{max}\) the S2 loss of $10,000, the cap is \(50{,}000/10{,}000 = 5\) contracts. Chapter 13C rounds down to 4 for a buffer, and Chapter 13E's Example 10, with $10,800 a contract, also reaches 4.
:::

Read the card's net return of 3% to 5% a year as a return on the collateral, as Chapter 13E's Example 2 reads its own 2% to 4%. The card's first kill step, halting new entries above 1.00, starts earlier than the single 1.05 trigger of that example.

| Exit | Hold, and reduce slightly in liquidity stress |
| Expected return | A live retail Sharpe ratio of about 0.25 to 0.40, with lower drawdowns than SPX |
| Hidden exposure | An equity beta still of about 0.6. It underperforms in high-beta rallies, as in 2020 and 2021 |
| Stress loss | Not given on the card |
| Sizing cap | 3% to 8% of the equity sleeve |

On a $550,000 sleeve, 19% to 38% is $104,500 to $209,000 in factor funds, with the rest in the broad index.

### Card 10: end-of-quarter rebalancing

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Edge source | Institutional, Source 3: flow driven by mandates |
| Category | Structural overlay |
| Evidence tier | T2: Etula and colleagues (2020); NBER Working Paper 33554 |
| Regime required | A divergence of more than 1.5% between SPX and bonds during the quarter |
| Instrument | ES and ZN futures, taxed under Section 1256 |
| Entry | Direction: if stocks outperformed bonds, short ES and long ZN; if stocks underperformed, long ES and short ZN. This is the sign correction from the first version of the framework, so do not reverse it. Enter 5 trading days before the quarter ends |
| Exit | 1 to 3 trading days into the new quarter |
| Expected return | About 17 bps of next-day equity effect at an extreme divergence. Untested live for retail |
| Hidden exposure | A recent paper, with uncertain retail replication |
| Stress loss | Not given on the card |
| Sizing cap | 0.5% of the portfolio at risk per leg for the first 8 quarters |
| Kill rule | After 4 quarters, a hit rate below 50% or a negative P&L: suspend. A quarterly divergence below 1.5%: skip the trade |
| Confidence | Low to medium: T2, and replication is needed |

The direction rules of the end-of-quarter (EOQ) trade are called out because an earlier version of the framework had the sign backward, and a reviewer caught it (Chapter 10G). A reversed sign does not merely fail to earn: it takes the side of the flow it meant to front-run. The card keeps the size small for 8 quarters, while Chapter 13E's Example 6 lets it grow after 4 good ones.

## Filter cards

A filter card is not a strategy. It holds no capital, and it sizes the cards above up or down, as Chapter 10H explains. The four filters are the Edge Book's.

### Card F1: the VIX term structure

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Filter | The ratio of the VIX to the VIX3M |
| Use | Modulate the size of the short volatility sleeve |
| Rule, layered | Above 1.00 for 3 closes: halt new short volatility entries. Above 1.05 for 3 closes: halt all put sales and reduce existing positions by 50%. Above 1.15 for 3 closes: close non-essential short volatility. Below 1.00 for 5 closes: re-enter in phases, 25%, 50% and 100% over 3 weeks |
| Do not | Buy VXX or VIX futures because of this filter |

### Card F2: realized against implied volatility

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Filter | 21-day realized volatility against current implied volatility |
| Use | Confirm the short volatility regime |
| Rule | Realized below implied: the regime is acceptable for selling premium. Realized above implied: the VRP is negative, so halt new entries. Realized above implied plus 5 volatility points: close existing positions if they are oversized |
| Do not | Treat it as a standalone signal. Always combine it with the term structure |

### Card F3: the liquidity composite

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Filter | The TED spread, the MOVE index and the high-yield [[option-adjusted spread]] (HY OAS), combined |
| Use | Reduce all leveraged exposure |
| Rule | The TED spread above 200 bps, the MOVE above 150, or HY OAS above 600 bps: reduce all leveraged exposure by 50% and halt new positions |
| Normalization | Wait for a sustained recovery before re-entering |

### Card F4: the sign of GEX, at low confidence

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Filter | The direction of gamma exposure (GEX), positive or negative |
| Use | Soft regime context only |
| Rule | Negative GEX: expect higher realized volatility, and trim put-writing by 10% to 15%. Positive GEX: expect compressed volatility, and size normally |
| Do not | Trade specific GEX levels, such as a [[call wall]] or a [[put wall]], or size positions by the magnitude of GEX |
| Note | A Cboe study of 2025 puts 0DTE market-maker (MM) hedging at only about 0.2% of liquidity. Use the sign as a supplementary signal only |

Chapter 10H states two of these rules a little differently. Its liquidity rule also cuts short volatility by 50% and equity beta by 25%. It gives the sign of GEX only 10% to 15% of one filter's weight, and ignores it when it disagrees with the other filters.

## Do-not-trade cards

These cards exist to inoculate you against narrative-driven retail bait. Both sources wrote them, and where both rejected the same strategy, the two cards are merged here. The Edge Book's cards are X1 to X7, and the Dealer Mechanics Lab's three others follow as X8 to X10.

### Card X1: the drift before FOMC announcements

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Why you might be tempted | Lucca and Moench (2015) documented an average return of 49 bps before FOMC announcements, from 1994 to 2011 |
| Why it is dead | Kurov, Halova, Wolfe and Gilbert (2021) found the effect disappeared after 2015. The cause was the Fed's transparency reforms: the dot plot in 2014 and forward guidance |
| Lesson | Edges with a clear mechanism can decay suddenly when policy responds, or when the institutional context beneath them changes |
| Verdict | Do not trade |

The [[pre-FOMC drift]] is Chapter 10I's first decayed edge. The Lab's card rejects it too, with the same two papers.

### Card X2: S&P 500 inclusion

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Why you might be tempted | A historical inclusion premium of 7% or more (Shleifer 1986) |
| Why it is dead | Greenwood and Sammon (2024) find it under 1% after 2010. Index funds got smarter, and arbitrageurs ate the obvious mispricing |
| Lesson | Sophisticated capital can eat an institutional edge |
| Verdict | Do not trade |

### Card X3: naive earnings short volatility on single names

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Why you might be tempted | The [[IV crush]] after earnings takes 30 volatility points or more, and premiums look "rich" 1 to 2 days before |
| Why it is wrong | Adams and Neururer (2020) find a *negative* mean despite a positive median at retail size. The structural negative skew of a single stock's earnings exceeds the premium collected: the skew tax exceeds the premium harvested |
| Institutional variant | The cross-sectional version of Cao, Han, Tong and Zhan (2022), across 200 or more names, works. A retail account cannot replicate its infrastructure |
| Lesson | A win rate is not an expected value (EV). Structural negative skew dominates at single-name retail size |
| Verdict | Do not trade it systematically. Acceptable: a small [[calendar spread]] on a rare opportunity |

Chapter 13E's Example 8 works this card through an NVDA report, where the expected value comes out at about −$4 per option sold.

### Card X4: scalping GEX levels

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Why you might be tempted | Public GEX models show specific call walls and put walls, with a compelling story about dealer hedging |
| Why it is wrong | Public GEX models depend on their assumptions, and vendors disagree. The explosion of [[0DTE]] options after 2022 changed the structure. A Cboe study of 2025 puts 0DTE market-maker hedging at about 0.2% of liquidity: of SPX liquidity on the Edge Book's card, of futures liquidity on the Lab's |
| Lesson | A compelling narrative is not evidence quality |
| Verdict | Do not trade specific levels. Acceptable: the directional sign, as low-confidence regime context |

Both traditions reject the scalp, as Chapters 1G, 10A and 10I note. They differ on how much weight the levels deserve for judging a trade's path, and the Lab's other chapters give them more (Chapters 8A and 11A).

### Card X5: systematic iron condors

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Why you might be tempted | A "defined risk" income strategy in markets that are usually range-bound, with theta accumulating every day |
| Why it is wrong | The CBOE [[CNDR index]] returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024, against about 5,500% for the SPX total return. That underperformance over 38 years is decisive. Wing breaks dominate the aggregate P&L |
| Lesson | A defined maximum loss per trade is not a defined aggregate loss |
| Verdict | Do not trade systematically. Acceptable: a small, discretionary condor on an occasional opportunity at extreme implied volatility |

The two totals are compound rates of about 5.5% and 11% a year (Chapter 10I). Chapter 12C teaches the [[iron condor]] as a structure chosen for a regime, and the Lab's own card agrees that the systematic version fails.

### Card X6: a put spread funding a LEAP

| Field | Content |
|---|---|

| Verdict | Do not trade either side |

### Card X8: buying the Friday rip into Monday

## How to use the cards

Both sources give the same five steps, and the Edge Book adds detail to two of them:

1. **Print or copy** the cards relevant to your portfolio.
2. **Refer to them before trading,** and verify every condition on the card.
3. **Update them when the regime changes,** marking each card "halted" or "active" with the date of the change.
4. **Review them quarterly,** confirming that no kill rule has triggered.
5. **Recalibrate them once a year** against the latest evidence.

The cards are deliberately rigid, and their value lies in being binding when you are tempted to override them.

::: practice
Keep the cards where you place orders, not in a folder. Mark each allocatable card with its current status and the date you last checked its kill rule, and keep the do-not-trade cards in the same place. The temptation they answer arrives as a new story, not as the old name. When a pitch reaches you, find its card before you read the pitch a second time, since most retail trading content promotes the strategies these cards reject (Chapter 10I). Record every card you overrode in your journal (Chapter 13H), with the reason, and review those entries first each quarter.
:::

## Rigidity and judgment

Some readers object that trading requires judgment, not rigid rules. Trading at the level of a *signal* does require interpretation, but trading at the level of a *strategy* requires written rules. The card format formalizes the strategy level. Within each card, you exercise discretion about whether its conditions apply right now, but the cards themselves are not for daily revision.

The balance is discretion at observation and mechanism at execution. When you see something interesting, you observe and interpret it, which is the observation step of Chapter 13C's pipeline. When you decide to act, the cards constrain how. This is what disciplined trading looks like in operation.

## The whole framework on one card

The Dealer Mechanics Lab closes its discipline section with the book in one paragraph. Classify any new "edge" by its source: risk premium, behavioral, institutional or microstructure. Score its conviction, \(E \times M \times R \times X \times S\), and apply the four-layer retail haircut. Stress-test it through S1 to S4, specify its kill criterion at all three levels, and then size for survivability with \(n \le \lfloor B_\text{exp}/L_\text{max} \rfloor\).

If you do this consistently, you filter out about 80% of retail trading content as low-conviction or rejected. The remaining 20% gets sized to anchor a portfolio that lasts decades. The framework's success depends not on finding rare edges but on **operating consistently with the edges that are available**. Most retail traders have access to the same setups, the same indicators and the same information, so the differences in outcomes come from differences in execution discipline. Everything else in this book, in the Lab's words, was the context that justifies the discipline.

::: warn When it fails
A card is only as current as its last review. A regime can change the meaning of a rule before anyone rewrites it, as the hiking cycle of 2022 may have done to the FOMC cycle. An edge can decay while its card still reads "active", as the drift before FOMC announcements did after 2015. The rigidity that protects you from impulse also carries a stale rule forward, which is why the cards have a quarterly review and an annual recalibration.
:::

::: rule
- **Trade only what has a card,** and only when every regime condition on it holds.
- **Where two cards or chapters disagree,** take the stricter number until you have reviewed the evidence.
- **Size from the card's rule,** never from how good the setup looks.
- **Follow the kill rule** the moment it fires, and change a card only at a scheduled review, never during a trade.
- **Before any new strategy,** look for it among X1 to X10.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: revising a card during a trade
Loosening a skip condition because today's setup looks unusually clean, or widening a stop because the price is close to it, turns the card back into an opinion. The second is the [[stop migration]] of Chapter 11N. Changes belong to the quarterly review, made in calm conditions and written down. A card edited mid-trade protects nothing.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading a do-not-trade card as a list of names
The cards reject structures, not tickers. A condor sold on QQQ instead of SPX, or earnings premium sold through a new ETF, is the same strategy under a new name. Match a pitch to a card by what it does, sells or depends on, not by what it is called.
:::

::: recap
- A setup card fixes a strategy's edge, regime, vehicle, entry, size, exits and kill rule in advance, so that nothing is decided at the screen.
- The Lab's four swing cards summarize Chapter 11H; where a card and that chapter differ, take the stricter number.
- The Edge Book's ten allocation cards cover the foundation premia, four factor tilts and two overlays, each with its stress loss and kill rule.
- Filter cards hold no capital and size the other cards, and the ten do-not-trade cards answer the pitches you meet most often.
- Discretion belongs to observation, and mechanism to execution. Cards change only at scheduled reviews.
:::

?? Your readings: VIX/VIX3M at 1.03 for 4 closes, 21-day realized volatility of 15% against implied of 19%, the TED spread at 40 bps and the MOVE at 110. Which of Cards 02, 03 and 04 allow new positions?
!! Card 02 does not. Its regime requires VIX/VIX3M below 1.00, and F1 halts new short volatility entries above 1.00 for 3 closes. The ratio has not reached 1.05, so existing puts are not cut. Card 03 has no regime requirement, so trend allocations proceed. Card 04 requires the TED spread below 200 bps and the MOVE below 150, and both hold, so carry may add positions.

?? A friend shows you a year of monthly iron condors that won 11 times out of 12. Which card applies, and what evidence overrides the temptation?
!! Card X5. A high win rate is exactly the pattern of a defined-risk short volatility strategy: small wins, occasional wing breaks. The CNDR index returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024 against about 5,500% for the SPX total return, 38 years of underperformance. Defined loss per trade is not defined loss in aggregate.

?? What is the difference between a setup card and a filter card?
!! A setup card describes a strategy you allocate capital to: its edge, entry, size, exits and kill rule. A filter card describes a measurement that holds no capital and sizes other strategies up or down, such as the VIX term structure. Trading a filter directly, for example buying VXX because the VIX is in contango, is the classic error the filter cards warn against.

?? What role does the kill rule play on each card?
!! It is the pre-committed answer to "what would prove me wrong?", written before the trade in calm conditions. On a swing card it is the invalidation and the time stop. On an allocation card it names a measurable trigger, such as a VIX/VIX3M threshold, a rolling Sharpe ratio or a tracking error, and the action the trigger forces. Its value is that it binds when your judgment is least reliable.

?? Why are Card 10's direction rules called out so prominently?
!! An earlier version of the framework had the sign backward. If stocks outperformed bonds, rebalancers sell stocks and buy bonds, so the front-runner goes short ES and long ZN. A reversed sign takes the opposite side of the very flow the trade exists to anticipate, so the error turns a small expected gain into a small expected loss.

?? During FOMC week, your normal Trend Pullback risks 1% of a $400,000 portfolio with a stop $4 below entry on SPY. What size and stop does the overlay allow?
!! Normal size is \(\lfloor 4{,}000/4 \rfloor = 1{,}000\) shares. The overlay halves it to 500 shares and widens the stop by 50%, to $6. The risk becomes \(500 \times 6 = \$3{,}000\), 0.75% of the portfolio, below twice the normal risk, so the entry is allowed.
