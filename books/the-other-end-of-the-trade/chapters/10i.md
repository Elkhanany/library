A textbook should teach negative space. Most retail trading content focuses on the strategies in this chapter, because they are vivid, easy to backtest and frequently traded, yet they do not survive an evaluation by [[evidence tier]]. ::: plain
Some strategies used to work and stopped. Some never worked as well as their story said. Some work, but only for firms with infrastructure a retail account does not have. When you meet a new strategy, try to file it in one of those three drawers before you put money behind it. If it fits one, you already know how it ends.
:::

## Three reasons to reject a strategy

There are three reasons to reject a candidate strategy:

1. **Decayed.** The edge was real but has substantially compressed, so trading the historical effect now produces near-zero or negative net returns.
2. **Never robust.** The "edge" was always thin or specific to a methodology. The narrative is appealing, but the evidence is weak.
3. **Institutional-only.** The edge is real but requires infrastructure unavailable to retail: data feeds, low-latency execution, cross-margining or large capital.

The pattern matters more than the list. When you meet a *new* strategy that is not actually allocatable, you should be able to file it in one of these three categories. Each is a different way for the net expected value of Chapter 1C to fail. Write a strategy's expected result per trade as its gross edge less its costs. A decayed edge has lost its gross edge, and a strategy that was never robust never had one that survives honest testing. An institutional-only edge keeps its gross edge, but only an institution's costs are low enough to leave it positive.

::: familiar
Medicine has the same three drawers. Some treatments lose their benefit once practice changes around them, so their old trial results no longer apply. Some biomarkers never validate beyond the cohort that discovered them. And some procedures do well mainly in high-volume centers, with the teams and equipment that a small hospital lacks. A decayed edge, a never-robust edge and an institutional-only edge are those three failures in a market.
:::

## Decayed: the drift before FOMC announcements

**The historical edge.** Lucca and Moench (2015, *Journal of Finance*, JF) found that from 1994 to 2011, equities earned about 49 bps in the 24 hours before scheduled FOMC announcements. The effect was concentrated in the stocks with the highest beta: high-beta stocks gained 80 bps or more, and low-beta stocks close to nothing. The mechanism was that uncertainty before the announcement led to risk-off positioning, which reversed as the announcement approached.

With about eight scheduled meetings a year, 49 bps each would add up to about \(8 \times 0.49\% = 3.9\%\) a year from eight days in the market. That arithmetic is why the **[[pre-FOMC drift]]** drew so much attention.

**Why it is rejected.** Kurov, Halova, Wolfe and Gilbert (2021, *JFEcon*) documented that the effect *disappeared* after 2015, because of the Fed's transparency reforms. They name the introduction in 2014 of the "dot plot" projections, the chart of each policymaker's forecast for rates, and the practice of **[[forward guidance]]**. Investors received more information between meetings, which reduced the premium for uncertainty on the day of the announcement. The realized drift from 2015 to 2024 was near zero.

**The lesson.** The decay risk of institutional and structural overlays is real, and it can be sudden. The mechanism has to persist for the edge to persist. When the mechanism changes, as the Fed's transparency practices did, the edge can vanish even with no rise in retail awareness.

::: rule
Do not allocate to the drift before FOMC announcements: use it as awareness only.
:::

The reader drags the boundary between eras and sees the average fall to near zero after 2015 while the scatter of single meetings stays wide. It shows a real effect ending when its mechanism changed, which no single meeting could have revealed.]

The Dealer Mechanics Lab's own do-not-trade cards reject it too (Chapter 13F). The FOMC trade the Lab does teach comes *after* the announcement, as dealers unwind their hedges (Chapters 7B and 11K), and Chapter 10G sets it beside the Edge Book's view.

## Decayed: the S&P 500 inclusion premium

**The historical edge.** Stocks added to the S&P 500 historically rallied about 7% between the announcement and the effective date (Shleifer 1986; Harris-Gurel 1986). The mechanism was that index funds were forced buyers, and arbitrageurs front-ran the flow of their buying.

**Why it is rejected.** Greenwood and Sammon (2024, JF) documented that the **[[S&P 500 inclusion effect]]** has since fallen below 1%, for three reasons:

- Index funds have become smarter about positioning before the effective date.
- Inclusion announcements are integrated into the market's broader awareness.
- Active arbitrageurs have largely eliminated the obvious mispricing.

The 7% effect of the 1990s has shrunk to 50 to 100 bps, which is arguably statistically insignificant.

::: worked Where the 7% went
**Step 1: the old window.** Suppose a stock trades at $100 when its inclusion is announced. In the 1990s it drifted up to about $107 by the effective date, and anyone who bought at the announcement collected most of that.

**Step 2: the crowded window.** Once enough arbitrageurs buy at the announcement, the price jumps at once, and little is left to collect afterward. On the later figures, a buyer after the announcement collects 50 cents to $1 on the same $100, against the risk that the stock falls over the waiting period.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: An inclusion event, then and later. Two illustrative paths of an added stock's cumulative return from announcement to effective date: one climbing about 7% across the window, one jumping at the announcement and then drifting under 1%. The reader moves the day of entry, and a readout gives what remains to collect. It shows an edge moving to the first second after the news, where only capital that acts within seconds can take it.]

**The lesson.** Edges with a clear mechanism can decay almost completely when capital exploits them efficiently. An institutional edge has a half-life that depends on how discoverable the underlying flow is.

::: rule
Do not trade S&P 500 inclusion plays. Even if you correctly anticipate an inclusion, the drift that remains is too small to compensate for the risk.
:::

No chapter of the Dealer Mechanics Lab teaches this trade. Chapter 2A describes the index's rebalances, and Chapter 1G uses the inclusion effect's decline to show how institutional edges end.

## Never robust at retail: naive earnings short volatility

**The narrative.** Implied volatility typically rises into a company's earnings report, as traders price the uncertainty of the announcement, and then falls sharply after it. That [[IV crush]] seems to open a window for selling premium, and many retail traders commit significant capital to selling volatility systematically around earnings.

**Why it is rejected for retail.** The key paper is the Adams-Neururer study: Adams and Neururer (2020), "Volatility Risk Premium and Stock Returns Around Earnings Announcements", in the *Review of Financial Studies* (RFS). Its finding is that for single names, at a size a retail account can trade, **naive short volatility around earnings has a negative mean despite a positive median.** Most of the time you make a small amount on the IV crush. Occasionally you lose a large amount, when the move exceeds what was expected: a revenue miss, a guidance cut or a surprise warning. The rare large losses dominate the frequent small gains.

If a trade wins an average of \(g\) with probability \(p\) and loses an average of \(L\) otherwise, its expected result is

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = p\,g - (1 - p)\,L \]
{#eq:ev}

while its median is a gain whenever \(p\) is above one half. The two can have opposite signs, which is the whole story of this strategy.

::: worked A positive median and a negative mean
**Step 1: the outcomes.** Suppose, hypothetically, you sell a straddle before each of many reports. In 80% of them the stock moves less than the straddle priced, and you keep $6 on average. In 20% it gaps well beyond, and you lose $30 on average.

**Step 2: median and mean.** The median trade is a $6 gain, since most trades win. By [eq:ev] the mean is \(0.8 \times 6 - 0.2 \times 30 = 4.8 - 6.0 = -\$1.20\) a report. The strategy wins four times in five and loses money.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: A straddle seller's results by report. A histogram of P&L per earnings report shows a tall bar of small gains and a thin tail of large losses, with the median and the mean marked. The reader sets the share of reports that gap beyond the priced move and the size of those gaps, and watches the mean cross below zero while the median stays positive.]

This is the structural shape of Chapters 1B and 1D, applied to one company at a time. Short volatility is short skew, and for a single name at retail size the skew tax is too large for the premium to cover.

**Why it works for institutions.** Cao, Han, Tong and Zhan (2022, RFS) document a profitable institutional version: portfolios that sell volatility across 200 or more names, built on the dispersion between them, with significant compensation on the short leg. It requires:

- The capacity to hold positions in 200 or more names.
- Cross-margining, so that positions offset one another across the book.
- Infrastructure to manage risk.
- Sophisticated analytics of the implied volatility surface.

Retail accounts cannot replicate this at scale. A single-name version does not capture the cross-sectional benefit; it takes only the negative-mean exposure. Diversification alone would not fix that. Two hundred bets with a mean of −$1.20 lose about $240 a round, only more steadily. The institutional version earns because it is built differently, and its breadth keeps one blowup from sinking the book.

**The least-bad retail variant: calendar spreads on liquid names.** A [[calendar spread]] that is long a longer-dated option and short a shorter-dated one sells the inflated front-month volatility while keeping exposure further out. It is defined-risk, its downside on a miss is limited, and it captures a moderate part of the IV crush. Even this is a small edge, better used for occasional small bets than as a sleeve.

::: warn Common mistake: "selling premium on every earnings report captures the average IV crush"
Adams and Neururer (2020) directly contradict this: for single names at retail size, the average is *negative*. The feeling that you "win more often than you lose" is the textbook negative-skew pattern in action.
:::

::: rule
- Do not run a systematic sleeve of single-name earnings short volatility.
- If you trade individual names around earnings, prefer calendar spreads to short premium.
- Use the earnings calendar to *avoid* being caught long premium that decays into a report.
:::

The Dealer Mechanics Lab agrees. Its do-not-trade cards reject single-name earnings short volatility on the same paper (Chapter 13F). Its pre-earnings momentum trade closes before the report (Chapter 11I), and its selection matrix meets an earnings week with a diagonal spread rather than short premium (Chapter 12A).

## Never robust: scalping gamma-exposure levels

**The narrative.** Public GEX models, such as those of SqueezeMetrics and SpotGamma, identify "call walls" and "put walls": strikes where dealer [[gamma exposure]] is concentrated. Retail traders sell at the [[call wall]] and buy at the [[put wall]], expecting dealer hedging to reverse moves at those levels.

**Why it is rejected.** Three reasons:

1. **Public GEX models are model-dependent.** Vendors give different numbers because they make different assumptions about who is long and who is short which strikes. The "ground truth" position cannot be known from public data.
2. **The explosion of [[0DTE]] options after 2022 changed the structure.** Models built before 2022 assumed that monthly and weekly options dominated. With more than 40% of SPX volume in 0DTE options, on the Edge Book's count, models built for the old structure need not reflect dealer positioning.
3. **A Cboe study of 2025 estimates 0DTE market-maker hedging at about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity.** That directly contradicts the narrative that dealer flows dominate the day. If hedging is 0.2% of liquidity, the level effects it creates are small relative to other drivers. The study is Mandy Xu's "0DTEs Decoded" (May 2, 2025). Because it measures same-day hedging only, reading it against walls built mostly from longer-dated open interest extends the measurement.

**What the academic literature does support.** Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009, RFS) established that *demand pressure* on options affects their prices, the theoretical basis for thinking that dealer positioning matters. But the effect operates at the level of *option prices*, not the price of the underlying. Its magnitudes are small per unit of demand, and specific rules for trading levels are not validated in the academic literature.

**The valid use** is the directional sign of GEX, as low-confidence regime context (Chapter 10H). Negative-GEX days tend to have higher realized volatility, and positive-GEX days compressed realized volatility. Use the sign for sizing, not for trading levels. Chapter 13B scores level scalping with the conviction score and gets 0.005, effectively zero.

::: warn Common mistake: "GEX is the most important new thing in trading, and reading dealer positioning gives me an edge"
Most of the GEX narrative is storytelling after the fact, around price moves that had already happened. The underlying data, open interest by strike, are real. The inferences drawn from them, dealers' net positioning and their predicted hedging flows, require assumptions that public models do not validate well. The Cboe study of 2025 says directly that the magnitude of same-day hedging is small. The narrative is more compelling than the evidence.
:::

::: rule
Do not trade GEX levels. Use the directional sign as low-confidence regime context only.
:::

The reader steps through the day and places scalping orders at the walls. It shows the walls drifting and disagreeing, and orders resting at levels that no longer hold anything.]

### What the Dealer Mechanics Lab chapters claim

The Lab builds the walls and the [[gamma flip]] from open interest (Chapter 8C), and it reads them as more than context. Its dashboard treats the largest call wall above spot as potential resistance and a magnet for pinning, and the largest put wall below as potential support. It calls spot within 0.5% of the flip a transitional regime (Chapter 11A). Its conviction score rewards a long thesis whose target sits well below the call wall and marks down one with a call wall directly overhead (Chapter 13B). With positive GEX, it expects range trading to win and breakouts to fail (Chapter 8D).

The Lab also measures how small the effects are. It reports pinning of about 16.5 bps on expiration days, and calls published GEX "a useful prior on typical days". It also reports gamma effects of about 16 bps per standard deviation, a figure that appears in no version of the paper it cites: Barbon and Buraschi give about 12 bp for single stocks and more than 20 bp for indexes. Its own do-not-trade cards reject scalping level by level, and they use the sign as a regime indicator (Chapter 13F). So both traditions reject the scalp. They differ on whether the levels deserve any weight at all. The Lab uses them to judge a trade's path and targets, while the Edge Book lists open-interest concentration as descriptive, not predictive, and keeps only the sign.

The pinning figure needs its scope. It is Ni, Pearson and Poteshman's (2005) lower bound for optionable single stocks from 1996 to 2002, not an index figure. For the index, Golez and Jackwerth (2012) found S&P 500 futures pulled toward the at-the-money strike when options on them expired, shifting at least $115 million of notional per expiration day.

## Never robust: systematic iron condors

**The narrative.** An [[iron condor]] is "defined-risk": it sells an out-of-the-money (OTM) call spread and an OTM put spread, which caps the loss on each side, and it profits in range-bound markets. Markets are usually range-bound, so condors usually win, and many retail "income" strategies are built on them.

**Why it is rejected.** **The Cboe [[CNDR index]] returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024, while the SPX Total Return index (SPX TR) returned about 5,500% over the same period.** That is an empirical rejection spanning decades: systematic condors *underperform massively* over long horizons.

A total return turns into a compound annual rate \(c\) through the growth factor of Chapter 1A. If a value grows from \(V_0\) to \(V_T\) over \(T\) years,

\[ c = \left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1 \]
{#eq:cagr}

::: worked What 690% against 5,500% means
**Step 1: terminal wealth.** On $10,000 invested in 1986, a return of 690% ends at $79,000 and a return of 5,500% at $560,000.

**Step 2: compound rates.** Over the 38.5 years from mid-1986 to the end of 2024, [eq:cagr] gives \(7.9^{1/38.5} - 1 = 5.5\%\) a year for the condor index and \(56^{1/38.5} - 1 = 11.0\%\) for the index it is written on.
:::

**Why condors fail.**

- Each leg has negative skew, and combining two short legs does not offset the skew: it adds to it.
- Condors are vulnerable to *one-sided breaks*. When SPX trends, one wing gets blown through and the "defined" maximum loss triggers.
- Tail risk is asymmetric, since both wings can be tested, in different scenarios.
- Theta accumulates slowly, and a few wing breaks a year erase a year of premium.

::: worked Twelve condors, two breaks
**Step 1: the year.** Suppose, hypothetically, you sell one condor a month for a credit of $150, with wings $10 wide, so each can lose at most \(\$1{,}000 - \$150 = \$850\).

**Step 2: the result.** Ten expire inside the short strikes and earn $1,500 in total. Two see a wing blown through and lose $1,700. The year loses $200 on a win rate of 83%.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: A condor book over a decade. Monthly condors on a synthetic index path, with the credit, the wing width and the distance of the short strikes set by the reader, accumulate a P&L beside buy-and-hold and a monthly at-the-money put-write. Wing breaks are marked where each erases months of premium. It shows a high win rate beside a lagging total.]

**The deeper lesson.** "Defined risk" is an operational property, in that you know your maximum loss, but it is not a property of the P&L. Systematic condors with explicit caps on their losses still underperform, because wing breaks happen often enough to outweigh the theta collected.

::: warn Common mistake: "defined-risk income strategies are safer than naked premium selling"
A defined maximum loss per trade is *not* a defined maximum loss in aggregate. The CNDR index's empirical underperformance is overwhelming evidence that systematic condors do not work. The "safety" of defined risk is illusory if the aggregate path includes enough wing breaks to dominate the wins.
:::

::: rule
Do not run systematic iron condor strategies: the empirical evidence is decisive. As a caveat, a discretionary condor at an occasional opportunity can sometimes work, for example when implied volatility is extreme relative to the expected move and the structure is very specific. Systematic, mechanical condor sleeves are empirically rejected.
:::

### What the Dealer Mechanics Lab chapters claim

The Lab teaches the condor as an intermediate neutral structure, a pure short-volatility bet (Chapter 12C). It is best when realized volatility is expected to come in meaningfully below implied, and worst when the index breaks out. It reports the CNDR index's underperformance itself, over the index's history from a backtest starting in 1986 and live data from 2007, and it draws the index against SPX with illustrative, synthetic series. It reads the lesson as specific to the condor's structure, since the PUT index's systematic put-writing has competed well on risk-adjusted terms (Bondarenko 2019). Chapter 5E, from the same tradition, counts condor writing among the short-premium strategies with positive long-run returns. Its strategy matrix picks a defined-risk condor when implied volatility is rich and the market is directionless, and sizes it at 0.25% of the portfolio at risk rather than 1% (Chapter 12A).

The two traditions therefore agree on more than they seem to. The Lab's own do-not-trade cards reject systematic condors on the same CNDR record (Chapter 13F), and the Edge Book allows the occasional discretionary condor. They differ in emphasis: the Lab teaches the condor as a regular tool, chosen for a regime and sized small, while the Edge Book treats it as an exception. The open question, as Chapter 10A puts it, is whether a condor chosen for the regime escapes the record of the mechanical index.

## Leverage with extra steps: the put spread and LEAPS call

**The narrative.** "Sell 1-year SPX [[put spreads|put spread]] to fund LEAP calls. The premium pays for the LEAP exposure. [[Portfolio margin]] makes this efficient. The result is a leveraged equity exposure with positive edge from VRP harvest." This is a popular framing in retail content, especially among traders with portfolio margin accounts. A LEAP is a long-dated listed option, one of the [[LEAPS]].

**Why it is rejected.** The structure is *economically equivalent* to about 1.4 times SPX exposure, depending on the specific deltas, with worse path properties. Chapter 13E works the example in full, with a decomposition of its Greeks and its P&L. Decomposed, it is three things:

- **The long LEAPS call** is roughly long SPX with a financing cost built in: 50 to 150 bps a year of [[embedded leverage]] drag (Frazzini-Pedersen 2012, 2022).
- **The short put spread** is a small harvest of the variance risk premium with a bounded loss, "−2 to −3 vol points realized after costs" in the Edge Book's words.
- **Net:** long about 1.4 times SPX, with embedded drag and margin requirements that depend on the path.

Three simpler alternatives give equivalent or better economic exposure, with less operational complexity and better path properties:

- **ES futures:** 1.4 times exposure with cleaner financing, at a basis of about SOFR plus 10 to 20 bps.
- **[[Box spread]] financing plus SPY:** similar economics, with no dependence on the mark-to-market (MTM).
- **A 2 times SPX [[leveraged ETF]] (SSO):** an expense ratio of 0.85%, but no LEAPS to roll and no put spread to manage.

**Why the "edge" claim is wrong.** The argument is that the VRP makes the structure profitable. But:

- The VRP from 1-year put spreads, after retail costs, is roughly 1 to 3 volatility points net.
- The embedded leverage drag in LEAPS is roughly 50 to 150 bps a year.
- The net "edge" over simple leverage is approximately zero, or small.
- The path properties are *worse*: margin expands under stress, and the position can be force-liquidated.

::: worked Paying for $400,000 of extra exposure
**Step 1: through LEAPS.** Take the $1 million account of Chapter 1E that wants $1.4 million of SPX exposure. Borrowing the extra $400,000 through LEAPS costs a drag of 50 to 150 bps on it: $2,000 to $6,000 a year.

**Step 2: through futures.** Through ES the same exposure is financed at about SOFR plus 10 to 20 bps, only $400 to $800 a year above the risk-free rate. The put spread's small premium has to make up that gap of up to $5,600 a year before it adds anything, and it has to survive the path.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: The combination against futures in stress. For the same effective exposure, bars give the loss of the put spread and LEAPS structure and of ES futures when SPX falls 20% and the VIX rises 25 points, beside each position's margin cushion as requirements expand. The reader sets the account size and the leverage, and sees which position is forced to close before the path completes.]

It is leverage, dressed up; calling it "edge" overstates what it adds. Chapter 1E warned against this structure as the leverage trap.

::: warn Common mistake: "the put spread plus LEAP gives me equity exposure plus a VRP edge"
The VRP component is small, 1 to 3 volatility points, and largely consumed by execution costs and by the embedded leverage drag in the LEAPS. After honest accounting, the structure adds essentially nothing over simple leverage. It is complexity that has been narrated as edge.
:::

::: rule
If you want leveraged equity exposure, use ES futures or 2 times ETFs. The put spread and LEAPS structure adds complexity without economic benefit.
:::

No chapter of the Dealer Mechanics Lab teaches this combination. Its nearest relative in the strategy atlas, the [[poor man's covered call]] of Chapter 12C, pairs a LEAPS call with short calls instead, and its long leg carries the same embedded drag.

## Catastrophic tails on both sides: VIX exchange-traded products

**Long VIX ETPs.** **VXX has lost 99.84% from its inception in 2009 through 2024.** The losses come from continuous [[roll decay|roll yield]]. The VIX term structure is in contango most of the time, so the [[exchange-traded product]] keeps selling expiring futures for less than it pays for their replacements. Chapter 10A works the arithmetic. The yearly roll decay is 5% to 15% in normal times, and much higher in extended periods of contango.

As a portfolio hedge, the carrying cost dominates. You would need very specific timing, entering near an inversion of the term structure and exiting before it mean-reverts, to come out ahead after costs, and most retail attempts fail.

**Short VIX ETPs.** **XIV was terminated after losing 96% in a single day, in February 2018.** The Bank for International Settlements puts the loss at 84% (Chapter 8H). SVXY has since been de-leveraged but is still subject to catastrophic single-day losses in volatility shocks. Chapter 8H tells the story of that day. The "short vol" trade through an inverse VIX ETP has negative skew even more extreme than that of direct put-writing, for three reasons:

- VIX moves are highly non-linear: a 5-point spike in the VIX is much more than a 5% move in SPX.
- The ETP's daily rebalancing forces it to buy volatility after a stress day, selling at the worst time.
- Termination clauses can wipe out the position.

::: worked Why an inverse product can end in a day
**Step 1: the daily reset.** A product that returns −1 times its index each day loses \(x\%\) whenever the index rises \(x\%\). If the index of VIX futures it shorts doubles in a day, the product loses everything.

**Step 2: short of doubling.** A rise of 96% in the index takes 96% of the product's value in one session. That leaves almost no path back, since a later 50% fall in the index only adds half to the product's tiny remainder, taking 4% of its old value to 6%.
:::

::: rule
Do not trade VIX ETPs. If you want volatility exposure, long or short, use SPX or XSP options directly, with explicit position management.
:::

The Dealer Mechanics Lab chapters agree that the VIX is a gauge, and they put VXX's roll drag at about 10% a year (Chapter 9A). They do use one VIX derivative: long-dated VIX calls, which Chapter 13H names the cleanest hedge for a book of short vega and Chapter 12F lists as the first choice for that purpose. Those are options on the VIX, not ETPs, so they fall outside this rejection, though the Edge Book's own advice for volatility exposure points to SPX and XSP options instead.

## What looks like edge but is not

When you evaluate any candidate strategy, these are red flags:

| Red flag | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| A vivid narrative without academic backing | Storytelling, not edge |
| A backtest covering a single decade | Not robust to a change of regime |
| A backtest on "easy" data, at the mid-quote with no slippage | It does not survive the retail haircut |
| A defined-risk wrapper that hides skew | The skew tax still applies |
| A strategy that needs constant attention | The behavioral haircut compounds |
| Reliance on one data vendor's signal | Model-dependent, not edge |
| Performance that depends on a recent regime | Specific to the sample, not structural |
| Polished content without transparency on costs | Costs likely dominate |

The textbook rule is short: **a real edge survives critical evaluation, and one that does not is narrative.**

## What this leaves you with

After Chapters 10B to 10I, the universe of allocatable strategies is manageable:

- **Foundation premia:** the equity risk premium (ERP), held as long equity (Chapter 10B), and the VRP, through cash-secured at-the-money (ATM) XSP put-writing only (Chapter 10C). Trend following, through funds such as DBMF or KMLM (Chapter 10D), and cross-asset carry, a small allocation where you can reach it (Chapter 10E), complete them.
- **Factor tilts:** quality (QUAL), momentum (MTUM), value (a RAFI fund or AVUV), low volatility (USMV) and size with quality (AVUV) (Chapter 10F).
- **Structural overlays, implemented cheaply only** (Chapter 10G): the FOMC even-week tilt through the sizing of equity, end-of-quarter (EOQ) rebalancing at a very small size with an explicit direction, and Halloween through the annual rebalance.
- **Regime filters:** the VIX term structure, canonical and phased; realized against implied volatility; the liquidity composite; and the sign of GEX as a low-confidence supplement (Chapter 10H).

The universe is manageable for a reason: most of what is marketed as "edge" does not survive evaluation. The taxonomy and the conviction framework of Chapter 13B filter aggressively.

Where a chapter of this book teaches a rejected trade, read both chapters before you act, and size by the more cautious one.
:::

::: warn When it fails
A rejection is a judgment on the evidence available, and evidence can change. An effect that decayed could return if its mechanism returned, and a strategy rejected on one decade's structure could behave differently in another. The discipline cuts both ways: do not trade a rejected strategy on a story, but do not dismiss new, well-tested evidence because a chapter once rejected the idea.
:::

::: rule
- **Allocate nothing to any strategy on the map of rejection.**
- **Keep the informational uses:** the sign of GEX as low-confidence context, the earnings calendar to avoid holding long premium into a report, and the VIX term structure as a filter.
- **File every new strategy** as decayed, never robust or institutional-only before you consider it, and require specific evidence that it is none of these.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "a strategy with many practitioners must be tradable"
Practitioner popularity is not evidence quality. Many strategies in this chapter, such as GEX scalping, iron condors and VIX ETPs, have huge retail communities. The communities exist because the strategies *feel* tradable: they have specific entries, dramatic narratives and frequent trades. The empirical evidence is what matters, and it does not support these strategies as positive-edge for retail. Do not confuse popularity with validity.
:::

Chapters 10B to 10H covered the strategies you can allocate to, and this chapter those you should specifically not trade. Together they complete the taxonomy. Part XIII turns the concepts into judgment: ten worked examples in Chapter 13E walk the full chain of reasoning end to end. Chapters 13F, 13C and 13G then give the setup cards, the pipeline from signal to trade and the kill criteria, and the reference chapters, R1 to R4, collect the tools.

::: recap
- There are three reasons to reject a strategy: its edge decayed, it was never robust, or it works only with institutional infrastructure.
- Pre-FOMC drift and the S&P 500 inclusion premium decayed when their mechanisms changed or capital arrived.
- Single-name earnings short volatility, GEX-level scalping, systematic iron condors and the put spread with LEAPS were never robust for retail: negative mean, model-dependent levels, a decades-long lag, and leverage in disguise.
- VIX ETPs fail on both sides: long ones bleed roll decay, and short ones carry tails that ended XIV in a day.
- Where the Dealer Mechanics Lab teaches one of these trades, both traditions still reject its systematic or scalping form. They differ on how much weight the rest deserves.
:::

::: check
?? Name the three reasons a strategy can be rejected, with an example of each.
!! Decayed: the drift before FOMC announcements, which disappeared after the Fed's transparency reforms, and the S&P 500 inclusion premium, down from about 7% to under 1%. Never robust: GEX-level scalping, or systematic iron condors, whose CNDR index returned about 690% against about 5,500% for the SPX Total Return index from 1986 to 2024. Institutional-only: the cross-sectional earnings short-volatility portfolios of Cao, Han, Tong and Zhan, which need 200 or more names and cross-margining.

?? Why does naive single-name earnings short volatility have a negative mean despite a positive median?
!! Most reports move less than the options priced, so the typical trade keeps a small gain from the IV crush. A few reports bring moves far beyond what was priced, from misses, guidance cuts or warnings, and those losses are large. In the chapter's toy, 80% of trades gain $6 and 20% lose $30, a median of +$6 and a mean of −$1.20. Short volatility is short skew, and for a single name the skew tax exceeds the premium.

?? What is the CNDR result, and what does it imply about systematic condors?
!! From 1986 to 2024 the CNDR index returned about 690%, against about 5,500% for the SPX Total Return index: about 5.5% a year compounded against about 11%. Systematic condors win often but lose on the wing breaks, which come often enough to outweigh the theta. Defined risk caps each trade's loss, not the aggregate, so a mechanical condor sleeve is rejected.

?? Decompose the put spread and LEAPS combination. Why is the "edge" claim wrong?
!! The LEAPS call is roughly long SPX with 50 to 150 bps a year of embedded leverage drag. The short put spread is a small VRP harvest, 1 to 3 volatility points net after retail costs, with a bounded loss. Together they are about 1.4 times SPX exposure. The premium barely covers the drag, so the net edge over simple leverage is about zero, while the path is worse: margin expands in stress and the position can be force-liquidated. ES futures or a 2 times ETF give the same exposure more simply.

?? Why have VIX ETPs, long and short, been catastrophic for retail accounts?
!! A long VIX ETP rolls futures in contango most of the time, losing 5% to 15% a year in normal conditions; VXX lost 99.84% from 2009 through 2024. A short VIX ETP earns that roll but carries an extreme left tail: VIX spikes are non-linear, daily rebalancing forces it to buy volatility after stress, and termination clauses can end it. XIV lost 96% in a single day in February 2018.

?? A friend proposes selling a straddle on every earnings report of a popular stock, because "it wins 80% of the time". Classify the proposal and say what, if anything, you would do instead.
!! It is on the map: naive single-name earnings short volatility, never robust at retail. A win rate of 80% is exactly the positive median that Adams and Neururer found beside a negative mean. If you want to trade around a report at all, use a calendar spread on a liquid name. It limits the loss on a miss and captures some of the IV crush, as an occasional small bet rather than a sleeve.
:::
