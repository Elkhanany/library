## Why vega per position is not enough

The aggregate vega of a swing portfolio is rarely discussed in retail education. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls it the single largest source of catastrophic loss in real premium-selling portfolios. Consider a swing trader running all of these at once:

- One SPX [[iron condor]], short vega.
- Two short [[put credit spreads|put credit spread]] on SPY, short vega.
- One short [[strangle]] on QQQ, short vega.
- A program of [[covered calls|covered call]] on individual stocks, short vega.
- A LEAP [[collar]] on a core equity holding, with mixed vega.

Each position, viewed alone, looks reasonable, and the trader has given each one 1% to 2% of the portfolio's risk. But the **[[aggregate vega]]**, the total dollar P&L for each volatility point the VIX moves, is typically the equivalent of being short several hundred dollars of SPX vega per point. When the VIX moves from 15 to 25 over three days, a routine stress episode and not a tail event, the mark-to-market loss across the portfolio can exceed the sum of the individual stop-loss budgets. Each stop was set on the price of its underlying and sized for the loss a price move would cause. A jump in implied volatility adds a loss that no price stop sees, in every position at once, before any single stop triggers.

This is the **vega correlation problem**. Short-vega positions across SPX, SPY, QQQ and individual stocks are *not* diversified: they all lose together in a VIX-spike regime. The rule of risking 1% a trade, which works well for delta exposure, produces concentrated portfolio risk on the vega axis.

::: familiar
The problem is overlapping toxicity. Each drug in a regimen can be dosed within its own limit, but three drugs that all suppress the marrow add their toxicity on the same axis, and the patient meets the sum. Each short-premium position has its own risk limit, and all of them lose on the same axis when implied volatility rises. An aggregate vega budget is a cumulative limit for the whole regimen, set on that shared axis.
:::

## Adding up the book's vega

For each position \(i\), write \(\nu_i\) for the option's vega per unit of the underlying per volatility point, the number a platform shows (Chapter 5C). With \(n_i\) contracts, a [[contract multiplier]] \(m\) of 100, and a sign \(s_i\) of +1 for a long position and −1 for a short one, the position's vega in dollars per volatility point is

\[ V_i = s_i\, n_i\, m\, \nu_i \]
{#eq:pv}

If your platform gives vega per unit of \(\sigma\), the formula's own value, where 1.0 means 100 volatility points, multiply by 0.01 as well. The Dealer Mechanics Lab writes the rule with that factor for this reason.

Single-name vega does not move one for one with the VIX. So scale each position by the **[[VIX beta]]** of its underlying, the number of points its implied volatility typically moves for each point of the VIX. The book's aggregate vega is then the sum

\[ V = \sum_i \beta_i\, V_i \]
{#eq:agg}

and a VIX move of \(\Delta\)VIX points changes the book's value by about \(V \times \Delta\text{VIX}\). The Lab gives typical VIX betas as ranges:

| Underlying | VIX beta | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SPX, SPY | 1.00 | By definition, since the VIX is SPX's volatility |
| QQQ | 0.85 to 0.95 | High beta, with slightly less movement in its implied volatility than SPX |
| IWM | 0.80 to 0.90 | Small companies, a different index |
| Large single stocks, such as NVDA, MSFT and AAPL | 0.60 to 0.85 | Varies by company |
| Mid-cap and small-cap stocks | 0.40 to 0.65 | Implied volatility specific to the company, less correlated with the VIX |

[FIGURE IDEA: Estimating a VIX beta. A scatter of daily changes in a stock's at-the-money implied volatility against daily changes in the VIX, with a fitted line whose slope is the stock's VIX beta. The reader picks a stock from a menu of synthetic examples and a window of 60 to 500 days, and sees the slope and its spread, with the Lab's range for that kind of stock shaded. It shows why a beta is an estimate with its own uncertainty, and why earnings days pull a single name's points off the line.]

A single at-the-money SPX option carries a lot of vega. Chapter 5C puts a 30-day at-the-money SPX call at about 6.8 index points per volatility point with the index at 6,000 and implied volatility at 20%, about $680 a contract. Two short SPX puts like it hold about −$1,360 of vega per point on their own.

::: worked Adding up a swing book
**Step 1: the positions.** Take the book above with hypothetical vegas per contract, in dollars per volatility point, as a platform would show them after the multiplier:

| Position | Side and size | Vega per contract | \(V_i\) | VIX beta | \(\beta_i V_i\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPX iron condor | Short 1 | $300 | −$300 | 1.00 | −$300 |
| SPY put credit spreads, first | Short 10 | $8 | −$80 | 1.00 | −$80 |
| SPY put credit spreads, second | Short 10 | $8 | −$80 | 1.00 | −$80 |
| QQQ short strangle | Short 3 | $30 | −$90 | 0.90 | −$81 |
| NVDA covered calls | Short 5 | $20 | −$100 | 0.75 | −$75 |
| AAPL covered calls | Short 5 | $15 | −$75 | 0.70 | −$52.50 |

**Step 2: the total.** By [eq:agg], the book holds \(V = -300 - 80 - 80 - 81 - 75 - 52.50 = -\$668.50\) per volatility point. The LEAP collar is left out, since a long put and a short call on the same holding offset each other's vega.

**Step 3: the stress.** A move in the VIX from 15 to 25 costs about \(10 \times 668.50 = \$6{,}685\), with no move in any price at all. On a $500,000 account that is 1.3% of the portfolio, spread across six positions none of whose stops has been touched.
:::

### How much is too much

For a swing account of $250,000 to $1 million, the Lab puts a reasonable upper bound on aggregate short vega at $500 to $1,500 per volatility point. At that bound, a spike of 10 points in the VIX costs $5,000 to $15,000 on the marks. Beyond that, the portfolio becomes a directional bet on the VIX rather than a diversified swing book. The book in the example, at $668.50, sits inside the band.

The Lab also gives a stress threshold in proportion to the account. When aggregate short vega exceeds 1% of the net asset value (NAV) per volatility point, the portfolio is over-concentrated: trim or hedge before adding any new short-premium position. The two limits do not agree. On a $1 million account, 1% of NAV per point is $10,000 per point, almost seven times the top of the band, and a 10-point spike at that level costs 10% of NAV. On $250,000 it is $2,500 per point. Use the dollar band as the working limit, since it binds first, and treat the NAV threshold as the point past which no new short premium goes on at all.

## A monitoring cadence for vega

- **Daily, at the end of the day:** compute aggregate vega from the current marks and compare it with last week's number. A large change needs investigating.
- **Weekly, at Friday's close:** write a full report of the aggregate vega, each position's contribution, and the share of NAV that a spike of 10 volatility points would cost.
- **Before each event,** such as an FOMC meeting, a [[Consumer Price Index]] (CPI) release, [[Non-Farm Payrolls]] (NFP) or a major earnings report in a name the portfolio holds, recompute the aggregate vega. Consider trimming if it is elevated.
- **At the stress threshold:** above 1% of NAV per volatility point, trim or hedge before adding any new short-premium position.

## Hedging short vega: the macro vega overlay

Three practical hedges fit a book with too much short vega:

1. **Long-dated VIX calls, 90 to 180 days to expiry.** The cleanest macro hedge of vega. Strike them at a VIX of 25 to 30, well above the mean of about 17 for the curve from the [[VIX9D]] to the VIX3M. Size them to cover *half* of the aggregate short vega, since a full hedge costs too much carry and defeats the purpose of harvesting the VRP. Roll them quarterly. The figure's strike of 1.4 times spot lands in that range when the VIX is between about 18 and 21.
2. **Long SPX puts, 90 to 180 days to expiry, struck at 90% to 92% of the index.** These carry direction along with vega. They are cheaper than VIX calls in absolute dollars but more correlated with the delta book, so they are less pure as a hedge of vega. They are useful when the swing portfolio is already net long delta.
3. **Purchases of long-dated SPX call spreads or put spreads, 60 to 90 days to expiry.** They have defined risk and a small positive vega. They are less powerful than the first two but cheaper, and useful as a partial offset when the short vega is modest, below 0.5% of NAV per volatility point.

[FIGURE IDEA: Three hedges side by side. For a book of short vega the reader sets, bars compare a half hedge built from VIX calls 90 to 180 days out, from SPX puts struck at 90% to 92% and from SPX spreads 60 to 90 days out: its cost, the vega it adds and the delta it brings. A path of the VIX and SPX that the reader draws then shows each hedged book's P&L through a spike. It makes plain why the puts suit a book already long delta and the calls a book that is not.]

**Do not hedge short vega with more option sales.** Selling deeper out-of-the-money (OTM) puts to "fund" the hedge increases the aggregate short vega rather than reducing it. The hedge must consume premium, not generate it. Chapter 12F lists the same first two hedges for a short-volatility book, and Chapter 10I sets these VIX calls apart from the VIX exchange-traded products it rejects.

## Common failure modes

::: warn Common mistake: stealth accumulation of vega
A trader adds one iron condor a month for six months. Each looks like 1% of risk, but the aggregate vega has grown to six times the size of one position, and no single decision triggered a review. Track aggregate vega as a portfolio-level metric, not only the risk of each position.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: a pre-event hedge that is too small
Before an FOMC meeting, the trader buys $500 of VIX calls "for protection" against a book with $1,200 of short vega per point. At a hypothetical $250 a call, $500 buys 2 calls and about $160 of vega, 13% of the exposure, which is too small to matter. Size a hedge as a fraction of the gross exposure it hedges, 30% to 50%, not as a fraction of free cash: here $360 to $600 of vega, 5 to 8 calls.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: hedging with VIX calls that expire this month
Short-dated VIX calls decay catastrophically. The VIX's own volatility of volatility, which the [[VVIX]] measures, is high, so the calls carry a lot of time value, and near expiry it melts fast while [[charm]] pulls their delta toward zero. Long-dated calls cost more in absolute dollars but decay more slowly, and they protect across a stress that unfolds over several weeks.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: Two VIX calls through a slow stress. The value of a VIX call expiring in 3 weeks and of one expiring in 4 months, both struck at 25, drawn along a VIX path that drifts sideways for a month and then spikes. The reader sets the timing of the spike, and sees the short-dated call expire almost worthless before the stress arrives while the long-dated one still pays.]

::: warn Common mistake: counting a LEAP collar as short vega
A covered call on a stock together with a protective put, the classic LEAP collar, is approximately vega-neutral. Do not count it as short vega in the aggregate.
:::

::: rule
- **Add up vega every day,** beta-adjusted, across every position, and report it weekly with the cost of a 10-point spike.
- **Keep aggregate short vega within $500 to $1,500 per point** on a $250,000 to $1 million account, and add no short premium above 1% of NAV per point.
- **Hedge about half of it** with VIX calls 90 to 180 days out, rolled quarterly, or with longer-dated SPX puts when the book is already long delta.
- **Recompute before every scheduled event,** and never hedge by selling more options.
:::

## The trade journal

```
Profit target:

=== MANAGEMENT ===
[Adjustments during the trade: additions, partial exits, stop moves]
[Allowed: a stop trailed up behind a winner, or a full exit.
 Not allowed: stop migration, moving the stop away from the price.]

=== OUTCOME ===
Exit reason: [profit target | stop | time stop |
              discretionary | event-driven]

Did the regime change during the hold?
```

The management line allows only two stop moves: a [[trailing stop]] raised behind a winning long position, or a full exit. It forbids [[stop migration]], moving the stop further from the price as the price approaches it (Chapter 11N), which turns a planned loss into a larger one. A trade's R-multiple is its P&L divided by the risk budget, R (Chapter 11H).

Three principles make the journal work:

1. **Write everything before the trade.** The pre-trade fields are filled in before you click buy, which catches lazy setups.
2. **Do not editorialize the outcome.** Record what happened, not what should have happened.
3. **Review monthly.** Scan the trades of the prior 4 to 6 weeks and look for patterns: which setups produced positive [[R-multiples|R-multiple]], and which regimes worked.

The journal is the framework's external memory. Without it, the framework cannot improve over time.

- Short-vega positions across SPX, SPY, QQQ and single stocks lose together in a VIX spike, so per-trade risk limits leave the vega axis concentrated.
- A position's vega in dollars per point is \(s_i\, n_i\, m\, \nu_i\). Scale each by its underlying's VIX beta and add them up.
- Keep aggregate short vega within $500 to $1,500 per point on a swing account of $250,000 to $1 million; the dollar band binds before the NAV rule.
- Hedge about half the short vega with VIX calls 90 to 180 days out, never with more option sales.
- Log every trade in the journal, with pre-trade fields filled before entry, and review the patterns monthly.

::: check
?? You are short 4 SPX puts with a vega of $650 a contract and short 10 QQQ puts with a vega of $12 a contract. What is the aggregate vega, and what does a 10-point VIX spike cost?
!! The SPX puts give \(-4 \times 650 = -\$2{,}600\) per point at a beta of 1.00. The QQQ puts give \(-10 \times 12 = -\$120\), scaled by 0.90 to −$108. The aggregate is −$2,708 per point, and a 10-point spike costs about $27,080. That is well above the $1,500 top of the band, so trim or hedge before adding anything.

?? Your book holds −$1,200 of vega per point, and you want to hedge half of it with VIX calls carrying about $80 of vega each. How many do you buy, and why not hedge it all?
!! Half is $600, so \(600/80 = 7.5\), which rounds up to 8 calls. A full hedge costs too much in carry: the calls decay while you wait for a spike, and flattening the vega completely gives up the VRP the book exists to harvest.

?? In the figure, what do the flat, half and full targets do to a book of −$400 per point?
!! Flat takes the vega to zero, which needs \(\lceil 400/80 \rceil = 5\) calls. Half takes it to −$200, which needs \(\lceil 200/80 \rceil = 3\) calls. Full leaves the whole −$400 in place, so the overlay buys nothing.
:::
