## What the VIX measures

The [[VIX]] is widely called the "fear gauge", a label that is useful as a mood but imprecise as mathematics. **The VIX is the model-free 30-day implied volatility of the S&P 500, expressed in annualized percentage points.** Its construction starts from the implied *variance* of the 30-day SPX log return, computed from a weighted strip of [[out-of-the-money]] option prices. It then takes the square root and multiplies by 100.

So a VIX of 18 means that the model-free 30-day implied volatility of SPX is 18% a year, not 18 units of variance. "Model-free" means that no pricing model stands between the option prices and the number. The [[implied volatility]] of Chapter 5E runs one option's price backward through the Black-Scholes formula, while the VIX reads variance directly off a whole strip of prices.

The distinction matters because variance and volatility move on different scales. Variance is volatility squared, so doubling implied volatility from 18 to 36 quadruples implied variance, from 324 to 1,296 in squared percentage points. The published VIX lives on the volatility scale, which is where a trader's feel for how far the index can move lives. By the rule of 16, a VIX of 18 prices a typical daily move of about \(18/16 = 1.1\%\).

It shows the variance bar racing ahead as volatility doubles, which is why a contract paid on variance balloons in a spike while the VIX only doubles.]

## Building variance out of options

::: plain
Picture a contract that pays, in 30 days, the variance that the S&P 500 actually shows over those 30 days. Its fair price today is the market's implied variance, and nobody needs to trade that contract for the price to exist. A strip of SPX options, held in the right amounts and delta-hedged, pays almost exactly the same thing, so the strip's cost is the contract's price. The VIX is the square root of that cost, in percent.
:::

### The formula

Cboe computes the 30-day implied variance \(\sigma^2\), annualized, with this formula:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma^2 &= \frac{2}{T}\sum_i \frac{\Delta K_i}{K_i^2}\,e^{rT}\,Q(K_i) \\
&\quad - \frac{1}{T}\Big(\frac{F}{K_0}-1\Big)^2
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:vix}

The published index is then \(\text{VIX} = 100\sqrt{\sigma^2}\): the square root returns to the volatility scale, and the factor 100 turns 0.18 into 18. The symbols in [eq:vix] mean the following:

- Time \(T\) is the time to expiry in years, \(30/365\) for 30 days.
- Each strike in the strip is \(K_i\), and \(\Delta K_i\) is the width it stands for: half the distance between its two neighbors, or the one-sided distance at either end of the strip.
- The price \(Q(K_i)\) is the [[mid-quote]] of the out-of-the-money option at \(K_i\), which is a put below the forward and a call above it.
- The **[[forward price]]** \(F\) is the price at which you could agree today to buy the index on the expiry date.
- The strike \(K_0\) is the listed strike immediately below \(F\).
- The rate \(r\) is the [[risk-free rate]], and the factor \(e^{rT}\) grows each option's price forward to expiry, for a reason the derivation below gives.

The first line adds up the strip's weighted option prices and scales them to an annual variance. The second line is a small correction for the forward not sitting exactly on a listed strike.

**The \(1/K^2\) weighting is not arbitrary**: it is the coefficient that makes a portfolio of options replicate a [[variance swap]]. Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou showed this in 1999, in the Goldman Sachs Quantitative Strategies Research Notes. Holding \(\Delta K/K^2\) of each strike across all out-of-the-money strikes gives a position whose P&L is, exactly, total realized variance minus a fixed strike, which is a variance swap. **The VIX is the discrete version of the fair strike of that swap**, scaled and annualized.

Every term connects back to dealer hedging through the gamma-theta decomposition of Chapter 7A. A delta-hedged option earns \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2[(\Delta S/S)^2 - \sigma^2\Delta t]\) over each interval. Weight this by \(1/K^2\) and sum over strikes, and the gammas combine into a roughly constant exposure per percent move wherever spot is. That flat dollar gamma is exactly the property a variance swap requires, and Chapter 7A works it through. **The VIX is, fundamentally, the cost of replicating variance through delta-hedged options.**

### Where the formula comes from

The derivation needs only algebra, the log series and one integral read as accumulation. Its heart is a single identity: over a small move, a squared return is twice the gap between the simple return and the log return.

::: grind Deriving the VIX formula
**Step 1: a squared return from logs.** For a small daily return \(R\), the log return is \(\ln(1+R) = R - R^2/2 + R^3/3 - \dots\) Dropping the cube and everything after it, and rearranging, gives

\[ R^2 \approx 2\,[\,R - \ln(1+R)\,] \]
{#eq:sqret}

For a 2% day the left side is 0.000400 and the right side is \(2 \times (0.02 - 0.019803) = 0.000395\).

**Step 2: add up the days.** The daily log returns add up to \(\ln(S_T/S_0)\), the log of the final price over the first (Chapter 1A). Summing [eq:sqret] over the days to expiry therefore gives

\[ \sum_t R_t^2 \approx 2\sum_t R_t - 2\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \]

The left side is the realized variance over the period, which is what a variance swap pays. The right side is two trades. The first, \(\sum_t R_t\), is what you earn by holding $1 of the index and resetting it to $1 every day, a dynamic trade like a delta hedge. The second, \(-\ln(S_T/S_0)\), depends on the final price alone: it is called a **[[log contract]]**, and options can build it.

**Step 3: value the daily trade.** Take no dividends, to keep the algebra short. Under the [[risk-neutral probabilities|risk-neutral probability]] that price options (Chapter 5C), each day's expected return is the risk-free rate for that day, so the daily trade is worth \(rT\) in expectation.

**Step 4: measure the log contract from the forward.** With no dividends the forward is \(F = S_0e^{rT}\), so \(\ln(F/S_0) = rT\) and

\[ -\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} = -\ln\frac{S_T}{F} - rT \]

Now define the payoff \(g(S) = S/F - 1 - \ln(S/F)\). It is zero at \(S = F\), its slope there is zero, and its curvature, the slope of its slope, is \(1/S^2\).

**Step 5: build g from options.** A payoff that is zero with zero slope at \(F\) is a pile of options, puts below \(F\) and calls above it, holding \(g''(K)\,dK = dK/K^2\) of each strike:

\[
\begin{aligned}
g(S_T) = &\int_0^F \frac{(K-S_T)^+}{K^2}\,dK \\
&+ \int_F^\infty \frac{(S_T-K)^+}{K^2}\,dK
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(x^+\) means \(x\) when it is positive and zero otherwise, which is an option's payoff. To check it for a final price below the forward, note that only the puts struck above \(S_T\) pay. They add up to \(\int_{S_T}^F (K - S_T)/K^2\,dK = \ln(F/S_T) + S_T/F - 1\), which is \(g(S_T)\). **This is where the \(1/K^2\) comes from: it is the curvature of the log contract.**

**Step 6: price the strip.** An option's price is its expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate, so its expected payoff is its price grown by \(e^{rT}\). With \(Q(K)\) the out-of-the-money price at each strike,

\[ \mathbb{E}[g(S_T)] = e^{rT}\!\int_0^\infty \frac{Q(K)}{K^2}\,dK \]

Under the same probabilities the forward is the expected final price, so \(\mathbb{E}[S_T/F - 1] = 0\) and the expected log contract is \(\mathbb{E}[-\ln(S_T/F)] = e^{rT}\int_0^\infty Q(K)/K^2\,dK\).

**Step 7: assemble.** Take expectations in Step 2 and substitute Steps 3, 4 and 6, and the two \(rT\) terms cancel:

\[ \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t R_t^2\Big] \approx 2e^{rT}\!\int_0^\infty \frac{Q(K)}{K^2}\,dK \]

Divide by \(T\) to annualize, and replace the integral by a sum over the listed strikes, each carrying its width \(\Delta K_i\). That is the first line of [eq:vix].

**Step 8: the correction.** Cboe switches from puts to calls at \(K_0\), the strike just below \(F\), rather than at \(F\) itself. Redoing Steps 4 to 7 with the switch at \(K_0\) leaves an extra \(-2[x - \ln(1+x)]\), with \(x = F/K_0 - 1\). By the log series that is about \(-x^2\), and dividing by \(T\) gives the second line of [eq:vix].
:::

::: worked A six-strike VIX
The formula is opaque until you compute one. In this stylized strip of six strikes, every term lands somewhere.

**Step 1: the setup.** SPX is at 4,500 and its 30-day forward \(F\) is 4,503. Time is \(T = 30/365 = 0.0822\) years, the rate is \(r = 0.05\), and the strike immediately below the forward is \(K_0 = 4{,}500\). The strip has six out-of-the-money contributions:

| Strike | Type | Mid-quote \(Q(K)\) | \(\Delta K\) | \((\Delta K/K^2) \times Q\) |
|---:|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,400 | put | $18 | 50 | \(4.65 \times 10^{-5}\) |
| 4,450 | put | $32 | 50 | \(8.08 \times 10^{-5}\) |
| 4,500 | \(K_0\) | $52 | 50 | \(1.28 \times 10^{-4}\) |
| 4,550 | call | $48 | 50 | \(1.16 \times 10^{-4}\) |
| 4,600 | call | $30 | 75 | \(1.06 \times 10^{-4}\) |
| 4,700 | call | $14 | 100 | \(6.34 \times 10^{-5}\) |

At an inside strike, \(\Delta K\) is half the distance between its neighbors, so at 4,600 it is \((4{,}700 - 4{,}550)/2 = 75\). At the boundary strikes, 4,400 and 4,700, it is the one-sided distance to the neighbor.

**Step 2: sum the strike contributions.** The first row is \(50 \times 18/4{,}400^2 = 900/19{,}360{,}000 = 4.65 \times 10^{-5}\), and the whole column adds up to \(\sum_i (\Delta K_i/K_i^2)\,Q(K_i) = 5.41 \times 10^{-4}\).

**Step 3: scale by 2/T and the growth factor.** With \(2/T = 24.33\) and \(e^{rT} = e^{0.05 \times 0.0822} = 1.0041\), the first line of [eq:vix] is \(24.33 \times 1.0041 \times 5.41 \times 10^{-4} = 0.01322\).

**Step 4: subtract the forward correction.** The second line is \((1/T)(F/K_0 - 1)^2 = 12.17 \times (6.67 \times 10^{-4})^2 = 5.4 \times 10^{-6}\). That is effectively negligible at this strike spacing, which is why a forward sitting right on top of \(K_0\) is the desirable choice in Cboe's specification.

**Step 5: combine and take the square root.** The variance is \(\sigma^2 = 0.01322 - 0.0000054 = 0.01321\), so the index is \(\text{VIX} = 100\sqrt{0.01321} = 11.5\).
:::

The toy strip lands at 11.5 because its quotes correspond to an implied volatility of around 12%, a calm-regime strip. The same chain with every quote roughly doubled gives a VIX near 16 to 17, and with every quote roughly tripled, typical of quotes after 2020, it gives one near 20.

Those numbers show how the index scales. **The VIX grows with the square root of the strip's premium level, not in proportion to it.** Doubling every quote doubles the variance and multiplies the VIX by \(\sqrt{2} = 1.41\), from 11.5 to 16.3, while tripling multiplies it by \(\sqrt{3} = 1.73\), to 19.9. The geometry of the formula does not change, which is the payoff of doing it by hand once.

Two caveats separate the toy from the real index. The real VIX is computed from about 150 strikes under Cboe's methodology, not six, and what changes is the precision, not the chain of steps. The published VIX also interpolates between two expirations, the near and the next, to reach exactly 30 days, where the toy uses one.

## Why the VIX rises when SPX falls

The \(1/K^2\) weighting puts disproportionate weight on low-strike options: at equal spacing, a put at 4,400 counts \((4{,}700/4{,}400)^2 = 1.14\) times as much as a call at 4,700 of the same price. Combine that with the equity [[volatility skew]], in which out-of-the-money puts trade at higher implied volatility than out-of-the-money calls. By the Dealer Mechanics Lab's account, a 1% downside move in SPX then re-prices the put wing far more than a 1% upside move re-prices the call wing.

**On that account, this is why the VIX is structurally inverse to SPX.** It is not "fear" as such, but the mechanical asymmetry of how variance replication weights the strikes where the market is hedged. The correlation between daily SPX returns and daily VIX changes is approximately −0.80 across decades of data. The Dealer Mechanics Lab attributes that correlation not to fear and stock prices moving together, but to the maths of variance replication. That maths requires a put-side reweighting that responds asymmetrically to direction.

The mechanism needs implied volatility that stays attached to its strikes as spot moves. Take the figure's default smile, in which implied volatility rises by 0.18 for each unit of \(\ln(K/F)\) below the forward and falls by only 0.04 for each unit above it. Hold each strike's implied volatility fixed while spot falls 1%, and the new at-the-money strike sits at \(\ln(0.99) = -0.010\) on the old smile. Its implied volatility is higher by \(0.18 \times 0.010 = 0.0018\), 0.18 of a [[volatility point]]. After a 1% rise, the new at-the-money strike sits on the flat call side, where implied volatility is lower by only \(0.04 \times 0.010\), 0.04 of a point. The fall re-prices the options around the money four and a half times as much as the rise.

The strip builder cannot show this asymmetry. Its smile moves with the forward, so a shock leaves every option's implied volatility unchanged relative to the forward. If you hold the same default smile fixed to its strikes instead, a calculation the figure does not make, the asymmetry appears and grows with the size of the move. A 5% fall then lifts the computed index by about 0.7 points, while a 5% rise lowers it by about 0.4.

## The vanna loop

Recall [[vanna]] from Chapters 5D and 7B: \(\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma\), the change in an option's delta as implied volatility moves. When the VIX rises, the deltas of out-of-the-money puts become more negative. A [[dealer|market maker]] who is short those puts, the dealer of record for most institutional hedging demand, sees its effective long exposure increase, and to stay delta-neutral it must sell stock.

Selling stock pushes SPX further down, and a further fall lifts the VIX further. The higher VIX deepens the put deltas again, and the loop goes round once more. **Vanna is the technical name for the variable that makes VIX-driven sell-offs so violent.**

The figure below walks one pass of the loop, a step at a time. A dealer is short 1,000 out-of-the-money SPX puts struck at 4,400 against an institutional hedger, with spot at 4,500, the VIX at 20 and the put's implied volatility at 23%. Move the step slider from 0 to 5, or press play. Four readouts follow the loop: SPX, the VIX, the shift in the dealer's delta, and the stock it sells, in [[share-equivalents|share-equivalent]] for 1,000 contracts of 100.

[FIGURE: vanna-feedback-loop]

At step 1 SPX drops 1% to 4,455, and the spot move alone makes the put delta 0.04 more negative through gamma, so the dealer's delta shifts 4,000 longer. At step 2 the VIX rises 8%, from 20 to 21.6, and the put's implied volatility rises from 23% to roughly 24.8%. At step 3 vanna fires: the volatility move alone deepens the put delta by about 0.07 more, leaving the dealer 11,000 longer than it should be. At step 4 it sells those 11,000 share-equivalents, which is 0.11 of delta on 1,000 contracts of 100. By step 5 the selling has pushed SPX 2.5% below the start and the VIX 18% above it, and the dealer's delta shift reaches 20,000 with a hedge flow of 19,500. In normal markets each pass is smaller than the last, while in stress the system can become locally unstable.

The loop's arithmetic starts from a hedge that is easy to get backward.

::: worked The opening hedge, with the sign right
**Step 1: the puts' delta.** The figure does not give the puts' expiry, so suppose 30 days, with a zero rate. By Chapter 5C a put's delta is \(N(d_1) - 1\), and with spot 4,500, strike 4,400 and implied volatility 23%,

\[
\begin{aligned}
d_1 &= \frac{\ln(4{,}500/4{,}400) + 0.00217}{0.23\sqrt{0.0822}} \\
&= \frac{0.0225 + 0.0022}{0.0659} = 0.374
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(0.00217 = \tfrac12 \times 0.23^2 \times 0.0822\). The put's delta is therefore \(N(0.374) - 1 = -0.354\).

**Step 2: the dealer's delta and hedge.** The dealer is short the puts, so it holds their delta with the sign flipped, \(1{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.354 = +35{,}400\) share-equivalents: short puts are long delta. To be flat it sells 35,400 share-equivalents through index futures or a basket of the stocks, a short hedge of tens of thousands of units.

**Step 3: the loop's first sale.** By step 4 of the figure the put delta is 0.11 more negative, so the dealer's long delta has grown by 11,000. It sells 11,000 more, which takes its short hedge to 46,400.
:::

The figure's numbers are illustrative inputs, as its caption says, not the output of a model. For scale, check them against Black-Scholes for the 30-day put of the example. The 1% fall alone moves its delta by about 0.06 through gamma, and the rise in its implied volatility from 23% to 24.8% then deepens it by only about 0.004 through vanna, not 0.07. Vanna is modest for a put only 2% below spot. It is larger further out in the downside wing, the typical institutional hedge book (Chapter 5D), and its push grows with the size of the volatility jump and of the book, which in a real spike is the whole street's.

::: familiar
The vanna loop has the shape of cytokine release syndrome after CAR-T therapy. Activated T cells release cytokines, the cytokines activate more immune cells, and those release more cytokines in turn. Mild cases settle while severe ones escalate, and blocking one link, IL-6 with tocilizumab, can break the cascade. The market's links are a falling SPX, a rising VIX, deeper put deltas and dealer selling, and in stress the loop can run away. What breaks it from the other side is a fall in implied volatility, which pulls the put deltas back toward zero and turns the dealers into buyers (Chapter 5D).
:::

## Trading the VIX: futures, ETPs and options

The VIX is computed from a strip of SPX options, and it is *not* directly tradable. What you can trade comes in three kinds.

**[[VIX futures]]** are monthly contracts. Each expires on a Wednesday, 30 days before the SPX monthly [[options expiration]] (OPEX), and settles to a [[special opening quotation]] (SOQ) of the VIX on its expiry morning (Chapter 2A).

**VIX options** are cash-settled options on the VIX index level, with notional based on the index level at $100 per VIX point (Chapter 5A).

**VIX [[exchange-traded products|exchange-traded product]]** (ETPs) hold VIX futures, and four are common:

| ETP | Issuer | Exposure |
|---|---|---|
| VXX | Barclays | Long the first and second VIX futures |
| UVXY | ProShares | 2× leveraged long VIX futures |
| SVXY | ProShares | −0.5× inverse VIX futures |
| VIXY | ProShares | Unleveraged long VIX futures |

### Contango and the roll

Under normal conditions the VIX futures curve is in [[contango]]: the front-month future sits above spot VIX, and the second month sits above the front. The Dealer Mechanics Lab gives the economic reason as the [[variance risk premium]], since buyers pay extra for protection over each forward window.

For long-volatility ETPs, contango is a **cost**. In the source's description, every month they sell the front contract at the lower price and buy the second at the higher one, locking in a loss. Strictly, the swap itself costs nothing, because selling one contract to buy a dearer one only leaves you holding fewer contracts. The loss comes as each contract ages: in contango a future bought above spot drifts down toward spot as its expiry nears, even if spot VIX holds still. That drift is the [[roll yield]], which is negative for a long position in contango.

::: worked A slope you can barely see
**Step 1: one month.** Suppose the curve keeps its shape from month to month, with the second-month future at 15.00 and the front month at 14.87. You buy the second month at 15.00, and a month later it has become the front month at 14.87. You have lost 0.13 points, 0.87% of 15.00, while the VIX has not moved.

**Step 2: a year.** Twelve such months leave \((1 - 0.0087)^{12} = 0.90\) of the money, a drag of about 10% a year from a slope that is hard to see on a chart.
:::

That is the size the source reports: the accumulated drag is approximately −10% annualized for VXX, and UVXY suffers roughly twice this drag. For inverse-volatility ETPs, contango is a **benefit**, and SVXY profits from the roll. The roll was the dominant return source for XIV and SVXY before 2018, until an eight-month low-volatility regime made their positioning catastrophically large and triggered Volmageddon.

In stress the curve inverts into [[backwardation]], with the front month above the second. The roll then reverses, so long-volatility ETPs profit and inverse-volatility ETPs lose. Backwardation is a reliable stress indicator: a ratio of the VIX to the [[VIX3M]] crossing 1.0 preceded major SPX drawdowns in nearly every multi-year cycle.

The ETP issuers, Barclays for VXX and ProShares for UVXY and SVXY, are themselves significant participants in VIX futures. Their daily rebalancing is mechanical and well understood. The 16:08 EST volume spike on February 5, 2018 was the cumulative effect of XIV's forced rebalancing in the [[closing auction]], interacting with dealers' hedging of volatility of volatility (vol-of-vol).

**The operational implication:** VIX futures and VIX ETPs are *not* the same instrument as the VIX index. The roll structure adds a multi-percent annualized drag in contango, or a boost in backwardation, and the futures' basis to spot VIX can diverge in stress. Use the VIX as a regime indicator, and trade VIX futures and options as separate instruments with their own dynamics.

The Edge Book goes further and rejects VIX ETPs, long and short, for a retail account (Chapter 10I). It puts the roll decay of long VIX ETPs at 5% to 15% a year in normal times, which brackets the Dealer Mechanics Lab's 10%. It also treats the VIX term structure as a [[regime filter]] that sizes other trades, never as a trade in itself (Chapter 10H). The two sources agree that the index is a gauge, and differ on whether its derivatives belong in a retail account.

## The VIX family

Cboe launched **[[VIX1D]]** in April 2023 to measure one-day expected volatility. It did so explicitly because the 30-day VIX no longer captured the short-horizon volatility regime driven by same-day options (Chapter 9B). VIX1D applies the same model-free variance-replication formula to the very-near-dated SPX option strip. The Cboe VIX family includes six indexes:

| Index | Horizon of implied volatility | Note |
|---|---|---|
| VIX1D | 1 day | Launched in April 2023 |

The relationships among these indexes form the VIX [[term structure]]. In normal markets it is in contango, short below long, reflecting the variance risk premium: implied volatility is bid up further out, because more can happen over longer horizons. In stress it inverts into backwardation, short above long, as panic concentrates demand into near-dated puts.

In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's words, the sign of [[gamma exposure]] (GEX) combined with the shape of the VIX term structure is the most common composite signal that serious traders use to characterize the regime. Its standard combinations are these:

| Net dealer GEX | VIX term structure | Regime |
|---|---|---|
| Strongly positive | Steep contango | **Stable**: premium selling favored |
| Mildly positive | Mild contango | **Normal**: neutral character |
| Near zero | Flat or barely inverted | **Transition**: reduce size |
| Negative | Backwardation | **Stress**: long volatility favored, mean reversion dangerous |
| Deeply negative | Deep backwardation | **Crisis**: defensive only, mechanical strategies suspended |

This five-regime classification underlies the composite scorecard of Chapter 11G and the conviction score of Chapter 13B. Chapter 9C turns a four-regime version of it into a classifier you can drive, and sets it beside the Edge Book's lower opinion of GEX.

::: practice
For a retail account, the VIX is first an instrument panel. Its level divided by 16 gives the typical daily move the options market is pricing, and the VIX/VIX3M ratio gives the shape of the term structure, below 1 in contango and above 1 in backwardation.

What you can actually hold is different. A long VIX ETP pays the roll in contango before the VIX moves at all: about 10% a year for VXX on the Dealer Mechanics Lab's figure, and roughly twice that for UVXY. An inverse product earns that roll and carries the tail that ended XIV (Chapter 10I).
:::

::: warn When it fails
The replication rests on Step 1 of the derivation, which drops the cube of each daily return. That is harmless on ordinary days but not on a crash day: for a 10% fall the identity gives 0.0107 against a true squared return of 0.0100. A strip with few strikes in the wings, like the six-strike toy, also misses the variance that lies beyond its last strike. Finally, the regime table inherits every weakness of GEX, whose sign rests on an assumption about who holds what (Chapter 8A).
:::

::: warn Common mistake: treating a VIX ETP as the VIX
The VIX spikes in a crash and falls back afterward, and a long VIX ETP does not simply follow it up and down. Between spikes it pays the roll every month, and in stress its futures can sit far from spot VIX. Holding one as a standing hedge is a bet that a spike arrives before the roll has eaten the protection you paid for.
:::

::: rule
- **Read the VIX, and do not hold it by default.** Use its level and the VIX/VIX3M ratio as regime inputs, which both sources endorse.
- **If you trade VIX futures or ETPs**, treat each as a futures position with a roll. A long position must beat the roll, about 10% a year for VXX in contango, before it earns anything, and an inverse position should be sized for a day on which it loses almost everything.
- **If you follow the Edge Book**, do not trade VIX ETPs at all, and take volatility exposure through SPX or XSP options with explicit position management (Chapter 10I).
:::

::: recap
- The VIX is 100 times the square root of a model-free 30-day implied variance, read off a strip of out-of-the-money SPX options weighted by \(\Delta K/K^2\).
- The \(1/K^2\) is the curvature of the log contract. Options plus a daily-rebalanced holding replicate realized variance, so the VIX is the cost of replicating variance through delta-hedged options.
- The VIX grows with the square root of the strip's premium level: double every quote and it rises by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\).
- It moves against SPX, with a correlation of about −0.80, because the steep put wing and the \(1/K^2\) weights re-price a fall more than a rise. Vanna turns a falling market and a rising VIX into dealer selling.
- The VIX itself is not tradable, and its futures and ETPs carry a roll: a cost in contango and a gain in backwardation.
:::

::: check
?? If the VIX goes from 15 to 30, by what factor does the implied variance change, and what daily move does each level price?
!! Variance is volatility squared, so it goes from \(0.15^2 = 0.0225\) to \(0.30^2 = 0.09\), a factor of 4. By the rule of 16, a VIX of 15 prices about \(15/16 = 0.94\%\) a day and a VIX of 30 about 1.9% a day.

?? In the six-strike toy, every quote rises by half. What does the VIX become, and why is it not 11.5 times 1.5?
!! The first line of [eq:vix] scales with the quotes, so \(\sigma^2 = 1.5 \times 0.013226 - 0.0000054 = 0.019834\), and the VIX is \(100\sqrt{0.019834} = 14.1\). The index is a square root of the premium level, so it rises by \(\sqrt{1.5} = 1.22\), not by 1.5.

?? A dealer is short 2,000 SPX puts with a delta of −0.30 each. What is its hedge, and what does a rise in implied volatility make it do?
!! Short puts are long delta, \(2{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.30 = 60{,}000\) share-equivalents, so the hedge is a short of 60,000. The puts are out of the money, so their vanna is negative and a rise in implied volatility makes their delta more negative. The dealer's long delta grows and it sells more, which is the first turn of the vanna loop.

?? A long VIX ETP rolls along a curve that keeps its shape, with the second month at 20.0 and the front month at 19.5. What does it lose in a month with the VIX unchanged, and over a year?
!! A contract bought at 20.0 becomes the front month at 19.5, a loss of \(0.5/20 = 2.5\%\) in the month. Twelve such months leave \(0.975^{12} = 0.74\) of the money, a loss of about 26% from the roll alone, so a steeper contango costs more.

?? Why can the VIX strip builder not show the VIX rising more on a fall than it falls on a rise?
!! It carries its whole smile along with the forward, so after a shock every option has the same implied volatility relative to the forward as before, and the index barely moves: 18.507, 18.505 and 18.503. The asymmetry needs implied volatilities that stay with their strikes, or rise in a fall, so that a fall moves the money onto the steep put side of the smile.
:::
