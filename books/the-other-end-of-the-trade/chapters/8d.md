Everything in Parts I to VII compresses to a single regime question: **is the dealer book net long [[gamma]] or net short gamma?** The answer sets the sign of the feedback loop between hedging and prices, and with it the whole character of intraday price action.

::: plain
A dealer who is long gamma behaves like a thermostat, cooling the room when it warms and heating it when it cools. A dealer who is short gamma behaves like a microphone held too close to its speaker, so that every sound comes back louder. The news that moves prices is the same in both cases. What differs is whether the dealers' hedging pushes back against each move or joins it.
:::

## Two regimes, one mechanism

The two regimes are mirror images of each other, row by row:

| | Dealers long gamma: positive GEX | Dealers short gamma: negative GEX |
|---|---|---|
| Hedging | Sells strength, buys weakness | Buys strength, sells weakness |
| Intraday behavior | **[[Mean reversion]]** | **[[Momentum]]** |
| Volatility | **Compressed** relative to its unhedged level | **Expanded** relative to its unhedged level |
| Favored style | Range trading | [[Trend following]] |
| Premium | Selling it is favored, because dealer hedging suppresses the realized volatility you are betting against | Buying it is favored, because realized volatility is amplified |
| What fails | Breakouts often fail, because the dealer flow fades them | Mean reversion is dangerous: fades against the move get crushed |

A toy example shows the mechanism. Suppose GEX is +$5 billion per 1% move. By Chapter 8A's hedge-flow formula, a 1% rally then has dealers selling about $5 billion of index exposure and a 1% fall has them buying about $5 billion, so each move meets a flow against it. If GEX is −$5 billion instead, the same rally has dealers buying about $5 billion and the same fall has them selling about $5 billion. The news, and the first 1% move it caused, are identical in both cases. Only the sign of the response differs.

The table is the Dealer Mechanics Lab's, and the theory later in this chapter adds two rows to it. The sign of dealer gamma changes liquidity as well as volatility, and neither sign makes prices better informed.

## The mathematics of feedback

Published GEX is positive when hedging is stabilizing, but the feedback maths reads more naturally with the opposite sign. So define the **[[destabilizing gamma]]** \(G\) as the negation of published GEX, in whatever units the model uses. It is positive when dealers are net short gamma, so that their pro-trend hedging amplifies moves. It is negative when they are net long gamma, so that their contra-trend hedging damps moves. With that [[sign convention]], the Dealer Mechanics Lab writes

\[ \sigma_\text{eff}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{(1 - \kappa G)^2} \]
{#eq:eff}

in which each symbol has a plain meaning, and the feedback coefficient \(\kappa\) carries the state of the market:

| symbol | meaning |
|---|---|
| \(\sigma\) | the information volatility: how volatile prices would be from news alone, with no hedging feedback |
| \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) | the **[[effective volatility]]**: the volatility prices show once dealer hedging feeds back into them |
| \(G\) | the destabilizing gamma, the negation of published GEX |
| \(\kappa\) | the feedback coefficient: how far prices move per unit of hedging flow, larger when market depth is thin and when dealers hedge aggressively |

Only the product \(\kappa G\) matters, so the units of \(G\) and \(\kappa\) can be anything that makes it a pure number. Taking square roots, and keeping \(\kappa G\) below 1, gives \(\sigma_\text{eff} = \sigma / (1 - \kappa G)\), and the denominator sorts the regimes:

| \(G\) | Published GEX | Dealers | \(1 - \kappa G\) | Effective volatility | Regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| below 0 | positive | long gamma | above 1 | below \(\sigma\) | **Stabilized** |
| 0 | zero | flat | 1 | equal to \(\sigma\) | **Neutral** |
| above 0 | negative | short gamma | below 1 | above \(\sigma\) | **Amplified** |
| toward \(+1/\kappa\) | strongly negative | deeply short gamma, in extreme stress | toward 0 | toward infinity | **Edge of feedback explosion** |

In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's reading, that divergence is, mathematically, what February 5, 2018 (Volmageddon), March 16, 2020 (the Great Liquidity Crisis) and August 5, 2024 (the yen carry unwind) looked like. Chapter 8H takes the cases in detail and weighs that reading against what regulators and researchers later found.

::: grind Where the formula comes from: a feedback multiplier
One simple model produces [eq:eff] exactly. It is a toy rather than the Dealer Mechanics Lab's derivation, but it shows what \(\kappa\) and \(G\) do.

**Step 1: the first round.** News moves the price by a return \(\varepsilon\), with standard deviation \(\sigma\). Dealers hedge that move, and their trade moves the price by a further \(\kappa G\,\varepsilon\), with the first move if \(G > 0\) and against it if \(G < 0\).

**Step 2: the rounds that follow.** The extra move needs hedging too, which adds \(\kappa G\) times it, \((\kappa G)^2 \varepsilon\), and that needs hedging in turn. When \(\kappa G\) lies between −1 and 1, the rounds shrink and the total is a geometric series:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{move} &= \varepsilon\,\big(1 + \kappa G + (\kappa G)^2 + \cdots\big) \\ &= \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \kappa G} \end{aligned} \]

**Step 3: the volatility.** Dividing a random quantity by a constant divides its standard deviation by the same constant. The total move therefore has standard deviation \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\), and squaring it gives [eq:eff].

**Step 4: over time.** The regime figure spreads the same feedback over successive steps, \(r_t = w\,r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\). A shock then reaches the price as \(\varepsilon_t\) in its own step, \(w\,\varepsilon_t\) in the next and \(w^2\varepsilon_t\) in the one after, for a total of \(\varepsilon_t/(1 - w)\) once the echoes die out. The paths' volatility over many steps follows the same formula, with \(w = \kappa G\).
:::

The next figure draws the grind box's rounds one at a time, in units of the news shock \(\varepsilon\). ::: worked The regime figure, in the formula
**Step 1: the weights.** The regime figure's feedback of 0.45 is \(\kappa G = -0.45\) under positive GEX and \(\kappa G = +0.45\) under negative GEX.

**Step 2: the multipliers.** Under positive GEX the multiplier is \(1/(1 + 0.45) = 0.69\), so hedging removes about 31% of the volatility. Under negative GEX it is \(1/(1 - 0.45) = 1.82\), so hedging adds about 82%. These are the cascade figure's two totals.

**Step 3: the comparison.** The ratio of the two multipliers is \(1.82/0.69 = 2.64\). The regime figure's six paths span about 3.6 points under negative GEX against 1.5 under positive, a ratio of about 2.4. Six paths over 60 steps are too small a sample for a closer match.
:::

::: familiar
The feedback multiplier works like the design effect of a cluster-randomized trial. When outcomes within a cluster are positively correlated, the variance of an estimate is inflated by a factor that grows with the correlation, while a negative correlation would deflate it. Returns work the same way: positive feedback correlates each step with the one before and inflates the variance of the cumulative move, while negative feedback deflates it. The [[variance ratio test]] of Chapter 3B measures exactly this inflation.
:::

### Instant or lagged: what the regimes do to autocorrelation

The grind box summed its rounds within one instant, and the regime figure spread them over successive steps. Both give the same multiplier for the volatility of the cumulative move, \(\sigma/(1 - w)\). They differ on [[autocorrelation]]. If dealers hedge within the instant, each return is larger or smaller than the news, but it carries nothing of the return before: volatility changes and autocorrelation does not. If they hedge one step late, as in the figure, each return carries \(w\) times the one before, and the lag-1 autocorrelation is \(w\) itself.

The table's rows on mean reversion and momentum are therefore the lagged reading, and two lines of research bear on it. Baltussen, Da, Lammers and Martens (2021) study intraday returns on over 60 futures markets from 1974 to 2020. They find that "the return during the last 30 minutes before the market close is positively predicted by the return during the rest of the day". They link that [[intraday momentum]] to the gamma hedging demand of option [[market makers|market maker]] and [[leveraged ETFs|leveraged ETF]]. The predictive power "reverts over the next days".

Grossman and Zhou (1996) model portfolio insurers in general equilibrium. Like a dealer short gamma, an insurer sells as prices fall and buys as they rise, and a later section returns to the insurers of 1987. Insurers sell to other investors in bad states, and to induce those investors to buy, the risk premium must rise. The authors show that portfolio insurance "increases price volatility, causes mean reversion in asset returns". So the price overshoots within the move and partly comes back afterward. Short gamma means momentum within the move, not necessarily over the days that follow.

The measured sizes are small beside the figure's 0.45. In single stocks, Yang (2015), a job-market paper that was not published, finds that moving from the lowest to the highest quintile of option writers' hedging demand raises daily return autocorrelation from −5.0% to −1.6%. That is a change of 3.4 percentage points of autocorrelation, where the figure's weight of 0.45 is an autocorrelation of 45%. Chapter 8G gives the rest of the measured effects.

### The divergence is nonlinear

A linear reading, that at \(G = 0\) effective volatility equals \(\sigma\), understates what makes the formula matter in practice. Fix \(\sigma = 0.15\), a baseline information volatility of 15%, and a small illustrative feedback coefficient \(\kappa = 0.0010\), then sweep \(G\) across the regimes:

| G (positioning regime) | \(1 - \kappa G\) | \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| −800 (deeply stabilizing) | 1.80 | 8.3% |
| −400 (mildly stabilizing) | 1.40 | 10.7% |
| 0 (neutral) | 1.00 | 15.0% |
| +400 (mildly destabilizing) | 0.60 | 25.0% |
| +600 (moderate stress) | 0.40 | 37.5% |
| +800 (acute stress) | 0.20 | **75%** |
| +900 (near-critical) | 0.10 | **150%** |
| +950 | 0.05 | 300% |
| +990 (edge of explosion) | 0.01 | 1,500% |

From \(G = +800\) to \(G = +900\), a roughly 12% increase in destabilizing positioning, \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) doubles from 75% to 150%. **That is the operational signal: small changes in aggregate positioning produce outsized realized-volatility consequences in the stress regime.** The slope of the curve makes this exact: differentiating \(\sigma / (1 - \kappa G)\) with respect to \(G\) gives

\[ \frac{d\sigma_\text{eff}}{dG} = \frac{\kappa\,\sigma}{(1 - \kappa G)^2} \]
{#eq:slope}

::: worked How steep the curve gets
**Step 1: at neutral.** At \(G = 0\), [eq:slope] gives \(0.0010 \times 0.15 = 0.00015\) per unit of \(G\), so a hundred more units of \(G\) add about \(100 \times 0.00015 = 0.015\), or 1.5 points of volatility.

**Step 2: in acute stress.** At \(G = +800\) the denominator is \(0.2^2 = 0.04\), so the slope is \(0.00015/0.04 = 0.00375\), which is 25 times as steep. A straight line from there predicts 37.5 more points for the next 100 units of \(G\).

**Step 3: the curve.** The table's actual rise from \(G = +800\) to \(+900\) is 75 points, double even that, because the slope keeps growing along the way. The local sensitivity of \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) to \(G\) is unbounded as \(G\) approaches \(1/\kappa\).
:::

The Dealer Mechanics Lab draws a lesson for the [[gamma flip]], the level at which estimated dealer gamma crosses zero. It reads the curve as the reason the flip matters as a level, even though no single trade is responsible for it. Read the formula closely and it adds a qualification. At \(G = 0\) damping turns into amplification smoothly, and nothing in [eq:eff] jumps there. The point where the curve turns violent is \(\kappa G = 1\), and it depends on \(\kappa\), which is liquidity, as much as on positioning. No study found tests the flip as a price level (Chapter 8G).

The value \(\kappa = 0.0010\) is only illustrative, since in practice \(\kappa\) is fitted to the regime and varies with depth, with dealer aggressiveness and with how concentrated positioning is. The qualitative shape, slow at the wings and explosive near the critical point, is the universal feature. A later section says what \(\kappa = 0.0010\) and \(G = +800\) mean in dollars.

**A note on signs.** \(G\) is the *negation* of standard published GEX. Suppose a vendor reports "GEX = +$2.3B" under the JPM-era convention, where positive means stabilizing. The corresponding \(G\) in [eq:eff] is −$2.3B. Both ways of signing the number coexist in practice, so check which one your model or vendor uses before you read the sign.

## The theory behind the formula

The formula is older than the Dealer Mechanics Lab. A line of papers from the 1990s derived it, or close relatives of it, from explicit models of hedgers whose trades move prices. Knowing that literature tells you what the formula assumes and where it stops being true.

### Frey and Stremme, and Frey

Frey and Stremme (1997), in *Mathematical Finance*, split a market into two groups. "Reference traders" are price takers with an ordinary demand for the stock. "Program traders" follow a hedging rule for a book of options they have written, and so must trade whenever the price moves. Setting supply equal to demand gives the price process, and the abstract reports "an explicit expression for the transformation of market volatility" under the hedgers' impact:

> It turns out that volatility increases and becomes time and price dependent. The strength of these effects however depends not only on the share of total demand that is due to hedging, but also significantly on the heterogeneity of the distribution of hedged payoffs.
> Frey and Stremme (1997)

In their logarithmic-demand case, as later papers restate it, the volatility becomes \(\sigma/(1 - \rho S \phi_S)\). Here \(\rho\) measures how much the hedgers' trading moves the price, and \(\phi_S\) is how fast their holding changes with the price. That is the book's \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\), with \(\kappa = \rho\) and \(G = S\phi_S\). The last clause of the abstract matters for the rest of this part. A hedged book spread evenly over many strikes changes volatility everywhere a little, while a book concentrated at one strike changes it a lot near that strike and little elsewhere. That is the theoretical root of strike-local effects, [[pinning]] among them (Chapter 8E).

Frey (1998), in *Finance and Stochastics*, gives the cleanest version. One large trader's holding moves the price directly, and the trader replicates a claim by holding its [[delta]]. The attempt produces a nonlinear version of the Black-Scholes equation, whose solution Frey proves exists and is unique.

::: grind Frey's model in one step
**Step 1: the price.** Write \(\alpha_t\) for the large trader's holding of the stock. Frey's price follows
\[ \begin{aligned} dS_t ={}& \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \\ &+ \rho S_t\,d\alpha_t \end{aligned} \]
where \(dW_t\) is the random news of Chapter 7A and \(\rho\) is the "market liquidity parameter": each share the trader buys lifts the price by the fraction \(\rho\).

**Step 2: the hedge.** A trader who replicates a claim worth \(V(t, S)\) holds \(\alpha_t = V_S\), the claim's delta. When the price moves by \(dS\), the holding changes by \(V_{SS}\,dS\), plus terms in \(dt\) that do not affect volatility.

**Step 3: substitute.** The random part of the price move becomes \(dS = \sigma S\,dW + \rho S V_{SS}\,dS\). Collecting the \(dS\) terms gives \(dS\,(1 - \rho S V_{SS}) = \sigma S\,dW\).

**Step 4: the volatility.** Dividing through, the price's volatility is \(\sigma/(1 - \rho S V_{SS})\). A claim with a convex payoff has \(V_{SS} > 0\), so its replicator buys after rises and sells after falls, and volatility goes up. That is a dealer who has sold the claim, or a portfolio insurer who promised it: short gamma, \(G = S V_{SS} > 0\). The equation is singular where \(\rho S V_{SS}\) reaches 1.
:::

State Frey's assumptions whenever you quote the formula: impact is linear and permanent, the hedger rebalances continuously to the delta of a known book, and the feedback stays below its critical value. A later section says how each assumption fails.

### Other derivations, and a counter-result

Three more papers of the 1990s reach the same shape from different starting points.

- **Platen and Schweizer (1998)** add hedgers' demand to noise and speculative demand in an equilibrium model. Hedging makes volatility "endogenously determined by agents' trading behavior", and their simulations "quantitatively substantiate the idea that option price distortions can be induced by feedback effects from hedging strategies". The feedback bends the implied volatility smile and skew of Chapters 5E and 5G, not only realized volatility.
- **Sircar and Papanicolaou (1998)** use reference traders and "programme traders" who hedge by Black-Scholes, and derive a nonlinear pricing equation. Their models "do predict increased implied volatilities with minimal assumptions beyond those of the Black-Scholes theory", and reduce to Black-Scholes when the feedback is removed. When hedgers are a small share of the market, the correction to \(\sigma\) is small, which fits a modest effect on typical days.
- **Schönbucher and Wilmott (2000)** derive the modified price process for an illiquid market and simulate the replication of a put, which is portfolio insurance. The family of models they belong to gives volatility of the form \(\sigma/(1 - \rho\lambda(S) S u_{SS})\), rising as the replicating strategy's convexity grows.

Loeper (2018), in *The Annals of Applied Probability*, gives the rigorous modern version for a hedger whose every trade moves the price linearly. The resulting Black-Scholes equation "is fully non-linear and singular". Loeper shows that it is nonetheless "well posed", and proves the "existence of smooth solutions for a large class of final payoffs". The qualifier "a large class" matters: the equation is still singular, and the results cover payoffs that keep the hedge clear of the singular point.

The sign rule is not a theorem of every model. Basak (1995) models portfolio insurers as optimizing investors in a frictionless general equilibrium. He finds that "the market volatility and risk premium are decreased, and the asset and market price levels increased, by the presence of portfolio insurance". The rule of this chapter comes from models with price impact and limited liquidity. Take those away and the result can reverse.

A newer [[working paper]] by Sornette, Ulmann and Wehrli, posted on SSRN, adds the asymmetry the nonlinear table shows. It finds that option hedging creates short-lived directional instabilities. The effect is "strongly asymmetric, with a significantly more pronounced effect on the spot price when the option market maker is short compared to long the option". The damping side is bounded, and the amplifying side is not.

## Portfolio insurance and October 1987

**[[Portfolio insurance]]** was a strategy of the 1980s that gave a stock portfolio a floor without buying listed puts. The insurer replicated a put by trading, selling stock or index futures as prices fell and buying as they rose. That is a synthetic long put, and its hedging is a short-gamma program: it trades with every move, like a dealer short gamma. It is the historical case of the formula's amplifying side.

The Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms, known as the Brady Commission, reconstructed the market of October 14 to 20, 1987, from trade records. Its report of January 1988 put the assets under portfolio insurance at about $60 billion to $90 billion, "only 2-3% of equity assets outstanding". On October 19, as summaries of the report give it:

- Sell programs by three portfolio insurers made up just under $2 billion of the New York Stock Exchange's sales of just under $21 billion.
- In futures, three portfolio insurers sold the equivalent of $2.8 billion of stock, and portfolio-insurance sales totaled about $6 billion: $4 billion in futures and $2 billion in stock.
- In the first half hour a few insurers sold futures equivalent to just under $400 million of stock, 28% of public volume. Between 11:40 AM and 2:00 PM they sold about 10,000 [[futures contracts|futures contract]].
- Roughly 40% of futures sales by traders other than market makers came from portfolio insurers.

The Commission concluded that futures-based strategies were not the "sole cause" of the fall but "a significant factor in accelerating and exacerbating the declines". One account of the report puts insurance selling at "roughly 15% of total stock and futures sold that day". Rubinstein (1988), a co-founder of a portfolio-insurance firm and so an interested party, estimated "perhaps 12 per cent of the dollar change in net sold positions on October 19". He judged a large causal role unlikely. He also noted that the crash undermined two preconditions of every portfolio-insurance program: low transaction costs and price continuity. Replication fails exactly when prices gap, the same weakness a short-gamma dealer has.

::: worked A small share of assets, a large share of a day
**Step 1: the stock.** At $60 billion to $90 billion, insured assets were 2% to 3% of equities.

**Step 2: the flow.** On October 19 insurers sold about $6 billion. In the first half hour, their futures sales were 28% of public volume.

**Step 3: the lesson.** A flow that is small against what exists can be large against what trades in a given hour. [[Hedge flow]] per 1% move, the unit of GEX, is a flow, and it has to be set against the market's depth at that moment, which is what \(\kappa\) does.
:::

Why so large a fall from so small a share? Gennotte and Leland (1990) put the question in their abstract: "traditional models cannot explain how a relatively small amount of selling could cause so large a price drop". In their model some traders are informed, others infer information from prices, and hedging demand is not fully observed. Markets are then "much less liquid in their model than in traditional models". In the authors' words, "Discontinuities (or 'crashes') can occur even with relatively little hedging". The crash needs the hedging to be unobserved or underestimated. If hedging demand is known, its price impact is much smaller.

Two papers of the same period make the information point from other angles. Grossman (1988) argues that without a traded put there is "less information about the future price volatility associated with current dynamic hedging strategies". As a result, "the stocks' future price volatility can rise because of a current lack of information about the extent to which dynamic hedging strategies are in place". Jacklin, Kleidon and Pfleiderer (1992) show that if the market underestimates dynamic hedging, prices first run above fundamentals, and "the price falls when the amount of portfolio insurance is revealed". Black (1988), in an essay on the crash, noted that futures prices went far below their theoretical values just when portfolio insurers wanted to sell.

The lesson for a GEX reader is double-edged. Listed [[open interest]] is visible, so the hedging it implies can be anticipated, which is Grossman's case for traded options. The hedging a public map cannot see, in FLEX, [[over-the-counter]] and structured-product books, is the kind that did the damage in these models (Chapter 8B).

Kyle and Obizhaeva, in a paper on large bets and stock market crashes in the *Review of Finance*, measured how badly a linear intuition failed in 1987. Portfolio insurers sold $10.48 billion of index futures and $3.27 billion of NYSE stocks from October 15 to 20, $13.75 billion gross. "Conventional wisdom" predicted declines of 0.51% net and 0.63% gross. Their invariance model, calibrated on over 400,000 portfolio transition orders, predicted 16.77%. The actual decline was 32%.

## Two tests of the sign rule

### A measured market: currency options

Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette (2022), in the *Journal of International Money and Finance*, fit a model of this family to real positions. An earlier version was a Swiss National Bank working paper of 2019. Their model has two aggregate traders, an option market maker and an option market taker, "whose exposures reflect the total outstanding positions of all option traders in the market". The net hedge flow moves the spot rate through "a simple linear permanent impact model". Foreign exchange options trade over the counter, with no public open interest, so the authors rebuilt the market maker's gamma from trade-repository records. Their sample runs from October 21, 2017 to June 30, 2018, in EURUSD and USDJPY.

Spot volatility "is increased (decreased) by a negative (positive) gamma exposure" of the market maker. The rebuilt gamma was negative, because option takers were net long options. In size, "a negative gamma exposure of the OMM of approximately -1000 billion USD" leads "to an absolute increase in volatility of 0.7% in EURUSD and 0.9% in USDJPY". Here OMM is the option market maker, and about −1,000 billion dollars is roughly what the authors' reconstruction shows. The gamma is in the authors' own measure, whose unit the abstract does not state. The increases are absolute: 0.7 and 0.9 points of annualized volatility, under one point in the two largest currency pairs. The 2019 version explains the larger USDJPY effect by "the higher market impact of a transaction in the USDJPY spot market", EURUSD being the more liquid. Impact is the book's \(\kappa\): the same dealer gamma does more in the thinner market.

### A simulated market: liquidity and price discovery

Buis, Pieterse-Bloem, Verschoor and Zwinkels (2024), in the *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control*, simulate an order book with an **[[agent-based model]]**. That is a simulation in which many traders follow simple rules and prices emerge from their orders. The authors add delta hedgers with a positive or negative net gamma, and compare normal and stressed conditions. Their findings, in the abstract's words:

- "the presence of dynamic hedgers enhances market liquidity under normal conditions";
- "positive gamma helps sustain liquidity in stressed scenarios, while negative gamma depletes it";
- "an increase in the net gamma positioning of dynamic hedgers reduces volatility and increases market stability, whereas a negative gamma positioning increases volatility and makes the market more prone to failure";
- "Price discovery typically worsens when dynamic hedgers become more prevalent, regardless of the sign of their positioning."

These are the two rows the regime table lacked. Negative gamma drains liquidity in stress, which is how \(\kappa\) rises in the same step as \(G\). Because the two move together, the explosive corner of [eq:eff] is reached faster than either alone suggests. And hedging of either sign worsens **[[price discovery]]**, the process by which trading moves prices to what the available information justifies. A damped, pinned market is not a better-informed one. The authors suggest steering hedgers' net gamma as a policy tool, most useful for illiquid instruments. The simulation's background traders place orders by simple random rules, a "zero-intelligence" order book, so the result is a model's, not an estimate.

A market where the dealer's gamma is measured rather than assumed points the same way. Atanasova and co-authors (2025) study the bitcoin options venue Deribit from January 2021 to December 2024. When market makers' aggregate gamma inventory is negative, they "trade in the same direction as BTC price momentum", and option spreads widen. A one-standard-deviation fall in the measure goes with effective spreads 0.12% wider for [[out-of-the-money]] calls and 0.19% for puts.

## What sets κ: impact, in dollars and points

### The units

Only \(\kappa G\) matters, but a reader holding a GEX number in dollars needs \(\kappa\) in matching units. Take \(G\) in billions of dollars per 1% move, the negation of published GEX. Write \(\lambda\) for the impact of hedge flow: the index points that $1 billion of buying or selling moves the index.

A 1% move is \(0.01S\) points. By Chapter 8A's hedge-flow formula it makes the dealers trade \(G\) billion dollars with the move, and that flow moves the index a further \(\lambda G\) points. The feedback per unit of move is the second move over the first:

\[ \kappa G = \frac{\lambda\,G}{0.01\,S} \]
{#eq:units}

so \(\kappa = \lambda/(0.01S)\) per billion dollars. At \(S = 6{,}000\), a 1% move is 60 points.

::: worked The table in dollars and points
**Step 1: the table's κ.** With \(G\) in billions of dollars per 1% move, \(\kappa = 0.0010\) at [[SPX]] 6,000 means \(\lambda = 0.0010 \times 60 = 0.06\) points per $1 billion.

**Step 2: the table's G.** The acute-stress row, \(G = +800\), is then $800 billion of dealer short gamma per 1% move, and the last row $990 billion. The Dealer Mechanics Lab puts aggregate dealer GEX in the range of $20 billion to $100 billion per 1% move (Chapter 8A), so the table's stress rows sit far above any total it quotes.

**Step 3: a larger impact.** Take instead \(\lambda = 1\) point per $1 billion, the starting impact of the pinning figure in Chapter 8E. Then \(\kappa = 1/60\) per billion, and the acute-stress \(\kappa G = 0.8\) needs only \(0.8 \times 60 = \$48\) billion of dealer short gamma per 1% move.
:::

The same rows, restated at the two impacts. \(G\) is in billions of dollars per 1% move, so a negative \(G\) is a positive published GEX.

| \(\kappa G\) | \(\sigma_\text{eff}\) at \(\sigma = 15\%\) | \(G\) at 0.06 points per $1B | \(G\) at 1 point per $1B |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −0.4 | 10.7% | −$400B | −$24B |
| +0.4 | 25.0% | +$400B | +$24B |
| +0.8 | 75% | +$800B | +$48B |
| +0.9 | 150% | +$900B | +$54B |

Now run the check the other way. Take a constant \(\lambda\) of 1 point per $1 billion, and dealers short $20 billion to $100 billion of gamma per 1% move. That gives \(\kappa G\) from 0.33 to 1.67: amplifying at the bottom of the range and past the explosion point at the top. Nothing in the evidence shows the index behaving that way on ordinary days (Chapter 8G). Either impact per dollar of hedge flow is smaller and more curved than a constant \(\lambda\), or the flow actually hedged is much smaller than gross GEX, or both. What impact research says about \(\lambda\) decides which.


### What impact research says

The idea of one number for impact per unit of flow goes back to Kyle (1985). In his model of an informed trader, the market makers' price rises in proportion to net [[order flow]]. The slope, known as Kyle's lambda, is the price move per unit of flow, and market depth is its inverse. In that model the slope is set by how much information the flow might carry. Hedge flow is mechanical, and a market that can recognize it has less reason to move far on it, so its impact tends to be smaller and to fade.

Measured impact is not linear in size. The price impact of a metaorder, a large order worked in pieces, grows roughly as the square root of its size, the law of Chapter 2D, [eq:2d/sqrt]. In the form the research uses,

\[ I \approx Y\,\sigma_D\sqrt{\frac{Q}{V_D}} \]
{#eq:sqrtimpact}

where \(I\) is the move, \(Q\) the size of the order, \(V_D\) the daily volume and \(\sigma_D\) the daily volatility, with \(Y\) a constant of order one. Sato and a co-author survey all liquid Tokyo Stock Exchange stocks over eight years. They find the exponent "equal to 1/2 within statistical errors at both the individual stock level and the individual trader level". Bucci and co-authors (2019) find "a clear crossover between a linear market impact regime and a square-root regime" in 8 million US institutional trades. Zarinelli and co-authors (2015) find that the square root fits "only across about two orders of magnitude in order size", a logarithm fitting better across five. They also find that impact relaxes on average to "approximately 2/3 of the peak impact". Tóth and co-authors (2011) trace the law to a latent order book that "vanishes around the current price".

::: worked A day's hedge flow under the square root
**Step 1: the assumptions.** These inputs are assumptions for illustration, not measurements: a daily volatility of 1%, about 16% a year; $500 billion of S&P 500 exposure traded a day; and \(Y\) between 0.5 and 1.

**Step 2: $1 billion.** By [eq:sqrtimpact], \(\sqrt{1/500} = 0.045\), so a $1 billion flow over a day moves the index by \(Y \times 1\% \times 0.045\). For \(Y\) from 0.5 to 1 that is 2.2 to 4.5 bps, or 1.3 to 2.7 points at 6,000.

**Step 3: $10 billion.** Now \(\sqrt{10/500} = 0.14\), a move of 7 to 14 bps, or 4 to 8.5 points. Ten times the flow moves the index about three times as far.

**Step 4: the loop gain.** Dealers short $20 billion to $100 billion per 1% move would, on these assumptions, add 0.10 to 0.45 of each 1% move. That is a gain of 0.10 to 0.45, against 0.33 to 1.67 with a constant 1 point per $1 billion.
:::

Four more findings bracket \(\lambda\) for the index:

- **The permanent anchor.** Gabaix and Koijen (2021), in an NBER working paper, estimate that "investing $1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5", a price elasticity of demand of 0.2. It is an estimate for persistent flows over a quarter, and it is contested. At an assumed US market value of $55 trillion, $1 billion of persistent flow would add about $5 billion, roughly 1 bp or half an index point at 6,000. Hedge flow that later reverses leaves less than that behind, while its transient impact is larger.
- **Predictable flows.** Brøgger (2021) finds that the daily rebalancing of leveraged volatility products is predictable in size, sign and timing. Yet "larger and more predictable flows have smaller price impact coefficients, and ... there are no excess profits from trading ahead of rebalancing flows". A flow everyone can see coming is partly absorbed in advance, which is Gennotte and Leland's point from the other side.
- **Stress.** Kyle and Obizhaeva's crash comparisons show how far impact can rise. On May 6, 2010, a seller's program of 75,000 E-mini contracts, about $4.1 billion, coincided with a 5.12% drop in five minutes, from 1,113 to 1,056, against an invariance prediction of 0.61%. In 1987 the actual fall was about twice the prediction, and in 2010 about eight times. The authors read the larger-than-predicted drops as a sign that "rapid selling exacerbates transitory price impact".
- **Depth over time.** A working paper by Boyarchenko, Larsen, Venter and Whelan is described in Larsen's 2024 doctoral thesis. It finds that "the depth and dealer number of the S&P 500 futures contract have decreased by 80% over a ten-year period from 2012 to 2022". It is unpublished, and the figure depends on how it defines depth. If it holds, the same GEX moves the index more than it did a decade before, so GEX numbers from different years are not comparable without a depth adjustment.

No published estimate gives the impact of dealer hedge flow in SPX in points per $1 billion. The pinning figure's range of 0 to 3 points per $1 billion spans the calm-day bracket for flows worked over a day. Stress would need values several times higher.

::: worked Measured effects, read as loop gains
**Step 1: [[0DTE]] options.** Amaya, Garcia-Ares, Pearson and Vasquez (2025), a working paper distributed by Cboe, bound the effect of market makers' 0DTE gamma on S&P 500 volatility (Chapter 8G gives the study). It adds at most 3.3 points of annualized daily volatility, and 6.4 points over 30-minute periods. Assume an information volatility of 15%. Then \(\sigma_\text{eff}/\sigma\) is at most \(18.3/15 = 1.22\), which is \(1/(1 - \kappa G)\) for \(\kappa G = 1 - 15/18.3 = 0.18\). Over 30 minutes, \(1 - 15/21.4 = 0.30\).

**Step 2: a leveraged-ETF episode.** Zhao (2026), an arXiv preprint, estimates that in Korea in 2026, self-reinforcing leveraged-ETF rebalancing "raised SK Hynix's annualized volatility from 100.0% to 136.7% over nine weeks". That is a multiplier of 1.367, a gain of \(1 - 1/1.367 = 0.27\).

**Step 3: the comparison.** Both gains are far below 1, and far below the table's stress rows. No study found estimates an SPX feedback gain near the singular point.
:::

## What the formula leaves out

Each of the formula's assumptions fails in a known way, and each failure has a literature:

1. **Linear, permanent impact.** Impact is concave and partly transient, as above.
2. **Continuous hedging of a known book.** Almgren and Li (2016) find that an optimal hedger "trades smoothly towards the classical Black-Scholes delta, with trading intensity proportional to instantaneous mishedge and inversely proportional to illiquidity". Aubert, Chevalier and Ly Vath (2025) model a market maker whose hedging has transient as well as permanent impact, and who hedges in discrete bursts rather than continuously. Either way the flow is spread out and lumpy, not delivered at each tick.
3. **Everyone else's demand is unchanged.** Other traders anticipate predictable flow and trade against it, as Brøgger's result shows, so gross mechanical flow is not net price pressure.
4. **Feedback below its critical value.** Near \(\kappa G = 1\) the smooth model gives way to gaps and crashes, as in Gennotte and Leland's model and Kyle and Obizhaeva's comparisons.

Two further omissions matter for anyone reading a map of open-interest gamma. The first is new trades. Egebjerg and Kokholm (2024), in an SSRN working paper, split the impact of market makers' hedging in two. The "gamma effect" is rebalancing an unchanged book as spot moves, and the "inventory effect" is hedging new option trades. Studying the gamma effect alone "can underestimate the total effect when the OMM's inventory adjustments reinforce the gamma impact and overestimate it when the adjustments move in the opposite direction". In SPX data, both components "are significant and with impact in line with the model predictions". A burst of customer call buying is hedged at once and moves the index even when the stock of gamma has not changed. A map of yesterday's open interest shows only the first effect.

The second omission is other hedgers. \(G\) in the formula is everyone who trades with the move, not only option dealers. A leveraged or inverse ETF with assets \(A\) and multiple \(L\) trades \(L(L-1)xA\) after a day's return \(x\), always with the move (Chapter 8H). Tuzun (2013), in a Federal Reserve working paper, estimated for the fund assets of that time that "A 1% increase in broad stock-market indexes induces LETFs to originate rebalancing flows equivalent to $1.04 billion worth of stock". Volatility-targeting funds and trend followers also trade with moves, on longer lags. Target-date funds trade against them: Parker, Schoar and Sun (2023) find that they move retirement investors into "macrocontrarian strategies that sell stocks after relatively good stock market performance". Dealer GEX is one term in the market's net \(G\), and a map of option gamma shows none of the others.

## How large the feedback is in practice

The dealer-feedback story is real and quantifiable, but its practical effect is modest on a typical day. Chapter 8G reviews the studies that measure it and sets them side by side in their own units. They run from Barbon and Buraschi's intraday momentum and reversal in stocks to the 0DTE studies of Dim, Eraker and Vilkov and of Adams and co-authors.

In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's reading, the episodes that everyone remembers are February 2018, March 2020, the GME squeeze and the August 5, 2024 unwind. They are the **right tail** of this distribution, where feedback briefly approached the unstable threshold (Chapters 8H and 8I). On most days, GEX is a probabilistic prior you can use to bias your strategy selection, not a deterministic forecast.

What to do with a regime reading is Chapter 8J's subject, and the book's two sources weigh it differently. The Dealer Mechanics Lab pairs the sign of GEX with the shape of the [[VIX]] [[term structure]] as a decision boundary for choosing strategies. The Edge Book keeps the sign as a low-confidence filter, worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight, and ignores it when it disagrees with the term structure (Chapter 10H). Chapter 8J sets out both, and turns the regime into a multiplier on size rather than a trigger for trades.

::: practice
In an account, the regime changes size and stops before it changes which trades you take. A stop set at two standard deviations of a day's move sits at about 1.9% when effective volatility is 15%, because a day's standard deviation is then \(15\%/\sqrt{252} = 0.94\%\) (Chapter 5E). At 29%, the top of the explorer's default stress run, the same rule puts the stop at about 3.7%, and a stop left at 1.9% is hit by ordinary noise. Size falls for the same reason: with the stop nearly twice as far away, the same dollar risk buys about half the position.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: Stops and size across the regime. The reader drags effective volatility from 10% to 60%, and sets a stop in daily standard deviations and a dollar risk budget. The figure shows the stop's distance in percent and the position size that keeps the dollar risk fixed. It shows the stop widening and the size shrinking together as the regime turns.]

::: warn When it fails
The regime read fails in three ways. The sign itself can be wrong, since it rests on a convention about who holds what (Chapter 8B). \(\kappa\) is not a constant: when depth vanishes, the same destabilizing gamma that meant mild amplification can approach the singular point, where a linear intuition badly understates the move (Chapter 8H). And the formula's own assumptions, linear permanent impact and continuous hedging of a known book, fail most in the episodes where it predicts the most.
:::

::: rule
Read the regime as a statement about volatility, and convert before you compute:

- **Convert the sign first.** \(G\) is minus published GEX, and published GEX carries a vendor's convention (Chapter 8B). Check both before you read either.
- **Use the formula for direction and shape, not for a number.** No published estimate gives \(\kappa\) for SPX hedge flow, so do not turn a GEX number into a volatility forecast.
- **Watch depth as closely as positioning.** The same \(G\) matters more on a thin day, when \(\kappa\) is high, and thin depth and short gamma tend to arrive together.
- **Size stops from effective volatility**, as in the practice callout, and cut size when dealers are short gamma and the market is thin at the same time.
- **Leave the choice of strategy to Chapter 8J**, where the sign is a size multiplier, not a trigger.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: reading the regime as a forecast
Positive GEX does not mean the market cannot move: on most days the regime is only a probabilistic prior. The episodes that did damage were the right tail, when the feedback briefly approached its unstable threshold. A sizing rule has to survive those episodes, not only the typical day.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: mixing up G and GEX
The feedback formula uses \(G\), the negation of published GEX. Put a vendor's "+$2.3B" into it unconverted, and a stabilizing regime reads as an amplifying one. Convert the sign first, and then check the vendor's convention from Chapter 8B as well. A dollar GEX also needs an impact in points per dollar before it means anything in [eq:eff], by [eq:units].
:::

::: recap
- Long dealer gamma (positive GEX) makes hedging trade against moves: mean reversion and compressed volatility. Short dealer gamma (negative GEX) makes it trade with them: momentum and expanded volatility.
- With destabilizing gamma \(G = -\text{GEX}\), effective volatility is \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\): damped for \(G < 0\), amplified for \(G > 0\), and unbounded as \(G\) nears \(1/\kappa\). Its slope grows as \(1/(1 - \kappa G)^2\), so small changes in positioning matter most in stress.
- The formula is Frey and Stremme's (1997) and Frey's (1998), from models with linear, permanent impact and continuous hedging of a known book. The sign rule holds in models with impact and limited liquidity, not in every equilibrium model.
- In dollars, \(\kappa G = \lambda G/(0.01S)\). The illustrative \(\kappa = 0.001\) is 0.06 points per $1 billion, and its stress rows are hundreds of billions per 1%. Measured impact is concave, lower for predictable flow and several times higher in fast one-sided selling.
- Measured effects are modest on typical days and large only in the right tail. What to do with the regime is Chapter 8J's subject.
:::

!! Since \(1 - 0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.6\), effective volatility is \(0.20/0.6 = 33\%\). A positive \(G\) is negative published GEX: dealers are net short gamma, and their hedging amplifies moves.

?? With \(\sigma = 0.15\) and \(\kappa = 0.0010\), how much does effective volatility rise when \(G\) moves from +400 to +600, and when it moves from −600 to −400?
!! From +400 to +600 it rises from 25% to 37.5%, which is 12.5 points. From −600 to −400 it rises from \(0.15/1.6 = 9.4\%\) to \(0.15/1.4 = 10.7\%\), about 1.3 points. The same 200 units of positioning do about ten times as much on the destabilizing side.

?? Why do the regime figure's negative-GEX paths trend, although the random shocks have no drift?
!! Each return carries +0.45 times the one before, so a move tends to be followed by another in the same direction, which is positive autocorrelation, or momentum. Over many steps each shock's effect on the price is multiplied by \(1/(1 - 0.45) = 1.82\).

?? A vendor shows GEX of −$15 billion per 1% move with SPX at 6,000. If each $1 billion of hedge flow moves the index 2 points, what is \(\kappa G\), and what does [eq:eff] give for an information volatility of 16%? Why should you not trust the answer?
!! \(G = +15\), so by [eq:units] \(\kappa G = 2 \times 15/60 = 0.5\), and effective volatility is \(16\%/0.5 = 32\%\). The answer rests on a constant impact of 2 points per $1 billion, which no study measures for SPX. Real impact is concave and partly transient, the flow actually hedged can be smaller than gross GEX, and the sign itself rests on the vendor's convention.

?? Portfolio insurance covered only 2% to 3% of equity assets in 1987. How could it matter so much?
!! What matters is the flow against what trades at the moment, not the stock against what exists. On October 19 insurers sold about $6 billion, and in the first half hour their futures sales were 28% of public volume. Gennotte and Leland add that when the market cannot see the hedging, it is far less liquid than traditional models assume, so relatively little hedging can cause a crash.

?? In Frey's model, where does the formula break, and what does the break mean?
!! Where \(\rho S V_{SS}\), the book's \(\kappa G\), reaches 1. There the nonlinear Black-Scholes equation is singular: the hedger's own trades would move the price as much as the move they respond to, and the smooth price process no longer exists. Models that go past that point, such as Gennotte and Leland's, produce gaps and crashes rather than a very high but finite volatility.
