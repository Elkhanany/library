A factor tilt modulates the equity [[sleeve]] to capture a documented, systematic excess return. The five factors in this chapter are quality, momentum, value, low volatility and size. Each has a different mechanism, a different risk of decay and a different drawdown profile. Most of them had their best decade in academic papers, and live retail experience is humbler. They are worth an allocation in moderate amounts, as tilts rather than concentrations, but harvesting them takes patience measured in decades.

::: plain
Sort every stock in the market by one trait, such as how profitable the company is or how much its price rose last year. If the stocks at one end of the sort have earned more than those at the other end over many decades, the trait is a factor. A tilt holds a little more of the favored end than the index does. It is a lean, not a bet on a few names, and it pays off, if at all, over decades rather than quarters.
:::

## What a factor is

An **[[equity factor]]** is a trait by which stocks can be ranked, where the ranking has predicted returns. Researchers measure one with a **[[long-short portfolio]]**. They buy the stocks at the favored end of the ranking and sell short an equal dollar amount of those at the other end. The difference in return is the factor's return, and it is close to independent of whether the market as a whole rose or fell.

::: worked Ten stocks, one sort
**Step 1: rank.** Rank ten stocks by some trait and form two groups: the top two and the bottom two.

**Step 2: hold both sides.** Hold $10,000 of the top group and sell short $10,000 of the bottom group. Over the next month the top group returns 1.4% and the bottom group 0.2%.

**Step 3: the factor return.** The long side makes $140 and the short side loses $20, for a net $120. That is 1.2% on the $10,000 of each side: the factor paid 1.2% that month.

**Step 4: repeat.** Re-rank every month and record the difference. Decades of those monthly differences are the factor's record.
:::

Written as a formula, the factor's return in month \(t\) is

\[ F_t = R^{\text{top}}_t - R^{\text{bottom}}_t \]
{#eq:factor}

where \(R^{\text{top}}_t\) and \(R^{\text{bottom}}_t\) are the average returns of the two groups. With the numbers above, \(F_t = 1.4\% - 0.2\% = 1.2\%\). Each factor below is a version of [eq:factor] with its own ranking.

A **tilt** is the version you can hold. Instead of a long-short portfolio, you own a fund that holds more of the favored stocks than their share of the index, and nothing short. Its return is roughly the market's plus a fraction of the factor's.

### How to read the evidence

Factor papers report average monthly returns, [[Sharpe ratios|Sharpe ratio]] and t-statistics. You know the t-statistic from trials: an estimated effect divided by its standard error. For a factor it has a simple link to the Sharpe ratio, and that link explains why factor evidence needs decades of data.

::: grind From a Sharpe ratio to a t-statistic
**Step 1: the t-statistic of a mean.** With \(n\) monthly returns of mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s\), the standard error of the mean is \(s/\sqrt{n}\). So \(t = m\sqrt{n}/s\).

**Step 2: bring in the Sharpe ratio.** A long-short factor is already an excess return, so its monthly Sharpe ratio is \(m/s\). That makes \(t = \text{SR}_{\text{month}}\sqrt{n}\).

**Step 3: annualize.** The annual Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR}_{\text{month}}\sqrt{12}\) (Chapter 1D), and \(Y\) years hold \(n = 12Y\) months. Substituting both, the factors of \(\sqrt{12}\) cancel and leave the result below.
:::

\[ t = \text{SR} \times \sqrt{Y} \]
{#eq:tstat}

Here SR is the annual Sharpe ratio and \(Y\) the number of years of data. A factor with a Sharpe ratio of 0.4 reaches \(t = 2\) after 25 years, since \(0.4 \times \sqrt{25} = 2\). With only 9 years of data, the same factor shows \(t = 0.4 \times 3 = 1.2\), which no trialist would call evidence. Published t-statistics come from particular samples and constructions, so treat [eq:tstat] as a guide to orders of magnitude rather than a way to recompute them.

[FIGURE IDEA: Years to evidence. The reader sets an annual Sharpe ratio from 0.1 to 1.0, and the figure plots the t-statistic against years of data, with horizontal lines at 2 and 3. A second slider adds a number of factors tried, and moves the bar upward as the deflated Sharpe ratio of Chapter 1D would. ## Quality

::: plain
Profitable, stable, growing companies with conservative investment have outperformed unprofitable, volatile, declining ones. Part of the reason is behavioral: investors over-extrapolate growth in junky names. Part is a risk premium: quality companies carry less risk of financial distress.
:::

::: worked Two companies
**Step 1: the two stocks.** Stock A has a 25% [[return on equity]], stable margins and a conservative balance sheet. Stock B has volatile earnings, a growth-story narrative and a leveraged balance sheet.

**Step 2: their prices.** Stock B trades at 10 times its earnings, a [[price-to-earnings ratio]] (P/E) of 10, and Stock A at 18 times. B looks the cheaper of the two.

**Step 3: what follows.** Empirically, a stock like A outperforms over periods of 5 years or more. B's "growth story" tends to disappoint, while A keeps compounding.
:::

### What quality means

Quality is multi-dimensional. Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019, *Review of Accounting Studies*, RAS) build their **quality minus junk** (QMJ) factor around four pillars:

- **Profitability:** gross profits divided by assets, return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE).
- **Growth:** the stability and the rate of growth of profits.
- **Safety:** low beta, low leverage and low volatility of earnings.
- **Payout:** stable dividends and share buybacks.

Return on equity is a year's profit divided by the shareholders' stake in the company, its book equity. Return on assets divides the same profit by everything the company owns. Holding high-quality stocks long and junky stocks short gives the QMJ factor.

Four pillars have to become one ranking. A simple way to combine them puts each measure on a common scale with a z-score, the number of standard deviations it sits from the average stock, and averages the four:

\[ Q = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(z_{\text{prof}} + z_{\text{grow}} + z_{\text{safe}} + z_{\text{pay}}\right) \]
{#eq:quality}

A stock whose pillars sit at +1.2, +0.4, +0.8 and −0.4 standard deviations scores \(Q = (1.2 + 0.4 + 0.8 - 0.4)/4 = 0.5\). Ranking every stock by \(Q\) gives the sort of [eq:factor].

### The evidence

- **Novy-Marx (2013, *Journal of Financial Economics*, JFE)**, "The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium", won the 2013 Fama-DFA Prize.
- **Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019)** report a QMJ [[alpha]] of 0.66% a month in the US and 0.45% globally, across about 24 countries. Alpha is the return beyond what standard factor models explain (Chapter 1D), and 0.66% a month is roughly 8% a year.
- **Quality was the single factor that did not crash through the value drawdown of 2017 to 2022**, a key test in real markets rather than in a backtest.

### Why it exists

The mechanism is mixed:

- **A risk-premium component:** quality firms carry lower distress risk, and investors might rationally pay a premium for safety.
- **A behavioral component:** investors overpay for a narrative of growth and underpay for boring profitability.

The mixed source explains why quality has held up better than purely behavioral factors. Part of the premium is risk-based and does not decay; part is behavioral and might (Chapter 1G).

::: practice
Three funds bring quality into a retail account:

- **QUAL**, the iShares MSCI USA Quality ETF, with a 0.15% expense ratio, tracks the MSCI Quality Index.
- **COWZ**, the Pacer US Cash Cows 100 ETF, with a 0.49% expense ratio, buys a high [[free cash flow]] yield and holds a more concentrated list.
- **DGRO**, the iShares Core Dividend Growth ETF, is a proxy through the stability of dividends.

An **[[expense ratio]]** is a fund's yearly charge as a share of your holding: 0.15% costs $15 a year on $10,000. QUAL is a *partial* implementation of academic quality. It follows MSCI's index methodology, which captures perhaps 60% to 70% of the academic factor premium. That is still meaningful, and it is close to the live factor portfolio after fees and execution. The difference between QUAL and COWZ is breadth: QUAL holds a broad index, while COWZ concentrates on one pillar, cash generation, in fewer names.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Through the value drawdown of 2017 to 2022, quality's drawdowns were much smaller than those of other factors, but no factor is permanently safe. A regime that rewards risky, junky names, such as the meme-stock rally of 2020 and 2021, can produce one to two years in which quality underperforms.
:::

::: rule
Give quality 5% to 15% of the equity sleeve, through QUAL or COWZ. Do not over-concentrate: tilt toward quality rather than replacing SPX with it.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "quality is just a fancy way to buy expensive stocks"
Quality and value are *distinct* factors with different mechanisms. The QMJ construction of Asness and colleagues controls for valuation, and the quality premium exists *after* you control for value. Quality is not growth dressed up. It is a separate dimension, about fundamentals such as profitability and stability, where value is about price, such as the P/E ratio and the ratio of price to book value.
:::

## Momentum

::: plain
Stocks that have outperformed over the past 6 to 12 months tend to keep outperforming over the next 1 to 3 months. The mechanism is behavioral: investors under-react to news, then over-react at the extremes of a trend. The over-reaction is what creates the risk of a crash when a trend reverses.
:::

This is **cross-sectional** [[momentum]], which compares stocks with one another. Its cousin is the time-series momentum of Chapter 10D, which trades each asset on its own past. In the language of Chapter 3B, both are a positive weight on past returns.

::: worked Winners minus losers
**Step 1: the sort.** Sort all S&P 500 stocks by their total return over the last 12 months, measured as of last month. The most recent month is skipped, because it has reversal effects of its own.

**Step 2: the portfolio.** Buy the top decile, the 50 best performers, and sell short the bottom decile, the 50 worst. This is the canonical "winners minus losers" momentum factor.

**Step 3: one month of it.** Suppose the winners return 2.0% next month and the losers 0.8%. The factor earns \(2.0\% - 0.8\% = 1.2\%\).
:::

The factor is called UMD, for up minus down:

\[ \text{UMD}_t = R^{\text{Winners}}_t - R^{\text{Losers}}_t \]
{#eq:umd}

The winners are the top decile by return over the past 12 months, skipping the most recent month, and the losers are the bottom decile.

### The evidence

- **Jegadeesh and Titman (1993, *Journal of Finance*, JF)** is the canonical study: UMD earned about 1% a month, with a t-statistic of about 3 and a Sharpe ratio of about 0.5 unlevered. Unlevered means as the long-short portfolio stands, without borrowing to scale it up.
- **Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013, JF)**, "Value and Momentum Everywhere", found momentum pervasive across 8 asset classes globally.
- **Daniel and Moskowitz (2016, JFE)** add the *critical caveat*: momentum lost 73% from March to May 2009, during the V-bottom that ended a bear market.
- **Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023, JF)** document a decline of about 47% after publication, across hundreds of factors.

### Why it exists

Momentum is mostly behavioral, Source 2 of Chapter 1G:

- **Under-reaction to news:** investors anchor on old information.
- **Over-reaction at trend extremes:** investors chase what has already risen.
- **Possibly compensation for crash risk** in V-shaped reversals.

The crash risk has a mechanical side, which a toy example shows.

::: worked Why momentum crashes at a market bottom
**Step 1: after a bear market.** Suppose the market has fallen for a year. The losers decile is full of the stocks that fell hardest, which tend to be the most sensitive to the market: say a [[beta]] of 1.8. The winners are the stocks that held up, with a beta of, say, 0.6.

**Step 2: the portfolio's beta.** Long the winners and short the losers, the portfolio has a beta of \(0.6 - 1.8 = -1.2\). A factor meant to be neutral to the market has become a large bet that the market keeps falling.

**Step 3: the rebound.** The market then rallies 30% in three months. From beta alone, the winners rise \(0.6 \times 30\% = 18\%\) and the losers \(1.8 \times 30\% = 54\%\). The long-short portfolio loses \(54\% - 18\% = 36\%\) before any of the factor's own return.
:::

The numbers are hypothetical, but the direction is the one the evidence records. When the market reverses sharply at a bear-market bottom, the losers you are short rally violently and the winners you are long collapse.

The figure tracks the betas of the winners and losers deciles as the sort is refreshed each month, the long-short portfolio's net beta, and its cumulative return. ::: practice
Two funds implement momentum:

- **MTUM**, the iShares MSCI USA Momentum ETF, with a 0.15% expense ratio.
- **PDP**, the Invesco DWA Momentum ETF.

Momentum has high **[[turnover]]**, about 80% of the portfolio a year, and that makes it tax-inefficient. In a taxable account, the turnover generates [[short-term capital gains|short-term capital gain]], taxed at your ordinary income rate, which drag significantly on after-tax returns. Suppose, hypothetically, a sleeve gains $10,000 in a year and its trading realizes $8,000 of that as short-term gains. At a short-term rate of 35%, the tax is $2,800, or 2.8% of a $100,000 sleeve. An index fund held for years realizes almost none of its gain and defers the tax. Momentum is best held in a tax-advantaged account, such as a retirement account (Chapter 2E).
:::

::: warn When it fails
**Momentum crashes.** When the market reverses sharply, as a bear market bottoms, the losers you were short rally violently and the winners you were long collapse. Daniel and Moskowitz documented a loss of 73% in 3 months in 2009.

Their vol-scaled, or dynamic, construction reduces exposure when crash conditions are flagged. This roughly doubles the unconditional Sharpe ratio, the one measured over all periods together, but it is harder to implement in a retail account.
:::

::: rule
Give momentum 5% to 10% of the equity sleeve, through MTUM. That is smaller than quality, because momentum has a higher risk of decay and worse tax behavior. Two kill criteria apply:

- **After a sustained equity drawdown of 6 months or more**, momentum's crash risk is elevated. Cut the exposure by 50% until the equity market's 3-month return turns positive.
- **If MTUM's tracking error to the academic UMD factor exceeds 4% a year**, switch to another vehicle.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "momentum has worked for 30 years, so it will keep working"
Momentum has decayed materially since publication: Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023) document a decline of about 47%. It can also produce drawdowns of 70% in 3 months. The strategy still has a positive expected return, but a smaller one, with worse path properties, than data from the 1990s suggests.
:::

## Value

::: plain
Cheap stocks, those with a low price relative to fundamentals such as book value, earnings or cash flow, outperform expensive stocks over long horizons. Why is debated. The candidates are a risk premium for distress, a behavioral over-extrapolation of growth, and a problem measuring intangible assets under modern accounting.
:::

::: worked Cheap against expensive
**Step 1: the ratio.** For each stock, divide the book value of its equity by its market value. This is the **[[book-to-market ratio]]** (B/M): a high B/M means the market prices the company cheaply relative to its accounts.

**Step 2: the portfolio.** Sort the S&P 500 by B/M, buy the top decile, the cheap stocks, and sell short the bottom decile, the expensive ones. This is the canonical factor of Fama and French, HML, for high minus low.

**Step 3: its history.** It produced reliable excess returns from 1927 to 2010, and then went into the worst drawdown in modern history.
:::

The factor's formula is [eq:factor] with book-to-market as the ranking:

\[ \text{HML}_t = R^{\text{High B/M}}_t - R^{\text{Low B/M}}_t \]
{#eq:hml}

Modern variations rank on a composite of several valuation ratios, and may control for sector exposure:

- **E/P**, earnings divided by price, the inverse of the P/E ratio.
- **B/M**, book value divided by market value.
- **FCF/P**, free cash flow (FCF) divided by price.
- **EV/EBITDA**, the enterprise value (EV) of the whole company, its shares plus its debt less its cash, divided by its earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA). Here a *low* ratio marks a cheap company.

### The evidence

- **Fama and French (1993, JFE)** is the canonical study: HML earned 0.40% to 0.50% a month, with a t-statistic of about 3 and a long-run Sharpe ratio of about 0.4.
- **2010 to 2020 brought the worst documented value drawdown in modern history.** AQR Equity Market Neutral lost about 38% from peak to trough by December 2020.
- **Arnott, Harvey, Kalesnik and Linnainmaa (2021, *Journal of Portfolio Management*, JPM)**, "Reports of Value's Death May Be Greatly Exaggerated", attribute the drawdown to a widening of the valuation spread, which is cyclical, not to fundamentals.
- **The realized rebound:** AQR Absolute Return gained 43.5% in 2022 and 18.5% in 2023, and the Russell 1000 Value index gained 24.9% in 2024.

The **[[valuation spread]]** is how much more expensive the expensive stocks are than the cheap ones. The claim of Arnott and colleagues is easiest to see with numbers.

::: worked Losing without anything going wrong
**Step 1: the starting spread.** Suppose the cheap stocks trade at 1 times their book value and the expensive stocks at 4 times. The valuation spread is \(4/1 = 4\).

**Step 2: the fundamentals.** Over five years, both groups grow their book value at the same rate, so the businesses favor neither side.

**Step 3: the spread widens.** Investors bid the expensive stocks up to 6 times book, while the cheap stocks stay at 1. Relative to the expensive side, the cheap side has lost \(1 - 4/6 = 33\%\), although nothing went wrong in its businesses.

**Step 4: the spread narrows again.** If the spread later returns to 4, the same arithmetic runs backward. The cheap side gains \(6/4 - 1 = 50\%\) relative to the expensive side.
:::

::: grind Where the spread enters value's return
Write each group's price as its multiple \(m\) times its book value \(B\). Over a period, the ratio of the cheap group's growth to the expensive group's is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1 + R_c}{1 + R_e}
&= \frac{m_{c,1}B_{c,1}/(m_{c,0}B_{c,0})}{m_{e,1}B_{e,1}/(m_{e,0}B_{e,0})}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(c\) marks the cheap group, \(e\) the expensive group, 0 the start and 1 the end. Define the valuation spread as \(V = m_e/m_c\) and each group's book growth as \(1 + g = B_1/B_0\). Regrouping the multiples and the book values gives
:::

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1 + R_c}{1 + R_e}
&= \frac{V_0}{V_1} \times \frac{1 + g_c}{1 + g_e}
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:spread}

Value's return against growth stocks is the change in the valuation spread times the difference in fundamental growth. In the example, \(g_c = g_e\) and the spread goes from 4 to 6, so the ratio is \(4/6 = 0.667\): the one-third loss. A widening spread can swamp years of fundamental advantage, and a narrowing one can repay it quickly.

Two bars show the part of value's relative return from the change in spread and the part from fundamentals, with their product as the total. ### Why it exists

The source of the value premium is disputed:

- **The risk-premium view (Fama and French):** value stocks bear the risk of financial distress.
- **The behavioral view (Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny):** investors over-extrapolate growth and underpay for boring value.
- **The intangibles view (Lev and Srivastava):** book-to-market is mismeasured as intangible assets have grown. An HML adjusted for intangibles works better after 1993.

The third view needs one piece of accounting. Spending on research, software and brands is mostly recorded as an expense, not as an asset. A company that builds its value that way reports a small book value, so its B/M looks low, and the sort files it as expensive even when it is not. That is why the Lev-Srivastava framework matters for modern value: the ranking itself may be drifting.

It is probably all three. The source is mixed: mostly behavioral and partly risk-based.

::: practice
Three kinds of fund implement value:

- **RAFI and Schwab fundamental funds** (FNDX, FNDA) weight companies by fundamentals: cash flow, sales, book value and dividends.
- **IVE**, the iShares S&P 500 Value ETF, is traditional value.
- **AVUV**, the Avantis US Small-Cap Value ETF, captures value among small companies filtered for quality.

The haircut is steep: from an academic HML Sharpe ratio of 0.4, to 0.15 to 0.30 in live funds, to 0.10 to 0.30 in a user account. Much of the last step is behavior: investors leave during long drawdowns.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Value fails in multi-year drawdowns. The years 2010 to 2020 were the worst in modern history, with AQR funds losing about 40% over 5 years and breaking many investors' commitment. Had you panic-exited in 2020, you would have missed the rebound of 43.5% in 2022.
:::

::: rule
Take a small tilt, 3% to 8% of the equity sleeve, only if you are committed to multi-decade patience. **If you would panic-exit during a 5-year drawdown, do not allocate to value.** The episode of 2010 to 2020 showed how punishing the path can be.
:::

::: familiar
A regimen's benefit among patients who completed every cycle overstates what it delivers to everyone who starts it, because some stop early. A factor's published return is the completers' result: the investor who held through every drawdown. If a five-year drawdown would make you stop, your result is that of the patients who discontinued: the toxicity without the benefit. Decide before you start whether you can complete the protocol.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "value has come back, so this is a new value-friendly regime"
Value has rebounded from a deep drawdown, but it is still cyclical. The outperformance of 2022 to 2024 might be a regime shift. It might instead be a bear-market mean reversion that reverses in the next bull market. Do not extrapolate, and allocate to value only with multi-decade tolerance.
:::

## Low volatility and betting against beta

::: plain
Low-volatility stocks have outperformed high-volatility stocks on a risk-adjusted basis. This contradicts the textbook model of risk and return, which says that more exposure to the market should earn more. It is one of the most counterintuitive results in the factor literature. The proposed mechanism is that investors who cannot borrow bid up high-beta stocks to get more equity exposure, which depresses their later returns.
:::

The textbook model is the **[[capital asset pricing model]]** (CAPM). It says that a stock's expected excess return is its beta times the market's:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i] - r = \beta_i \left(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - r\right) \]
{#eq:capm}

Here \(R_i\) is the stock's return, \(R_m\) the market's, \(r\) the risk-free rate and \(\beta_i\) the stock's beta. With \(r = 0\) and a market expected to return 10%, a stock with a beta of 0.6 should return 6% and one with a beta of 1.5 should return 15%.

::: worked Two stocks against the model
**Step 1: what the CAPM predicts.** Stock A has a beta of 0.6 and an expected return of 6%. Stock B has a beta of 1.5 and an expected return of 15%, as computed above.

**Step 2: what happens.** Empirically, a stock like A returns more like 8%, and a stock like B more like 11%. Both diverge from the CAPM in the same direction: low beta earns more than predicted and high beta less.

**Step 3: the bet.** The **[[betting against beta]]** (BAB) strategy goes long Stock A levered up and short Stock B levered down, so that each side has a beta of 1.
:::

The factor scales each side to a beta of 1 before taking the difference:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{BAB}_t = {}&\tfrac{1}{\beta_L}\left(R_{L,t} - r\right) \\
&- \tfrac{1}{\beta_H}\left(R_{H,t} - r\right)
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:bab}

Here \(L\) marks the low-beta portfolio and \(H\) the high-beta one. The portfolio's beta is \(\beta_L/\beta_L - \beta_H/\beta_H = 0\), so it is market-neutral. With the numbers of the example and \(r = 0\), the long side earns \(8\%/0.6 = 13.3\%\) and the short side costs \(11\%/1.5 = 7.3\%\). BAB earns 6.0% a year with no market exposure, but only after borrowing 67 cents for every dollar in the low-beta side, since \(1/0.6 = 1.67\).

### The evidence

- **Frazzini and Pedersen (2014, JFE)**, "Betting Against Beta", report a US BAB Sharpe ratio of 0.78 over 1926 to 2012.
- **A critical caveat, from Novy-Marx and Velikov (2022, JFE)**, "Betting Against Betting Against Beta": the Frazzini-Pedersen Sharpe ratio is largely an artifact of two choices. The first is a non-standard rank weighting that overweights micro- and nano-caps, the smallest companies. The second is a specific way of shrinking the estimated betas. In a value-weighted version, the BAB Sharpe ratio falls to about 0.49: still positive, with a t-statistic of 3.5 and 76 bps a month, but materially smaller.
- **Bali and colleagues (2017)** find that lottery demand for high-beta, lottery-like stocks creates the same effect.

Both choices can be seen in miniature. A **value-weighted** portfolio weights each stock by its [[market capitalization]], so a giant company counts for more than a small one. A rank weighting gives each stock a weight set by its rank on beta, whatever its size, so tiny companies carry as much weight as giants. Beta shrinkage pulls each noisy estimate toward 1: with a hypothetical weight of 0.6 on the estimate, a measured beta of 2.0 becomes \(0.6 \times 2.0 + 0.4 \times 1 = 1.6\). Choices like these are ordinary, but the result should not depend on them.

### Why it exists

Low volatility is mainly an institutional friction, Source 3 of Chapter 1G:

- **Leverage constraints:** mutual funds and pensions cannot borrow. To get more equity exposure they buy high-beta stocks instead, and high beta becomes overpriced.
- **A behavioral overlay:** lottery demand for high-beta names.

::: practice
- **USMV**, the iShares MSCI USA Min Vol ETF, with a 0.15% expense ratio, is long-only low volatility.
- **SPLV**, the Invesco S&P 500 Low Volatility ETF, has a 0.25% expense ratio.

A long-only fund captures roughly half the long-short Sharpe ratio but is fully scalable. Do not try to replicate the leveraged long-short version: the leverage is what causes BAB to break in stress.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Liquidity stress has historically punished BAB. Chapter 10H measures it with the TED spread, a gauge of banks' funding costs, above 200 bps, and the MOVE index of Treasury volatility above 150.

Long-only low volatility still has equity beta, and it loses in crashes: about 0.6 of SPX's losses. In a 30% fall, that is a loss of about 18%. The fund is a smaller equity position, not a hedge.
:::

::: rule
Give low volatility 3% to 8% of the equity sleeve, through USMV. Keep it small, because the academic Sharpe ratio is overstated and the live haircut is large. In a liquidity-stress regime (Chapter 10H), cut the allocation by 25%.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "BAB has a Sharpe ratio of 0.78, so it is a great factor"
Novy-Marx and Velikov (2022) show that the 0.78 figure is specific to one methodology. The value-weighted version is 0.49: still positive, but materially smaller. Do not over-allocate on the Frazzini-Pedersen number.
:::

## Size, once the junk is removed

::: plain
Banz (1981) reported a size premium of about 5% a year: small companies outperformed large ones. On its own, the effect has been roughly flat since 1981. Among small companies of high quality, with the junky ones excluded, the size premium re-emerges substantially.
:::

::: worked One index, two groups inside it
**Step 1: the aggregate.** Compare the Russell 2000 index of small companies with the S&P 500 since 1980: their long-run returns are roughly equal.

**Step 2: split the small companies.** Divide the Russell 2000 into high-quality companies, profitable and with low leverage, and junk, unprofitable and highly levered. The high-quality small caps outperform significantly, and the junk small caps underperform significantly.

**Step 3: the blend.** The aggregate index is dragged down by its junk component. As hypothetical arithmetic: if 60% of the index earns 12% and 40% earns 3%, the index earns \(0.6 \times 12\% + 0.4 \times 3\% = 8.4\%\). Measured against large caps that also earn 8.4%, the whole group shows no premium at all.
:::

The factor usually measures size as SMB, for small minus big. Conditioned on quality, it becomes

\[ \text{SMB}^{\text{adj}}_t = R^{\text{small, quality}}_t - R^{\text{large}}_t \]
{#eq:smb}

where the first return is that of small, high-quality companies and the second that of large companies.

::: familiar
A trial's overall effect is a weighted average of the effects in its subgroups. A null overall result can hide one group that benefits and another that is harmed, if the two are mixed in the right proportions. Standalone size is the overall result; size among quality companies is the subgroup that benefits, and junk is the one that drags the average down.
:::

### The evidence

- **Banz (1981)** reported the original size premium of about 5% a year.
- **Asness, Frazzini, Israel, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2018, JFE)**, "Size Matters, If You Control Your Junk", find that the premium nearly doubles after controlling for quality.

### Why it exists

The source is mixed:

- A risk premium for the illiquidity and distress risk of small companies.
- Behavioral underpricing of obscure small companies.
- Conditioning on quality removes the "junk" drag.

::: practice
- **AVUV**, the Avantis US Small-Cap Value ETF, with a 0.25% expense ratio.
- **DFSV**, the Dimensional US Small Cap Value ETF, with a 0.30% expense ratio.
- **ZIG**, the Acquirers Fund, which buys deep value.

These funds filter for quality within small caps. A fund of all small caps, such as IWM, gives you standalone size, which is much weaker than size and quality combined.
:::

::: rule
Take a modest tilt, 3% to 7% of the equity sleeve, through AVUV or something similar. Standalone size is much weaker than the combination of size and quality.
:::

## Combining the factors

Within the equity allocation, the Edge Book suggests this set of tilts:

| Factor | Weight in the equity sleeve | Vehicle |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | 8% to 12% | QUAL or COWZ |
| Momentum | 5% to 10% | MTUM |
| Value | 3% to 8% | RAFI fund or AVUV |
| Low volatility | 3% to 8% | USMV |
| Size and quality | 3% to 7% | AVUV |
| Pure cap weight | The remainder (50% to 70%) | SPY or VTI |

The total of the factor tilts is 25% to 40% of the equity sleeve. Do not go higher: the [[tracking error]] against broad equity becomes large, and the diversification benefit between factors saturates.

Read the table as a range for each tilt, not as a sum. The rows' ranges add up to 22% to 45%, and holding the total inside 25% to 40% leaves 60% to 75% for the index, more than the remainder column says. The quality row's 8% to 12% also sits inside the 5% to 15% of the quality section. AVUV appears in two rows, so count it once. Chapter 10A gives its own range for the category as a whole.

### Why diversify across factors

Factors do not all underperform at the same time:

- **2020 to 2021:** momentum strong, value weak, quality held up.
- **2022:** value strong, momentum mixed, quality held up.
- **2008:** low volatility best, momentum and value badly hit.
- **The 1990s:** value strong, momentum strong, low volatility weaker.

Diversifying across 4 to 5 factors smooths the path materially. A single-factor concentration produces multi-year drawdowns that test patience. Chapter 1F showed why, and the same algebra shows where the benefit stops.

::: grind The Sharpe ratio of an equal mix of factors
**Step 1: the variance of an average.** Take \(N\) factors, each with volatility \(\sigma\), and with the same correlation \(\rho\) between every pair. An equal-weight mix has variance

\[ \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\big(1 + (N-1)\rho\big) \]

because it holds \(N\) variance terms of \(\sigma^2/N^2\) each and \(N(N-1)\) covariance terms of \(\rho\sigma^2/N^2\) each.

**Step 2: the Sharpe ratio.** If each factor has the same Sharpe ratio SR, the mix keeps the same excess return per unit held, while its volatility is the square root above. Dividing the one by the other gives the result below.
:::

\[ \text{SR}_N = \text{SR}\sqrt{\frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\rho}} \]
{#eq:mix}

With a hypothetical Sharpe ratio of 0.4 for each factor and a correlation of 0.2 between them, four factors give \(0.4 \times \sqrt{4/1.6} = 0.63\). As \(N\) grows, the fraction approaches \(1/\rho\), so the mix can never beat \(0.4/\sqrt{0.2} = 0.89\). That ceiling is what "the diversification benefit saturates" means: the fifth factor adds less than the second, and a twentieth adds almost nothing.

[FIGURE IDEA: Diversification with a ceiling. The reader sets the Sharpe ratio of each factor and the correlation between them, and the figure plots the Sharpe ratio of an equal mix against the number of factors from 1 to 20, with the ceiling SR divided by the square root of ρ drawn as a dashed line. ### What the whole set adds

- **An academic combined factor portfolio:** about 2% to 3% a year of excess return over cap weighting, with a Sharpe ratio of about 0.6.
- **A realistic retail implementation:** about 0.5% to 1.5% a year of excess return, with a Sharpe ratio of about 0.2 to 0.4 above cap weighting.

A Sharpe ratio measured on the excess over an index is the [[information ratio]] of Chapter 1D. The retail haircut is large for three reasons: ETF implementations are partial, tax friction matters, and behavioral exits during multi-year drawdowns are common.

The Edge Book also summarizes the set as adding 0.2 to 0.4 of Sharpe ratio to the equity sleeve. Read that as the information ratio of the tilts, not as a rise in the whole sleeve's ratio, as the arithmetic below shows.

::: worked What 1% a year does to a sleeve
**Step 1: the sleeve before.** Suppose, hypothetically, the equity sleeve earns 6% a year above cash with a volatility of 16%. Its Sharpe ratio is \(6/16 = 0.375\).

**Step 2: add the tilts.** The tilts add 1% a year of excess return and leave the volatility about where it was. The sleeve's Sharpe ratio becomes \(7/16 = 0.44\), a rise of about 0.06.

**Step 3: the tilts on their own.** If the tilts differ from the index with a tracking error of 3% a year, their information ratio is \(1/3 = 0.33\), inside the range of 0.2 to 0.4.

**Step 4: the patience it takes.** By [eq:tstat], an information ratio of 0.33 needs \((2/0.33)^2 \approx 37\) years to reach a t-statistic of 2. You cannot confirm from your own account, within an investing lifetime, that the tilts work.
:::

That last step is the reason the chapter keeps asking for multi-decade commitment. The tilts are worth doing, but you hold them because of the mechanism and the published evidence, not because your own returns prove them. Combining quality, momentum, value and low volatility is worth doing only with that commitment and an explicit understanding of the path.

## Why not time the factors

Factor performance is highly cyclical and difficult to predict. Most of the academic literature finds that **[[factor timing]]**, shifting weight toward whichever factor looks set to do well, adds no value relative to a static, diversified factor allocation. The exception is timing value by its valuation spread when the spread is extreme, and even that is contentious. Default to static, diversified tilts.

::: warn Common mistake: "I can time factor rotation by buying whichever factor is hot"
A factor that is hot has usually just had its valuation spread move in its favor, which by [eq:spread] is a return you have already missed. Rotation also adds turnover, and so taxes, to strategies whose edge is measured in fractions of a percent. Hold the tilts fixed and rebalance them to their weights.
:::

::: practice
- **Hold tilts, not concentrations.** Keep the cap-weighted index as the core of the equity sleeve, and the tilts to a total of 25% to 40% of it.
- **Place the funds by tax.** Momentum's turnover belongs in a tax-advantaged account. The low-turnover tilts, such as quality and low volatility, can sit in a taxable one.
- **Check the vehicle once a year.** Compare each fund's return with its academic factor. A tracking error above 4% a year is the Edge Book's signal to switch vehicle.
- **Expect years of underperformance.** Any single tilt can lag the index for a decade, and value lagged for most of one.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Factor tilts fail in three ways. They decay after publication, by about 47% on average across hundreds of factors. They crash, as momentum did in 2009 when it lost 73% in three months. And they drift through drawdowns so long that investors leave before the rebound, as value's did from 2010 to 2020. A tilt that is too large turns each of these into a portfolio problem.
:::

::: rule
- **Allocate 25% to 40% of the equity sleeve to tilts,** across four or five factors: quality first, then momentum, value, low volatility, and size among quality companies.
- **Size each tilt by its path, not its paper Sharpe ratio.** Value and momentum get less than quality because of their drawdowns, decay and taxes.
- **Skip a tilt you would abandon in a drawdown.** Value in particular is only for an investor who can hold through five bad years.
- **Do not rotate.** Keep the weights static and rebalance to them.
:::

The next chapter, Chapter 10G, turns from factors to calendar and flow effects: the FOMC cycle, end-of-quarter (EOQ) rebalancing, options expiration (OPEX) and the seasons. They are smaller than the factor tilts, and they modulate existing positions rather than holding a sleeve of their own.

::: recap
- A factor is a ranking of stocks that has predicted returns, measured as a long-short portfolio and held as a long-only tilt.
- Quality has held up best, because its premium is partly a risk premium. Momentum, value and low volatility are more behavioral or institutional, and have decayed or crashed.
- Paper Sharpe ratios of 0.4 to 0.78 shrink to 0.10 to 0.45 in a user account, and to about half for long-only low volatility.
- Diversifying across factors raises the Sharpe ratio toward a ceiling set by their correlation, and smooths the path.
- A realistic tilt adds 0.5% to 1.5% a year over the index, too little to confirm from your own record: hold it on the evidence, statically, for decades.
:::

Novy-Marx and Velikov (2022) showed that the Sharpe ratio of 0.78 depends on rank weighting that overweights the smallest companies and on a specific beta shrinkage; value-weighted, it is about 0.49.
