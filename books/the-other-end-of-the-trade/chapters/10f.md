A factor tilt modulates the equity [[sleeve]] to capture a documented, systematic excess return. The five factors here are quality, momentum, value, low volatility and size, each with its own mechanism, decay risk and drawdown profile. Most had their best decade in academic papers, while live retail experience is humbler. They are worth moderate allocations as tilts rather than concentrations, although harvesting them takes patience measured in decades.

::: plain
Sort every stock in the market by one trait, such as how profitable the company is or how much its price rose last year. If the stocks at one end of the sort have earned more than those at the other end over many decades, the trait is a factor. A tilt holds a little more of the favored end than the index does. It pays off, if at all, over decades rather than quarters.
:::

## What a factor is

An **[[equity factor]]** is a trait by which stocks can be ranked, where the ranking has predicted returns. Researchers measure it with a **[[long-short portfolio]]**, which buys the stocks at the favored end of the ranking and sells short an equal dollar amount of those at the other end. The difference in return between the two sides is the factor's return in month \(t\):

\[ F_t = R^{\text{top}}_t - R^{\text{bottom}}_t \]
{#eq:factor}

Here \(R^{\text{top}}_t\) and \(R^{\text{bottom}}_t\) are the average returns of the two groups. Suppose you hold $10,000 of the top group and short $10,000 of the bottom group, which return 1.4% and 0.2% in a month. You make $140 on one side and lose $20 on the other, so \(F_t = 1.2\%\). Because one side offsets the other, the factor's return usually depends little on whether the market rose or fell, a rule that momentum breaks below. Each factor in this chapter is a version of [eq:factor] with its own ranking.

[FIGURE IDEA: A factor sorting machine. Fifty simulated stocks carry a trait score and a next-month return. The reader sets the group sizes and how strongly the trait predicts returns, and the figure runs 240 months of the long-short portfolio beside the market. It shows a weak true link producing long losing stretches.]

A tilt is the version you can hold. Instead of a long-short portfolio, you own a fund that holds more of the favored stocks than their share of the index, and nothing short. Its return is roughly the market's plus a fraction of the factor's.

### How to read the evidence

Factor papers report monthly returns, [[Sharpe ratios|Sharpe ratio]] and the t-statistic you know from trials, an estimated effect divided by its standard error. For a factor, the t-statistic has a simple link to the Sharpe ratio.

::: grind From a Sharpe ratio to a t-statistic
**Step 1: the t-statistic of a mean.** With \(n\) monthly returns of mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s\), the standard error of the mean is \(s/\sqrt{n}\), so \(t = m\sqrt{n}/s\). A long-short factor is already an excess return, so \(m/s\) is its monthly Sharpe ratio and \(t = \text{SR}_{\text{month}}\sqrt{n}\).

**Step 2: annualize.** The annual Sharpe ratio is \(\text{SR}_{\text{month}}\sqrt{12}\) (Chapter 1D), and \(Y\) years hold \(n = 12Y\) months. Substituting both, the factors of \(\sqrt{12}\) cancel and leave the result below.
:::

\[ t = \text{SR} \times \sqrt{Y} \]
{#eq:tstat}

Here SR is the annual Sharpe ratio and \(Y\) is the number of years of data. A factor with a Sharpe ratio of 0.4 reaches \(t = 2\) after 25 years, since \(0.4 \times \sqrt{25} = 2\), but shows only \(0.4 \times 3 = 1.2\) after 9 years. Published t-statistics come from particular samples and constructions, so use [eq:tstat] for orders of magnitude rather than to recompute them.

[FIGURE IDEA: Years to evidence. The reader sets an annual Sharpe ratio from 0.1 to 1.0, and the figure plots the t-statistic against years of data, with lines at 2 and 3. It shows a Sharpe ratio of 0.3 needing about 44 years to reach a t of 2.]

## Quality

::: plain
Profitable, stable, growing companies with conservative investment have outperformed unprofitable, volatile, declining ones. Part of the reason is behavioral, because investors over-extrapolate growth in junky names. Part of it is a risk premium, because quality companies carry less risk of financial distress.
:::

::: worked Two companies
**Step 1: the two stocks.** Stock A has a 25% [[return on equity]], stable margins and a conservative balance sheet. Stock B has volatile earnings, a growth-story narrative and a leveraged balance sheet. B trades at 10 times its earnings, a [[price-to-earnings ratio]] (P/E) of 10, while A trades at 18 times, so B looks the cheaper of the two.

**Step 2: what follows.** Empirically, a stock like A outperforms over periods of 5 years or more, because B's "growth story" tends to disappoint while A keeps compounding.
:::

The [[quality factor]] is multi-dimensional. In a 2019 paper in the *Review of Accounting Studies* (RAS), Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen build their **quality minus junk** (QMJ) factor on four pillars:

- **Profitability:** gross profits divided by assets, return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE).
- **Growth:** the stability and the rate of growth of profits.
- **Safety:** low beta, low leverage and low volatility of earnings.
- **Payout:** stable dividends and share buybacks.

Holding high-quality stocks long and junky ones short gives the QMJ factor.

[FIGURE IDEA: A quality score builder. The reader drags the four pillar readings for a few hypothetical companies, and the figure turns each into a z-score, averages them and ranks the companies. Dropping the safety pillar shows a profitable but heavily indebted company climbing the ranking it does not deserve.]

### The evidence

- **Novy-Marx (2013)**, "The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium", in the *Journal of Financial Economics* (JFE), won the 2013 Fama-DFA Prize.
- **Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019)** report a QMJ [[alpha]], the return beyond what standard factor models explain (Chapter 1D), of 0.66% a month in the US and 0.45% globally, across about 24 countries. The US figure is roughly 8% a year.
- **Quality was the single factor that did not crash through the value drawdown of 2017 to 2022**, a key test in real markets rather than in a backtest.

### Why it exists

The mechanism is mixed. As a risk premium, quality firms carry lower distress risk, for which investors might rationally pay. As a behavioral effect, investors overpay for a narrative of growth and underpay for boring profitability. The mixed source explains why quality has held up better than the purely behavioral factors: the risk-based part of the premium does not decay, while the behavioral part might (Chapter 1G).

::: practice
Three funds bring quality into a retail account:

- **QUAL**, the iShares MSCI USA Quality ETF, has a 0.15% expense ratio and tracks the MSCI Quality Index.
- **COWZ**, the Pacer US Cash Cows 100 ETF, has a 0.49% expense ratio and holds a more concentrated list chosen for a high [[free cash flow]] yield.
- **DGRO**, the iShares Core Dividend Growth ETF, is a proxy through the stability of dividends.

An **[[expense ratio]]** is a fund's yearly charge as a share of your holding, so 0.15% costs $15 a year on $10,000. QUAL is a *partial* implementation of academic quality: MSCI's index methodology captures perhaps 60% to 70% of the academic factor premium. That is still meaningful: close to the live factor portfolio after fees and execution. QUAL and COWZ differ in breadth, a broad index against a concentrated list built on one pillar, cash generation.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Through the value drawdown of 2017 to 2022, quality's drawdowns were much smaller than those of the other factors, but no factor is permanently safe. A regime that rewards risky, junky names, such as the meme-stock rally of 2020 and 2021, can produce one to two years in which quality underperforms.
:::

::: rule
Give quality 5% to 15% of the equity sleeve, through QUAL or COWZ. Do not over-concentrate: tilt toward quality rather than replacing SPX with it.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "quality is just a fancy way to buy expensive stocks"
Quality and value are *distinct* factors with different mechanisms. The QMJ construction controls for valuation, so the quality premium exists *after* you control for value. Quality is not growth dressed up, because it measures fundamentals such as profitability and stability, where value measures price through ratios such as P/E and price to book value.
:::

## Momentum

::: plain
Stocks that have outperformed over the past 6 to 12 months tend to keep outperforming over the next 1 to 3 months. The mechanism is behavioral: investors under-react to news, then over-react at the extremes of a trend. That over-reaction is what creates the risk of a crash when a trend reverses.
:::

This is **cross-sectional** [[momentum]], which compares stocks with one another. Its cousin, the time-series momentum of Chapter 10D, trades each asset on its own past.

::: worked Winners minus losers
**Step 1: the sort.** Rank all S&P 500 stocks by total return over the last 12 months, as of last month, skipping the most recent month because it has reversal effects of its own. Buy the top decile of 50 winners and sell short the bottom decile of 50 losers, the canonical "winners minus losers" factor.

**Step 2: a month of it.** If the winners return 2.0% the next month and the losers 0.8%, the factor earns \(2.0\% - 0.8\% = 1.2\%\).
:::

The factor is called UMD, for up minus down. It is [eq:factor] with past returns as the ranking:

\[ \text{UMD}_t = R^{\text{Winners}}_t - R^{\text{Losers}}_t \]
{#eq:umd}

### The evidence

- **Jegadeesh and Titman (1993, *Journal of Finance*, JF)** is the canonical study. UMD earned about 1% a month, with a t-statistic of about 3 and a Sharpe ratio of about 0.5 unlevered, that is, without borrowing to scale the portfolio up.
- **Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2013, JF)**, "Value and Momentum Everywhere", found momentum pervasive across 8 asset classes globally.
- **Daniel and Moskowitz (2016, JFE)** add the *critical caveat* that momentum lost 73% from March to May 2009, in the V-bottom that ended a bear market.
- **Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023, JF)** document a decline of about 47% after publication across hundreds of factors.

### Why it exists

Momentum is mostly behavioral, Source 2 of Chapter 1G. Investors under-react to news because they anchor on old information, then over-react at trend extremes by chasing what has already risen. Momentum is possibly also compensation for crash risk in V-shaped reversals, whose mechanics a toy example shows.

::: worked Why momentum crashes at a market bottom
**Step 1: the portfolio after a bear market.** Suppose the market has fallen for a year. The losers are the stocks that fell hardest, which tend to be the most sensitive to the market, with a [[beta]] of, say, 1.8. The winners held up, with a beta of 0.6, so the long-short portfolio's beta is \(0.6 - 1.8 = -1.2\), a large bet that the market keeps falling.

**Step 2: the rebound.** The market then rallies 30% in three months. From beta alone the winners rise \(0.6 \times 30\% = 18\%\) and the losers \(1.8 \times 30\% = 54\%\), so the portfolio loses 36% before any return of its own.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: Momentum's hidden beta. The reader drags the market through a year-long fall and a rebound of chosen size. The figure tracks the winners' and losers' betas as the sort is refreshed monthly, with the portfolio's net beta and cumulative return. It shows the net beta turning strongly negative in the fall and the factor losing heavily early in the rebound.]

::: practice
Two funds implement momentum: **MTUM**, the iShares MSCI USA Momentum ETF, with a 0.15% expense ratio, and **PDP**, the Invesco DWA Momentum ETF.

Momentum's high **[[turnover]]**, about 80% of the portfolio a year, makes it tax-inefficient. In a taxable account the trading generates [[short-term capital gains|short-term capital gain]], taxed as ordinary income, which drag significantly on after-tax returns. Suppose, hypothetically, that trading realizes $8,000 of short-term gains in a year on a $100,000 sleeve. At a 35% rate the tax is $2,800, or 2.8% of the sleeve, where an index fund held for years defers almost all of its tax. Momentum is best held in a tax-advantaged account such as a retirement account (Chapter 2E).
:::

::: warn When it fails
**Momentum crashes.** When the market reverses sharply, as when a bear market bottoms, the losers you were short rally violently and the winners you were long collapse. Daniel and Moskowitz documented a loss of 73% in 3 months in 2009. Their vol-scaled, or dynamic, construction cuts exposure when crash conditions are flagged. This roughly doubles the unconditional Sharpe ratio, measured over all periods together, though it is harder to implement in a retail account.
:::

::: rule
Give momentum 5% to 10% of the equity sleeve, through MTUM. That is less than quality because momentum carries a higher risk of decay and worse tax behavior. Two kill criteria apply:

- **After a sustained equity drawdown of 6 months or more**, momentum's crash risk is elevated. Cut the exposure by 50% until the equity market's 3-month return turns positive.
- **If MTUM's tracking error to the academic UMD factor exceeds 4% a year**, switch to another vehicle.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "momentum has worked for 30 years, so it must keep working"
Momentum has decayed materially since publication, by about 47% in the study by Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023). It can also produce drawdowns of 70% in 3 months. The strategy still has a positive expected return, but a smaller one with worse path properties than data from the 1990s suggests.
:::

## Value

::: plain
Cheap stocks, whose price is low relative to fundamentals such as book value, earnings or cash flow, outperform expensive stocks over long horizons. Why they do is debated. The candidates are a risk premium for distress, a behavioral over-extrapolation of growth and a problem measuring intangible assets under modern accounting.
:::

::: worked Cheap against expensive
**Step 1: the ratio.** For each stock, divide the book value of its equity by its market value. A high **[[book-to-market ratio]]** (B/M) means the market prices the company cheaply relative to its accounts.

**Step 2: the portfolio and its history.** Sort the S&P 500 by B/M, then buy the cheap top decile and sell short the expensive bottom decile. This is the canonical Fama-French HML factor, for high minus low. It produced reliable excess returns from 1927 to 2010, then went into the worst drawdown in modern history.
:::

\[ \text{HML}_t = R^{\text{High B/M}}_t - R^{\text{Low B/M}}_t \]
{#eq:hml}

Modern variations may control for sector exposure and rank on a composite: E/P (earnings to price), B/M, FCF/P (free cash flow, FCF, to price) and EV/EBITDA. The last divides enterprise value (EV), the shares plus debt less cash, by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA).

### The evidence

- **Fama and French (1993, JFE)** is the canonical study. HML earned 0.40% to 0.50% a month, with a t-statistic of about 3 and a long-run Sharpe ratio of about 0.4.
- **The years 2010 to 2020 brought the worst documented value drawdown in modern history.** AQR Equity Market Neutral lost about 38% from peak to trough by December 2020.
- **Arnott, Harvey, Kalesnik and Linnainmaa (2021)** wrote "Reports of Value's Death May Be Greatly Exaggerated" in the *Journal of Portfolio Management* (JPM). They attribute the drawdown to a widening of the valuation spread, which is cyclical, not to fundamentals.
- **The rebound followed.** AQR Absolute Return gained 43.5% in 2022 and 18.5% in 2023, while the Russell 1000 Value index gained 24.9% in 2024.

The **[[valuation spread]]** is how many times more expensive the expensive stocks are than the cheap ones. Write each group's price as a multiple \(m\) of its book value. The spread is then \(V = m_e/m_c\), where \(e\) marks the expensive group and \(c\) the cheap one. From the start of a period, 0, to its end, 1, regrouping the multiples and the book values gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1 + R_c}{1 + R_e}
&= \frac{V_0}{V_1} \times \frac{1 + g_c}{1 + g_e}
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:spread}

where \(R\) is each group's return and \(g\) the growth of its book value. Value's return against expensive stocks is the change in the spread times the difference in fundamental growth.

::: worked Losing without anything going wrong
**Step 1: the spread widens.** Suppose the cheap stocks trade at 1 times book and the expensive ones at 4 times, a spread of 4. Both groups grow their book value at the same rate while investors bid the expensive stocks up to 6 times book. By [eq:spread] the ratio is \(4/6 = 0.667\), so the cheap side has lost 33% relative to the expensive side with nothing wrong in its businesses.

**Step 2: the spread narrows.** If the spread later returns to 4, the ratio is \(6/4 = 1.5\), a relative gain of 50%. That is the cyclical reading of Arnott and colleagues: prices alone can swamp years of fundamental advantage, then repay it.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: Value's return split in two. The reader sets the valuation spread at the start and end of a period and each group's book growth. Two bars show the part of value's relative return from the change in spread and the part from fundamentals. A preset widens the spread from 4 to 6 with equal growth and shows a loss of a third from prices alone.]

### Why it exists

The source of the value premium is disputed among three views:

- **The risk-premium view of Fama and French:** value stocks bear the risk of financial distress.
- **The behavioral view of Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny:** investors over-extrapolate growth and underpay for boring value.
- **The intangibles view of Lev and Srivastava:** book-to-market is mismeasured as intangible assets have grown. An HML adjusted for intangibles works better after 1993.

Accounting records most spending on research, software and brands as an expense rather than an asset. A company built that way reports a small book value, so the sort files it as expensive even when it is not. Probably all three views hold: the source is mixed, mostly behavioral and partly risk-based.

::: practice
Three kinds of fund implement value:

- **RAFI and Schwab fundamental funds** (FNDX, FNDA) weight companies by fundamentals: cash flow, sales, book value and dividends.
- **IVE**, the iShares S&P 500 Value ETF, is traditional value.
- **AVUV**, the Avantis US Small-Cap Value ETF, captures value among small companies filtered for quality.

The haircut runs from an academic HML Sharpe ratio of 0.4, to 0.15 to 0.30 in live funds, to 0.10 to 0.30 in a user account. Much of the last step is behavior, as investors leave during long drawdowns.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Value fails through multi-year drawdowns. The years 2010 to 2020 were the worst in modern history, with AQR funds losing about 40% over 5 years and breaking many investors' commitment. Had you panic-exited in 2020, you would have missed the rebound of 43.5% in 2022.
:::

::: rule
Take a small tilt of 3% to 8% of the equity sleeve, and only with a commitment to multi-decade patience. **If you would panic-exit during a 5-year drawdown, do not allocate to value.** The episode of 2010 to 2020 showed how punishing the path can be.
:::

::: familiar
A regimen's benefit among the patients who completed every cycle overstates what it delivers to everyone who starts it, because some stop early. A factor's published return is the completers' result, earned by an investor who held through every drawdown. If a five-year drawdown would make you stop, expect the result of the patients who discontinued: the toxicity without the benefit.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "value has come back, so this is a new value-friendly regime"
Value has rebounded from a deep drawdown, but it is still cyclical. The outperformance of 2022 to 2024 might be a regime shift, or it might be a bear-market mean reversion that reverses in the next bull market. Do not extrapolate it: allocate to value only with multi-decade tolerance.
:::

## Low volatility and betting against beta

::: plain
Low-volatility stocks have outperformed high-volatility stocks on a risk-adjusted basis. This [[low-volatility anomaly]] contradicts the textbook model, in which more exposure to the market earns more, and it is one of the most counterintuitive results in the factor literature. The proposed mechanism is that investors who cannot borrow bid up high-beta stocks to get equity exposure, depressing their later returns.
:::

The textbook model is the **[[capital asset pricing model]]** (CAPM). It says a stock's expected excess return is its beta times the market's:

\[ \mathbb{E}[R_i] - r = \beta_i \left(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - r\right) \]
{#eq:capm}

Here \(R_i\) is the stock's return, \(R_m\) the market's, \(r\) the risk-free rate and \(\beta_i\) the stock's beta.

::: worked Two stocks against the model
**Step 1: prediction and outcome.** With \(r = 0\) and a market expected to return 10%, [eq:capm] gives Stock A, with a beta of 0.6, an expected return of 6%, and Stock B, with a beta of 1.5, one of 15%. Empirically, a stock like A returns more like 8% and a stock like B more like 11%. Both diverge from the model in the same direction, with low beta earning more than predicted and high beta less.

**Step 2: the bet.** The **[[betting against beta]]** (BAB) strategy goes long Stock A levered up and short Stock B levered down, so that each side has a beta of 1.
:::

The factor scales each side to a beta of 1 before taking the difference:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{BAB}_t = {}&\tfrac{1}{\beta_L}\left(R_{L,t} - r\right) \\
&- \tfrac{1}{\beta_H}\left(R_{H,t} - r\right)
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:bab}

Here \(L\) marks the low-beta portfolio and \(H\) the high-beta one, and the portfolio's beta is \(\beta_L/\beta_L - \beta_H/\beta_H = 0\). With the example's numbers and \(r = 0\), the long side earns \(8\%/0.6 = 13.3\%\) and the short side costs \(11\%/1.5 = 7.3\%\). BAB earns 6.0% a year with no market exposure, though only after borrowing 67 cents for every dollar in the low-beta side, since \(1/0.6 = 1.67\).

[FIGURE IDEA: The security market line and the empirical line. The figure plots expected return against beta: the CAPM's straight line and a flatter line through hypothetical stocks the reader can drag. It builds BAB from the two and shows its return as the gap between the lines. A slider sets the cost of borrowing, and the return shrinks as leverage gets dearer.]

### The evidence

- **Frazzini and Pedersen (2014, JFE)**, "Betting Against Beta", report a US BAB Sharpe ratio of 0.78 over 1926 to 2012.
- **Novy-Marx and Velikov (2022, JFE)**, "Betting Against Betting Against Beta", add a *critical caveat*. The Frazzini-Pedersen Sharpe ratio is largely an artifact of two choices: a non-standard rank weighting that overweights micro- and nano-caps, the smallest companies, and a specific beta shrinkage. In a value-weighted version the BAB Sharpe ratio falls to about 0.49, still positive at a t-statistic of 3.5 and 76 bps a month, but materially smaller.
- **Bali and colleagues (2017)** find that lottery demand for high-beta, lottery-like stocks creates the same effect.

A **value-weighted** portfolio weights each stock by its [[market capitalization]], while a rank weighting gives tiny companies as much weight as giants. Shrinkage pulls each noisy beta estimate part of the way toward 1. Such choices are ordinary, which makes a result that depends on them fragile.

### Why it exists

Low volatility is mainly an institutional friction, Source 3 of Chapter 1G. Leverage-constrained investors such as mutual funds and pensions cannot borrow, so they buy high-beta stocks to get more equity exposure, and high beta becomes overpriced. Lottery demand for high-beta names adds a behavioral overlay.

::: practice
Two funds implement low volatility: **USMV**, the iShares MSCI USA Min Vol ETF, with a 0.15% expense ratio, and **SPLV**, the Invesco S&P 500 Low Volatility ETF, with 0.25%. A long-only fund captures roughly half the long-short Sharpe ratio but is fully scalable. Do not try to replicate the leveraged long-short version, because the leverage is what makes BAB break in stress.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Liquidity stress has historically punished BAB. Chapter 10H marks it by a TED spread, a gauge of banks' borrowing costs, above 200 bps, or a MOVE index of Treasury volatility above 150. Long-only low volatility still carries equity beta and loses in crashes, about 0.6 of SPX's losses. In a 30% fall that is a loss of about 18%, which makes the fund a smaller equity position rather than a hedge.
:::

::: rule
Give low volatility 3% to 8% of the equity sleeve, through USMV. Keep it small, because the academic Sharpe ratio is overstated and the live haircut is large. In a liquidity-stress regime (Chapter 10H), cut the allocation by 25%.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "BAB has a Sharpe ratio of 0.78, so it is a great factor"
Novy-Marx and Velikov (2022) show that the 0.78 figure is specific to one methodology. The value-weighted version is 0.49, still positive but materially smaller, so do not over-allocate on the Frazzini-Pedersen number.
:::

## Size, once the junk is removed

::: plain
Banz (1981) reported a size premium of about 5% a year: small companies outperformed large ones. On its own, the effect has been roughly flat since 1981. Among small companies of high quality, with the junky ones excluded, the size premium re-emerges substantially.
:::

::: worked One index, two groups inside it
**Step 1: the aggregate.** Since 1980, the Russell 2000 index of small companies and the S&P 500 have had roughly equal long-run returns. Split the Russell 2000 into high-quality companies, profitable and with low leverage, and junk, unprofitable and highly levered. The high-quality small caps outperform significantly, while the junk underperforms significantly.

**Step 2: the blend.** As hypothetical arithmetic, if 60% of the index earns 12% and 40% earns 3%, the index earns \(0.6 \times 12\% + 0.4 \times 3\% = 8.4\%\). Measured against large companies that also earn 8.4%, small companies as a whole show no premium, because the junk drags the aggregate down.
:::

Size is usually measured as SMB, for small minus big. Conditioned on quality, it compares small, high-quality companies with large ones:

\[ \text{SMB}^{\text{adj}}_t = R^{\text{small, quality}}_t - R^{\text{large}}_t \]
{#eq:smb}

Asness, Frazzini, Israel, Moskowitz and Pedersen (2018, JFE), "Size Matters, If You Control Your Junk", find that the premium nearly doubles after controlling for quality. The source is mixed: a risk premium for the illiquidity and distress risk of small companies, plus behavioral underpricing of obscure ones. Conditioning on quality removes the "junk" drag.

::: rule
Take a modest tilt, 3% to 7% of the equity sleeve, through a fund that filters for quality within small companies. **AVUV** is the Avantis US Small-Cap Value ETF, with a 0.25% expense ratio, and **DFSV** the Dimensional US Small Cap Value ETF, with 0.30%. **ZIG**, the Acquirers Fund, buys deep value. Standalone size, such as a fund of all small companies like IWM, is much weaker than size and quality combined.
:::

## Combining the factors

Within the equity allocation, the Edge Book suggests this set of tilts:

| Factor | Weight in the equity sleeve | Vehicle |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | 8% to 12% | QUAL or COWZ |
| Momentum | 5% to 10% | MTUM |
| Value | 3% to 8% | RAFI fund or AVUV |
| Low volatility | 3% to 8% | USMV |
| Size and quality | 3% to 7% | AVUV |
| Pure cap weight | The remainder (50% to 70%) | SPY or VTI |

The factor tilts together take 25% to 40% of the equity sleeve. Do not go higher, because the [[tracking error]] against broad equity becomes large while the diversification benefit between factors saturates. Read the table as a range for each tilt rather than as a sum. A total held to 25% to 40% leaves 60% to 75% for the index, more than the remainder column says, and AVUV appears in two rows, so count it only once. The Edge Book's own summary of the category, in Chapter 10A, puts the tilts lower, at 15% to 30% of the sleeve.

Factors do not all underperform at the same time:

- **2020 to 2021:** momentum strong, value weak, quality held up.
- **2022:** value strong, momentum mixed, quality held up.
- **2008:** low volatility best, momentum and value badly hit.
- **The 1990s:** value and momentum strong, low volatility weaker.

Diversifying across 4 to 5 factors smooths the path materially, while a single-factor concentration produces multi-year drawdowns that test patience. The algebra of Chapter 1F shows how much combining helps, and where the help stops.

::: grind The Sharpe ratio of an equal mix of factors
Take \(N\) factors, each with volatility \(\sigma\) and Sharpe ratio SR, with the same correlation \(\rho\) between every pair. An equal-weight mix holds \(N\) variance terms of \(\sigma^2/N^2\) and \(N(N-1)\) covariance terms of \(\rho\sigma^2/N^2\), so its variance is

\[ \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\big(1 + (N-1)\rho\big) \]

The mix earns the same excess return per dollar as each factor. Dividing that return by the square root of the variance gives the result below.
:::

\[ \text{SR}_N = \text{SR}\sqrt{\frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\rho}} \]
{#eq:mix}

With a hypothetical Sharpe ratio of 0.4 for each factor and a correlation of 0.2 between them, four factors give \(0.4 \times \sqrt{4/1.6} = 0.63\). As \(N\) grows, the fraction under the root approaches \(1/\rho\), so no number of such factors beats \(0.4/\sqrt{0.2} = 0.89\). That ceiling is what "the diversification benefit saturates" means: the fifth factor adds less than the second, and a twentieth adds almost nothing.

### What the whole set adds

An academic combined factor portfolio earns about 2% to 3% a year of excess return over cap weighting, with a Sharpe ratio of about 0.6. A realistic retail implementation earns about 0.5% to 1.5% a year, with a Sharpe ratio of about 0.2 to 0.4 above cap weighting, which is the [[information ratio]] of Chapter 1D. The retail haircut is large for three reasons: the ETFs implement the factors only partially, tax friction matters and behavioral exits during multi-year drawdowns are common.

The Edge Book also says that combining quality, momentum, value and low volatility can add 0.2 to 0.4 of Sharpe ratio to the equity sleeve. Read that as the tilts' information ratio, not as a rise in the whole sleeve's ratio.

::: worked What 1% a year does to a sleeve
**Step 1: the sleeve.** Suppose, hypothetically, the equity sleeve earns 6% a year above cash with a volatility of 16%, a Sharpe ratio of \(6/16 = 0.375\). Tilts that add 1% a year at about the same volatility lift it to \(7/16 = 0.44\), a rise of only about 0.06.

**Step 2: the tilts on their own.** If the tilts differ from the index with a tracking error of 3% a year, their information ratio is \(1/3 = 0.33\), inside the range of 0.2 to 0.4. By [eq:tstat], that needs \((2/0.33)^2 \approx 37\) years to reach a t-statistic of 2, so your own account cannot confirm within an investing lifetime that the tilts work.
:::

That is why the tilts are worth doing only with a multi-decade commitment and an explicit understanding of the path.

## Why not time the factors

Factor performance is highly cyclical and difficult to predict. Most of the academic literature finds that **[[factor timing]]**, shifting weight toward whichever factor looks set to do well, adds no value relative to a static, diversified factor allocation. The exception is timing value by its valuation spread when the spread is extreme, and even that is contentious. Default to static, diversified tilts.

::: warn Common mistake: "I can time factor rotation by buying whichever factor is hot"
A factor that is hot may simply have had its valuation spread move in its favor, which by [eq:spread] is a return already earned, and one that reverses if the spread narrows. Rotation also adds turnover, and so taxes, to strategies whose edge is measured in fractions of a percent.
:::

::: practice
Keep the cap-weighted index as the core of the equity sleeve. Put momentum, with its turnover, in a tax-advantaged account, and low-turnover tilts such as quality and low volatility in a taxable one. Once a year, compare each fund with its academic factor, and switch vehicle when the tracking error exceeds 4% a year.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Factor tilts decay after publication, and they crash, as momentum did in 2009. They also drift through drawdowns so long that investors leave before the rebound, as value investors did from 2010 to 2020.
:::

::: rule
- **Allocate 25% to 40% of the equity sleeve to tilts,** spread across quality, momentum, value, low volatility and size among quality companies.
- **Size each tilt by its path rather than its paper Sharpe ratio.** Value and momentum get less than quality because of their drawdowns, decay and taxes.
- **Skip any tilt you would abandon in a drawdown.** Value in particular is only for an investor who can hold through five bad years.
- **Do not rotate.** Keep the weights static and rebalance back to them.
:::

Chapter 10G turns to calendar and flow effects: the FOMC cycle, end-of-quarter (EOQ) rebalancing, options expiration (OPEX) and the seasons. They are smaller than the factor tilts and modulate existing positions rather than holding a sleeve of their own.

::: recap
- A factor is a ranking of stocks that has predicted returns, measured as a long-short portfolio and held as a long-only tilt.
- Quality has held up best because part of its premium is a risk premium. The more behavioral or institutional factors have decayed or crashed.
- Paper Sharpe ratios of 0.4 to 0.6 shrink to 0.10 to 0.45 in a user account, and BAB's 0.78 is 0.49 when value-weighted.
- Diversifying across factors raises the Sharpe ratio toward a ceiling set by their correlation.
- A realistic set of tilts adds 0.5% to 1.5% a year, too little to confirm from your own record: hold it statically, on the evidence, for decades.
:::

?? What mechanism does betting against beta rest on? What did Novy-Marx and Velikov show, and why is a long-only fund better for a retail account?
!! Leverage-constrained investors, such as mutual funds and pensions, buy high-beta stocks to get more equity exposure, which overprices them. The long-short version needs the leverage that breaks it in stress, while a long-only fund such as USMV keeps roughly half the Sharpe ratio without borrowing.
