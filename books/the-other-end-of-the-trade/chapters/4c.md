Derivatives are not a trick or a casino. They exist because real economic actors have real risks they want to transfer, and other actors are willing to take the other side at a price. The market's job is matching the two. Five distinct economic functions justify the existence of derivatives markets: hedging, price discovery, leverage, completing markets, and the insurance that pays the variance risk premium.

::: plain
Chapter 4A showed that a derivative is zero-sum between its two parties before costs. Zero-sum in dollars is not zero-sum in welfare. A farmer who locks in a price gives up some expected profit to remove a risk that could ruin the farm. By the expected utility of Chapter 1C, that trade can leave the farmer better off. The other side is paid to hold the risk, so both can come out ahead. A bet between two gamblers only moves money from one to the other.
:::

## Hedging: the original purpose

A farmer wants to lock in the price of corn before the harvest. A pension fund wants to lock in the cost of equity exposure for a decade. An airline wants to lock in the cost of jet fuel for the next year. A pharmaceutical company expecting a payment in a foreign currency wants to lock in the exchange rate.

All of them buy futures or options that pay off if the adverse scenario occurs. That transfers the price risk to a counterparty, often a speculator or market maker, who is willing to bear it for a premium. **[[Hedging]]** is the original economic justification for derivatives. It is traceable back to ancient grain markets and was formalized in modern form in the 19th-century Chicago Board of Trade.

::: worked A farmer's hedge, two ways
**Step 1: the exposure.** Suppose a farmer expects 10,000 bushels at harvest, and the futures price for harvest delivery is $4.50 a bushel. The crop's value moves one for one with the harvest price: a straight line the farmer already holds.

**Step 2: the futures hedge.** Selling futures on 10,000 bushels at $4.50 adds the mirror-image line, so the two sum to a flat $45,000. The farmer gives up the upside to remove the downside.

**Step 3: the put hedge.** Buying puts struck at $4.50 for a hypothetical $0.20 a bushel instead sets a floor of $45,000, less the $2,000 premium, and keeps the upside.

| Harvest price | Crop alone | With futures sold | With puts bought |
|---|---|---|---|
| $3.80 | $38,000 | $45,000 | $43,000 |
| $5.20 | $52,000 | $45,000 | $50,000 |
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: A hedger's three lines. The reader picks a hedger (a farmer, an airline buying fuel, an exporter receiving foreign currency) and sees its exposure as a line against the price at the horizon. Two toggles add a futures hedge, drawn as the mirror-image line, or a put hedge, drawn as a hinge, and the summed position is drawn in bold. Sliders set the hedge ratio and the put's strike and premium. It shows the futures hedge flattening the line and the put hedge flooring it at the cost of its premium.]

The size of global hedging activity is enormous: the BIS tracks foreign-exchange derivatives notional outstanding at roughly $100 trillion. Interest-rate swap notional outstanding exceeds $500 trillion. Commodity derivative notional is dwarfed by these but is still in the trillions.

For retail traders, the analogous personal hedge is rare but real, and it is hedging in the original sense. Someone with concentrated equity exposure, such as a tech employee with vested stock or a deferred compensation plan, might buy protective puts against a price decline.

## Price discovery

The price of a future or an option encodes the market's collective forecast of where the underlying is going. The [[VIX]] index is a forecast of the 30-day expected volatility of the S&P 500. It is extracted from the strip of SPX options by the variance-replication formula that Chapter 9A derives. Futures curves for crude oil, natural gas, copper and gold encode market expectations of supply, demand and storage costs at several horizons. Interest-rate futures and Eurodollar futures encode the market's expectation of central bank policy.

Read these prices with Chapter 4B in mind: its carry formula pins an index future to spot, the interest rate and the dividends, so the index's own forward-looking price lives mostly in its options. An option price, in turn, is a forecast with a premium in it: the last section of this chapter explains why the VIX runs above the volatility that follows it, on average.

Without derivatives markets, these forward-looking prices would not exist. Policymakers consume the information they provide: central banks watch interest-rate futures to gauge the market's expectation of their own future actions. So do corporate executives, whose CFOs use commodity futures to inform inventory decisions, and academic researchers, whose entire literatures study what derivatives markets say about future risk premia. The economic value of price discovery is harder to quantify than the value of hedging, but it is plausibly comparable in importance.

For options markets specifically, the **[[implied volatility surface]]**, the two-dimensional grid of implied volatilities across strikes and expirations, is a remarkably rich object. It tells you not just the average expected future volatility but also the asymmetry of expected moves, the [[skew|volatility skew]]. It also gives the [[term structure]] of expected volatility: [[contango]] when longer expiries carry higher implied volatility than shorter ones, [[backwardation]] when they carry lower. The surface is one of the primary inputs into the global financial risk management apparatus (Chapter 5G).

The selected row is drawn below as a smile, labelled with its skew, and the selected column as a term structure, labelled contango or backwardation. Presets show a calm surface and a stressed one. ## Leverage and capital efficiency

A futures contract or an option provides the same economic exposure as holding the underlying, with much less capital posted. ES futures let you control roughly $300,000 of SPX exposure with $15,000 to $22,000 of initial margin. That is leverage of \(300{,}000/22{,}000 \approx 14\) to \(300{,}000/15{,}000 = 20\) times on a notional basis. That is not just more risk but **capital efficiency**, which enables strategies that would otherwise be impossible.

A **[[risk parity]]** allocator wants to hold equity-like risk and bond-like risk in equal proportion. A $1 portfolio of 60% bonds and 40% equities cannot do it, because bonds have much lower volatility than equities. To match the equity risk, the bond allocation must be levered. Without futures markets, the leverage path does not exist, and neither does the strategy: risk parity as a category exists because of futures markets.

For two assets with volatilities \(\sigma_e\) and \(\sigma_b\), weights \(w_e\) and \(w_b\), and zero [[correlation]], the portfolio's variance is

\[ \sigma_p^2 = w_e^2\sigma_e^2 + w_b^2\sigma_b^2 \]
{#eq:twoasset}

Each term of [eq:twoasset] is one asset's contribution to the variance, so equal risk means \(w_e\sigma_e = w_b\sigma_b\).

::: worked Risk parity with hypothetical volatilities
**Step 1: where the risk sits.** Take hypothetical volatilities of 16% for equities and 5% for bonds, with 40% of the capital in equities and 60% in bonds. Then [eq:twoasset] gives contributions of \((0.4 \times 0.16)^2 = 0.0041\) and \((0.6 \times 0.05)^2 = 0.0009\). The equities carry \(0.0041/0.0050 = 82\%\) of the variance on 40% of the capital.

**Step 2: equal risk.** Setting \(0.16\,w_e = 0.05\,w_b\) with the weights summing to 1 gives \(w_e = 1/4.2 = 23.8\%\) and \(w_b = 76.2\%\). Each contributes \(0.238 \times 0.16 = 0.038\), so the portfolio's volatility is \(0.038 \times \sqrt{2} = 5.4\%\).

**Step 3: the leverage.** To run at a target volatility of 10%, the allocator scales both by \(10/5.4 = 1.86\): $0.44 of equities and $1.41 of bonds for each $1 of capital. The extra $0.86 of exposure, beyond the $1 of capital, comes from futures.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: A risk parity calculator. The reader sets the volatilities of equities and bonds, their correlation and a target volatility. Two pie charts compare a portfolio's split of capital with its split of risk, first for a chosen mix such as 40% equities and 60% bonds, then for the equal-risk weights. A readout gives the leverage the target needs and the dollars of each asset per $1 of capital. It shows equities dominating the risk of a bond-heavy mix, and leverage as the price of balance.]

Similarly, statistical arbitrageurs running long-short equity strategies need to deploy capital both long and short with low net exposure. Futures and options let them do so with high capital efficiency, freeing balance sheet to deploy across many positions.

For retail traders, the use of derivatives for capital efficiency is more contentious. Options leverage can compound losses as easily as gains, and many retail accounts have been destroyed by over-leveraged option positions. The ES futures route, favored by [[Section 1256|Section 1256 contract]], gives retail a clean leveraged equity exposure (Chapter 4B). But the appropriate sizing is typically much smaller than retail traders naively assume (Chapters 1E and 3D).

## Completing markets

Some payoffs cannot be expressed by linear instruments alone. "Pay me if SPX falls more than 20% over the next year, but only then" is not a position you can build by holding stocks and bonds. You need an option: with SPX at 6000, a one-year put struck at 4800 pays nothing unless the fall exceeds 20%. Derivatives let market participants construct arbitrary payoff functions.

The key word is holding, because the mathematical foundation of options pricing (Chapter 7A) is the proof that any continuous payoff can be replicated by a dynamic portfolio of the underlying and cash. That portfolio must be traded continuously as the price moves, which in real markets has costs and gaps (Part VI). A static holding of stocks and bonds cannot do it.

The economic term is market completeness: in a **[[complete market]]**, any conceivable contingent payoff can be constructed from primitive securities. The Arrow-Debreu state-price decomposition shows that markets are complete when there are as many independent securities as there are independent states of the world.

::: worked Completing a three-state market
**Step 1: two securities, three states.** Suppose the index ends in one of three states, down, flat or up, at 80, 100 or 120. A bond pays 1 in every state and the index pays 80, 100 or 120. Any mix of \(a\) bonds and \(b\) units of the index pays \(a + 80b\), \(a + 100b\) or \(a + 120b\): equal steps across the states, a straight line. No mix can pay 1 in the down state alone.

**Step 2: add a put.** A put struck at 100 pays 20, 0 or 0, so \(1/20\) of a put pays 1 only in the down state: an **[[Arrow-Debreu security]]** for that state.

**Step 3: build the rest.** One unit of the index, less 100 bonds, plus one put pays \(-20 + 20 = 0\), 0 and 20. So \(1/20\) of that pays 1 only in the up state. It is also \(1/20\) of a call struck at 100, an identity that Chapter 5F calls put-call parity. The bond less the other two pays 1 only when flat. Three independent securities complete a three-state market.
:::

[FIGURE IDEA: A state-price builder. Three columns stand for the down, flat and up states, and the reader adds securities (a bond, the index, puts and calls at chosen strikes) as rows of payoffs. The figure reports whether the securities span all three states and, when they do, solves for the holdings that build any target payoff the reader types, such as "1 only if down". With only the bond and the index, the target for the down state is flagged unreachable. It shows an option completing a market that stocks and bonds leave incomplete.]

In reality, markets are incomplete: there are vastly more possible states of the world than there are securities. But options dramatically expand the set of payoffs that can be priced and hedged. This is also why options markets are sometimes called "synthetic markets": they create payoff profiles that exist nowhere in the real economy, and let participants trade them. The [[defined-outcome ETF]] category of Chapter 4A is an example of this completion in action. These ETFs use FLEX options to construct payoffs, capped upside and partial downside protection over fixed periods, that retail investors could not otherwise access.

[FIGURE IDEA: A defined-outcome payoff. The fund's return over its period is drawn against the index's return, with sliders for the buffer (the first part of a loss the fund absorbs) and the cap on the gain. Below it, the same payoff is decomposed by the slope rule of Chapter 4B into the index and the options that build it, each drawn as its own line. It shows a payoff that no holding of stocks and bonds can produce, built from a few listed pieces.]

## Selling insurance: the variance risk premium

Selling options is structurally analogous to running an insurance company. Buyers want protection against an event, and sellers take on the risk of that event at sufficient compensation. Premiums are paid upfront, and claims are paid only if the event occurs.

The **[[variance risk premium]]** (VRP) is the empirical observation that, on average across decades and across markets, **option sellers earn more than the actuarially fair price** for the risk they assume. The premium they collect, on average, exceeds the expected payouts they make. The VRP is the compensation that draws sellers into the market in the first place.

::: worked An insurer's book, in miniature
**Step 1: the fair price.** Suppose a policy pays $1,000 with a probability of 2%, so that its expected payout, the actuarially fair price, is \(0.02 \times \$1{,}000 = \$20\).

**Step 2: the premium.** If sellers charge $25, the expected profit per policy is \(0.98 \times \$25 - 0.02 \times \$975 = \$5\). The seller keeps $25 in 98 cases out of 100 and loses $975 in the other two: positive on average, with a long left tail, the negative skew of Chapter 1B.
:::

The size of the VRP is roughly 3 to 5 percentage points of annualized variance, depending on the sample period and the strike selection. Bondarenko's data on the [[CBOE PUT Index]] (Chapters 1B and 1D) show that the index has captured this premium consistently since 1986. It delivered Sharpe ratios in the 0.6 to 0.7 range with a skew of approximately −2.1, exactly what the story of a premium paid for negative skew predicts.

Units matter here, because the Edge Book, the source of Chapter 10C, measures the same gap in [[volatility points|volatility point]] instead, at about 4 on average. Implied volatility of 20% against realized volatility of 16% is a gap of 4 volatility points, but in variance it is \(0.20^2 - 0.16^2 = 0.0144\), or 1.44 percentage points.

The economic explanation for why the VRP persists is structural. Institutional hedgers, such as pension funds, endowments and structured-product issuers, systematically overpay for downside protection, because their mandates require them to remain hedged regardless of price. Retail traders also tend to overpay for upside speculation, in lottery-ticket calls on meme stocks. The structural buyers of options drive prices up. The structural sellers, market makers and systematic premium sellers, collect the premium.

In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's view, the VRP is the single most important source of edge available to retail traders, and Part IX and Part XIII return to it in detail. The Edge Book is more guarded. It reads the apparent excess return of premium selling as largely payment for accepting negative skew. It also expects a self-managed account to keep far less of the premium than the index shows. Chapter 1D cuts the PUT Index's Sharpe ratio of about 0.65 to a realistic retail 0.20 to 0.40. Chapter 10C weighs the premium in full, and Chapter 13A sets out the haircut.

## What is not a justification

Derivatives are not justified by the desire to gamble. They are not justified by the existence of trading volume, or by the fact that people want to trade them. The economic case for derivatives is the five functions above. Without them the market would be zero-sum entertainment, or negative-sum after fees, since every contract's gains and losses cancel before costs (Chapter 4A).

For retail traders this matters operationally. Every options trade you make should be justifiable by one of the five economic functions. Are you hedging? Are you extracting price-discovery information, for example harvesting the variance risk premium because you have identified that implied volatility (IV) is structurally high? Are you deploying leverage for capital efficiency? Are you constructing a payoff that linear instruments cannot give you? Or are you gambling, in which case the math of Chapter 1C catches up with you eventually?

The framework of Part XIII returns to this: every trade should have an articulable mechanism for why its edge exists, mapped to one of the five functions (Chapters 13B and 13C). If you cannot articulate it, you are trading without a thesis, and trading without a thesis is gambling by another name.

[FIGURE IDEA: A five-function classifier. The reader describes a trade by answering a few questions (Do you already hold the risk it offsets? Is it sized by notional against less capital? Does its payoff bend? Are you selling protection? Does it rest on what option prices imply?) and the figure lights the function the trade serves, or "none: no thesis". Worked presets include a protective put, an at-the-money put sold, an ES position and a short-dated lottery call. It turns the chapter's decision rule into a checklist.]

::: familiar
A subgroup effect with no prespecified hypothesis and no biological rationale is the first thing a reviewer discounts. Among many comparisons it is the likeliest product of chance, and the one least likely to replicate. A trade with no mechanism is the same finding in a brokerage account. It may have worked in a backtest, but nothing says why it should keep working.
:::

::: practice
A protective put is the retail hedge in its plainest form. Suppose you hold 5,000 shares of your employer's stock at $100, and buy puts struck at $90 for a hypothetical $3 a share. The puts cost $15,000, and at expiry your holding cannot be worth less than \(5{,}000 \times \$90 - \$15{,}000 = \$435{,}000\), a worst case of 13% down. You pay the premium in every year the stock does not fall, as an insurer's customer does.
:::

::: warn When it fails
A hedge protects only against the price its contract references. If the contract's underlying differs from what you actually hold, the gap between the two, the basis risk, stays with you. The insurance function fails in the other direction: the seller's rare large claim arrives in a crash. The PUT Index's maximum drawdown was about 33% (Chapter 1E).
:::

::: rule
Before each trade, write one line naming its function:

- **Hedging**: it offsets a risk you already hold, and you accept its cost.
- **Price discovery**: it acts on what option prices imply, such as implied volatility structurally above realized.
- **Leverage**: it gives exposure you want with less capital, sized by notional.
- **Completing a payoff**: it gives a shape that stocks and bonds cannot.
- **Insurance**: it sells protection at a premium you have reason to think is rich.

If none fits, do not trade.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: calling a speculation a hedge
A hedge offsets a risk you already hold. Buying puts on an index you do not own, or calls on a stock you do not hold, is a position, not a hedge. It needs its own reason from the five functions, like any other position.
:::

With the family, its shapes and its reasons in place, Part V zooms in on the case this book is about: the option contract. The mechanics of premium, the Greeks, volatility and the dealer ecosystem that prices and hedges options all build on the framing of this part.

::: recap
- Derivatives exist to move risk to whoever is paid to hold it. Zero-sum in dollars, they can still leave both sides better off.
- Five functions justify them: hedging, price discovery, leverage and capital efficiency, completing markets, and insurance, which pays the variance risk premium.
- Option sellers earn more than the actuarially fair price on average, with negative skew. The two sources differ on how much of that a retail account keeps.
- Every trade should map to one of the five functions. A trade that maps to none is a gamble.
:::

::: check
?? A company expects €10 million in six months and sells it forward at $1.10 per euro. What does it receive in dollars if the spot rate then is $1.00, and if it is $1.20?
!! It receives $11 million either way. At $1.00 the euros are worth $10 million and the forward gains \((1.10 - 1.00) \times 10{,}000{,}000 = \$1{,}000{,}000\). At $1.20 they are worth $12 million and the forward loses $1 million. The hedge removed both the loss and the gain.

?? With equity volatility of 20%, bond volatility of 5% and zero correlation, what weights give equal risk, what is the portfolio's volatility, and what leverage reaches 10%?
!! Equal risk needs \(0.20\,w_e = 0.05\,w_b\), which with weights summing to 1 gives \(w_e = 20\%\) and \(w_b = 80\%\). Each asset then contributes \(0.2 \times 0.20 = 0.04\), so the portfolio's volatility is \(0.04 \times \sqrt{2} = 5.66\%\). Reaching a target of 10% then takes \(10/5.66 = 1.77\) times leverage.

?? In the three-state market, how do you build a security that pays 1 only in the flat state from the bond, the index and the put?
!! Take one bond, less the down security (\(1/20\) of a put), less the up security (\(1/20\) of the index, less 5 bonds, plus \(1/20\) of a put). That is 6 bonds, less \(1/20\) of the index, less \(1/10\) of a put. As a check, the combination pays \(6 - 4 - 2 = 0\) when down, \(6 - 5 - 0 = 1\) when flat and \(6 - 6 - 0 = 0\) when up.

?? Name the function, if any, behind each of these four trades.

- SPX puts bought against an index fund you hold.
- Monthly at-the-money XSP puts sold because implied volatility runs above realized.
- One ES contract bought instead of $300,000 of index shares.
- Weekly calls on a meme stock bought because it "feels ready to pop".

!! The first is hedging, and the second is insurance that harvests the variance risk premium. The third is leverage used for capital efficiency. The fourth maps to none of the five functions, which makes it a gamble.
:::
