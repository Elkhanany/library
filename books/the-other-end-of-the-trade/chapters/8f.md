Chapter 7B split the change in an option's [[delta]] into three parts, [eq:7b/ddelta]. They are [[gamma]] times the move in spot, vanna times the change in [[implied volatility]], and charm times the time that passes. It worked the last two for one dealer's book, a post-FOMC vol crush and a weekend. This chapter adds them up across the market, as Chapter 8A added up gamma.

::: plain
Gamma is hedging that waits for the price to move. Vanna and charm do not wait. When implied volatility falls after an event, or a day passes, the deltas of the options dealers hold change, and so do their hedges, with the price standing still. These flows are predictable in their timing, because volatility tends to fall after scheduled events and the calendar never stops. Their direction is not predictable without the one fact public data lack: which side of each option the dealers hold.
:::

## Vanna and charm across the market

### Vanna exposure and charm exposure

Chapter 8A summed each option line's [[dollar gamma]], signed by the dealers' side, into gamma exposure. The same sum over vanna gives **[[vanna exposure]]**, or VEX, the change in the dealers' delta, in dollars, when implied volatility rises one point:

\[ \text{VEX} = \sum_i s_i\,\text{vanna}_i \times 0.01\,S\,M N_i \]
{#eq:vex}

Here the sign \(s_i\) is +1 if dealers are long line \(i\) and −1 if they are short it. The line's vanna per unit of volatility is \(\text{vanna}_i\), and 0.01 turns that into one [[volatility point]]. The product \(S M N_i\) turns units of delta into dollars, with \(M\) the multiplier and \(N_i\) the [[open interest]]. The heatmap of Chapter 8C draws VEX strike by strike in its VEX view. When implied volatility changes by \(\Delta\sigma\) points, dealers trade \(-\text{VEX} \times \Delta\sigma\) to stay flat: they sell when VEX is positive and volatility rises.

The same sum over charm gives **[[charm exposure]]**, the change in the dealers' delta, in dollars, as one calendar day passes with spot and volatility fixed:

\[ \text{CHEX} = \sum_i s_i\,\frac{\text{charm}_i}{365}\,S\,M N_i \]
{#eq:chex}

with charm measured per year, time running forward. Each day, dealers trade \(-\text{CHEX}\).

### The signs, wing by wing

Vanna changes sign at the strike (Chapter 5D). For an [[out-of-the-money]] put it is negative: a rise in volatility makes the put's delta more negative. For an out-of-the-money call it is positive: a rise makes the call's delta larger. So a dealer short options on both wings carries VEX of opposite signs on each:

- **Short out-of-the-money puts:** positive VEX. A rise in volatility makes the puts' deltas more negative, so the dealer, short them, is longer and sells. A fall in volatility makes it buy.
- **Short out-of-the-money calls:** negative VEX. A rise in volatility makes the dealer shorter, so it buys. A fall makes it sell.

Skylit's education pages say that "A dealer who is short options carries positive vanna". That holds on the put wing only. Its own worked example, a dealer short puts who buys back stock as volatility falls, is the put case.

Charm pulls every out-of-the-money delta toward zero as time passes. A dealer short out-of-the-money puts holds a short hedge against them and buys it back as their deltas shrink. A dealer short out-of-the-money calls holds a long hedge and sells it. The two wings trade in opposite directions again.

::: worked A book a month from expiry
**Step 1: the book.** Take the made-up book of this chapter's figure. Customers are long SPX puts struck at 5,700, 5,800 and 5,900, 30,000, 30,000 and 20,000 contracts, all expiring in 28 days, so dealers are short them. Spot is 6,000, implied volatility 18% and the rate 4%.

**Step 2: the hedge.** The puts' deltas are −0.132, −0.222 and −0.336. The dealers' delta through them is therefore long, and their hedge is short futures worth $2.38 billion, $3.99 billion and $4.03 billion: $10.40 billion in all.

**Step 3: three exposures.** By the Black-Scholes [[Greeks]] of Chapter 5D, the book's GEX is −$2.7 billion per 1% move, its VEX +$0.53 billion per volatility point, and its CHEX −$0.14 billion a day.

**Step 4: three trades.** A 1% fall in the index makes the dealers sell about $2.7 billion. A one-point rise in implied volatility makes them sell $0.53 billion, and a one-point fall buy as much. And each calendar day, with nothing else changing, they buy $0.14 billion.
:::

### Time and volatility, one variable

With no interest rate and no dividends, an option's delta depends on volatility and time only through their combination \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), the standard deviation of the log price to expiry. A day passing shrinks \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), and so does a fall in implied volatility. To delta they are the same thing, and charm is vanna in another guise:

\[ \text{charm} = -\text{vanna} \times \frac{\sigma}{2T} \]
{#eq:charmvanna}

with charm per year of calendar time and \(T\) in years. One day with \(T\) days left does to delta what a fall in implied volatility of \(\sigma/(2T)\) does, with \(T\) in days. With \(\sigma\) in percent, the fall is in volatility points.

::: grind Why time acts like a fall in volatility
**Step 1: delta's argument.** With \(r = q = 0\), a call's delta is \(N(d_1)\), and
\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K)}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{2} \]
so \(\sigma\) and \(T\) enter only through \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\). A put's delta is \(N(d_1) - 1\), which changes by the same amounts.

**Step 2: vanna.** Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma\) gives \(\partial d_1/\partial\sigma = -d_2/\sigma\), so \(\text{vanna} = -\varphi(d_1)\,d_2/\sigma\), the formula of Chapter 5D.

**Step 3: charm.** Differentiating with respect to \(T\) gives \(\partial d_1/\partial T = -d_2/(2T)\). Time running forward shortens \(T\), so \(\text{charm} = \varphi(d_1)\,d_2/(2T)\).

**Step 4: the ratio.** Dividing Step 3 by Step 2 gives \(\text{charm}/\text{vanna} = -\sigma/(2T)\), which is [eq:charmvanna]. With a rate, charm carries an extra term, \(-r\varphi(d_1)/(\sigma\sqrt{T})\), so the identity holds only nearly.
:::

::: worked A day as a fall in volatility
**Step 1: a month out.** At 18% with 28 days left, a calendar day is worth \(18/(2 \times 28) = 0.32\) volatility points. The book's VEX is $0.51 billion per point at a zero rate, so a day's charm is \(0.32 \times 0.51 = \$0.16\) billion of buying. At a zero rate its CHEX is exactly −$0.16 billion, and dealers trade minus CHEX. With the figure's 4% rate the day's flow is $0.14 billion: the rate term trims it, and the direction is the same.

**Step 2: the last week.** With 5 days left a day is worth \(18/10 = 1.8\) points, and with 1 day left, 9 points. The same calendar day moves the hedge far more as expiry nears, which is why charm grows like \(1/T\).
:::

Two consequences follow for dealers short out-of-the-money puts. A vol crush and the passage of time push their hedges the same way, toward buying. And over a window that ends at expiry, the two decide only the timing. At expiry every out-of-the-money delta is zero, whatever path volatility took, so the day-by-day trades add up to the hedge held at the start. Volland's user guide says, in a vendor's words, that charm "is the opposite sign of vanna on each strike", because it is computed for "+1 day passing". The identity [eq:charmvanna] is the reason, exactly with no rate and nearly with one.

### What the evidence says

The calendar is real arithmetic, but its effect on prices is less certain. A Goldman Sachs strategist's teaching note, known through republication, describes the mechanics the figure draws: into expiration "near-the-money options' gamma rises, while far out-of-the-money options' gamma falls". The academic evidence is thin:

- **Stivers and Sun (2013)**, in the *Journal of Banking and Finance*, study the weekly returns of S&P 100 stocks over option-expiration weeks, the weeks ending on a month's third Friday. Those returns "tend to be high" against less option-active stocks, against the same stocks' other weeks and against risk-based alphas. The following weeks "underperform modestly". The authors suggest two partial contributors. One is "delta-hedge rebalancing by option market makers, with a reduction in short-stock hedge positions over the OE week", which is charm. The other is "declining risk perceptions over the OE week, as measured by option-derived implied volatilities", which works through vanna. The sample is single stocks, not the index.
- **Wang (2013)**, a doctoral thesis, finds that "weekly return reversals are significantly stronger surrounding option expiration days". The hedging pressure "mainly comes from index options rather than individual stock options", and "index option prices tend to be high before option expiration". It is a thesis chapter, not peer-reviewed.
- **Golez and Jackwerth (2012)** name the time decay of hedges as one channel of the expiration effects they find in S&P 500 futures (Chapter 8E).

No study found tests charm flows into weekly or same-day SPX expirations. For the monthly expiration, one [[working paper]] tests exactly the channel the figure draws, at the moment the options settle.

### Into the monthly a.m. settlement

Monthly SPX options settle on the Friday-morning special opening quotation, built from the first trade in each component stock, not on a closing price (Chapters 2A and 12H). Baltussen, Terstegge and Whelan, in an SSRN working paper first circulated in 2023 as "The Equity Derivative Payoff Bias", compare that settlement value with Thursday's close.

In the 2023 version, the settlement value is close to the prior close on ordinary days, but on settlement days it "exceeds Thursdays close by a significant margin". The overnight return "raises (lowers) S&P 500 call (put) option payoffs, inducing a wealth transfer of around $4 billion per year in SPX options alone". The authors find the bias "most consistent with inventory management by option market makers and/or market manipulation by sophisticated investors", both relying on an illiquid period before settlement.

A later version, circulated as "The Derivative Payoff Bias", reports that opening prices on third Fridays "are systematically biased upward and fully reverse by midday". The transfer is put at about $2.5 billion a year. The aggregate delta of dealers' option positions "drifts down into" the third-Friday expiry, "generating an incentive to buy equities". That drift predicts overnight equity returns into third-Friday expirations, and on no other days. In summaries of that version, SPX calls paid $10.46 billion on the average third-Friday morning against $10.23 billion at Thursday's close, $230 million a month. Retail investors, net short delta, lose at least $3.8 billion. One of the authors, Julian Terstegge, summarizes the channel in his 2025 doctoral thesis. In his words, "Charm spikes immediately before derivative payoffs are calculated and thus creates a derivative payoff bias, which exceeds $3bn a year".

Three cautions go with it. It is a working paper, and its headline numbers changed between versions, so quote the version. The a.m.-settled monthly is one contract design: PM-settled [[SPXW]] options and same-day expiries settle differently, and the paper says nothing about them. And the effect reverses by midday, the signature of price pressure rather than information.

Index expiration effects have a history. Stoll and Whaley (1987) studied the quarterly expirations of 1984 and 1985, when index futures and options expired together on the close, the day traders call **[[triple witching]]**. Last-hour volume and volatility were higher and the market tended to fall. The average price effect, measured by the next morning's reversal, was "about 0.4 per cent of the closing index value". S&P index option expirations on their own produced no abnormal movements. Their 1991 follow-up found smaller effects after the move to settlement on the open in 1987. The classic expiration effect came from unwinding futures arbitrage, not from option hedging.

## After expiration: the "window of weakness"

Practitioners have a name for the weeks after a monthly expiration. Cem Karsan of Kai Volatility Advisors describes the flows into expiration in talks and interviews. In his account, dealers "are often buying back stock as risk premia decays (charm) and buying back stock as implied volatility compresses (vanna)". After an expiration, in his words, comes "a window of non-strength some people call it a window of weakness", "a period where flows have subsided". A secondary account says that "the one to two weeks following the day of the monthly options expiration" are "a short period whereby supportive Vanna and Charm flows ... are much less prominent". Karsan also calls expiration week "the best time to sell volatility". Who coined the phrase is not established.

The **[[window of weakness]]** has a mechanical reading in this chapter's terms. After expiration the puts that were decaying are gone. The next monthly puts have about four weeks left, and by [eq:charmvanna] a calendar day is worth far fewer volatility points to them. In the figure, week 1's ordinary days bring $0.17 billion to $0.20 billion of buying, against $0.72 billion to $0.85 billion in week 4 before Friday. If dealers are short the puts, the supportive flow is weakest right after an expiry. Bank desks use a similar image: after one expiration, a Nomura note, as a vendor quoted it, described the aggregate "$Gamma" of [[QQQ]] dropping by over 50%, "unclenching" the index.

Three things keep it lore rather than knowledge:

1. **No test on SPX.** No published test by the practitioners who use the phrase was found, and none on the S&P 500 index. The closest academic result is Stivers and Sun's modest underperformance of S&P 100 stocks in the week after expiration.
2. **Its direction is a [[sign convention]].** The support into expiration needs dealers short the decaying puts. If customers are net sellers of those puts, dealers are long them, and the same decay makes dealers sell into expiration and stop selling after it.
3. **Calendar effects overlap.** The weeks after the monthly expiration also hold month-end flows, the [[VIX]] expiration, which practitioners pair with the SPX expiration as "OPEX and VOLEX", and in some months a Fed meeting. Nothing found separates them. No source on the VIX expiration's own effect was found.

Some dated flows are known. The [[JPM collar]] resets every quarter, about 35,000 SPX contracts per leg by vendors' accounts, and its put strike is one of the few whose holder is public (Chapters 8B and 8E). Vendors claim the market "sells off meaningfully after the quarterly roll", a claim no published test supports. [[Defined-outcome ETFs|defined-outcome ETF]] reset their [[FLEX options|FLEX option]] on fixed dates (Chapter 4A), another calendar no heatmap of listed open interest shows.

The window of weakness has the design problem of the "July effect" studies in teaching hospitals, which ask whether outcomes worsen when new residents start. ## The vol-crush rally

Chapter 7B worked one dealer's post-FOMC unwind. Implied volatility falls 7 points after the announcement, the deltas of out-of-the-money puts shrink, and a dealer short 5,000 of them buys back tens of thousands of [[share-equivalents|share-equivalent]]. Summed across the market, that is the vol-crush rally practitioners describe, the vanna half of Karsan's account, after the [[IV crush]] that follows a scheduled event (Chapter 12A). The Dealer Mechanics Lab puts its typical contribution after an FOMC decision at 0.3% to 0.5% of SPX at most (Chapter 7B). By [eq:vex], the flow is VEX times the fall in volatility. The figure's book, at +$0.53 billion per point, would buy about $3.7 billion on a 7-point crush, by the linear estimate that Chapter 7B found runs low.

What the research has tested is narrower:

- **No direct test.** The practitioner claim that falling implied volatility after events forces dealer buying of the index has no direct test in what the research found.
- **A tested channel from volatility to the index.** Bangsgaard and Kokholm (2025), in the *Journal of Banking and Finance*, study the end-of-day demand of VIX [[exchange-traded products|exchange-traded product]] for [[VIX futures]]. The [[market makers|market maker]] on the other side "can hedge their volatility exposure by trading SPX options and futures". The products' demand "is a strong predictor of the end-of-day SPX futures return in-sample and out-of-sample". The move partly reverses afterward, "suggesting that VIX futures hedging activities can move the SPX futures market for reasons unrelated to price discovery". It is a volatility-to-index hedging channel, measured, though not the option dealers' vanna itself.
- **The falling volatility of expiration week**, one of Stivers and Sun's two suggested contributors.

Two findings qualify every vanna number on a map. Hull and White (2017), in the *Journal of Banking and Finance*, show that "the normally calculated delta does not minimize the variance of changes in the value of a trader's position". The reason is that price and volatility move together. Their minimum-variance delta "takes account of both price changes and the expected change in volatility conditional on a price change". It reduces hedging variance by about 42% for the highest-strike calls and about 17% for [[in-the-money]] ones, in search summaries of their S&P 500 results. A dealer who hedges that way has already folded the typical vanna of a price move into its delta. Vanna then adds a separate flow only when volatility moves without the price, as after an event, or when the VIX jumps with the index still.

Willeboordse (2026), in *Finance Research Letters*, compares Black-Scholes gamma with the convexity option positions actually show, across five underlyings from 2020 to 2025. At a daily horizon the correlation turns "significantly negative (−0.25 to −0.47 across symbols)", more so as [[vega]] exposure grows relative to gamma exposure. The author reads it as "substantial vega contamination through negative spot–IV comovement". A map that adds a GEX flow and a VEX flow computed separately can count the same hedge twice.

The one practitioner test of vanna exposure found points the same way. FlashAlpha, testing its own product on 1,972 [[SPY]] days from 2018 to 2026, reports that VEX correlates +0.72 with the VIX and +0.76 with [[at-the-money]] implied volatility. Once those are controlled for, its correlation with next-day [[realized volatility]] is −0.01 (p = 0.77): VEX "is mostly a VIX proxy". Chapter 8G gives the study.

## Vendors' rules, checked against Black-Scholes

Skylit builds two rules on these Greeks. In its words, "GEX is the hand brake; VEX is the slope of the hill", and "GEX governs the range; VEX governs the drift". And on tenor: "For options 0–5 DTE, gamma is dominant; for 7–180 DTE, vanna is dominant". Both can be checked against the Black-Scholes Greeks of Chapter 5D, though neither has been tested against prices.

### "GEX governs the range; VEX governs the drift"

The rule has a basis in [eq:7b/ddelta]. The gamma term needs a move in price, so it shapes how each move develops: it damps or amplifies moves, which sets the range. The vanna and charm terms fire with the price standing still, so they can push the same way day after day, which is a drift. Three things qualify it:

- **Charm is the steadier drift.** Vanna pushes only while implied volatility moves, and its direction follows the direction of the move in volatility. The calendar-driven drift is charm's.
- **Both drifts carry a sign convention.** A drift upward into expiration needs dealers short the puts. Under the other sign, the drift reverses.
- **The flows are not separate.** When volatility moves with the price, as it usually does in the index, a minimum-variance hedger has folded part of the vanna into the gamma hedge. And Willeboordse's result says the two are hard to separate over a day.

### "Gamma for 0 to 5 days, vanna for 7 to 180"

Compare the two hedges per contract on a common footing: the change in the dealer's delta, in dollars, for a 1% move in spot and for a one-point rise in implied volatility. Black-Scholes gives their ratio in one line:

\[ \frac{\text{vanna}}{\Gamma\,S} = -d_2\sqrt{T} \]
{#eq:vannagamma}

so a one-point rise in volatility moves the delta as much as a spot move of \(-d_2\sqrt{T}\) percent. The table takes a put with a delta of −0.25 at each expiry, SPX at 6,000, 18% implied volatility and a 4% rate. The last column is the move in implied volatility, per 1% move in spot, at which the vanna hedge would equal the gamma hedge.

| Days to expiry | Strike | Hedge change, 1% move | Hedge change, 1 vol point | Volatility move to match |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 5,963 | $202,000 | $7,000 | 29 points |
| 5 | 5,920 | $91,000 | $6,900 | 13 points |
| 7 | 5,906 | $76,000 | $6,900 | 11 points |
| 30 | 5,822 | $37,000 | $6,600 | 5.6 points |
| 90 | 5,728 | $21,000 | $6,200 | 3.4 points |
| 180 | 5,664 | $15,000 | $5,800 | 2.6 points |

::: worked Reading the table at 7 days
**Step 1: the two hedges.** One contract of the 25-delta put with 7 days left has a hedge that changes by $76,000 for a 1% move in the index and by $6,900 for one point of implied volatility.

**Step 2: the ratio.** By [eq:vannagamma], \(d_2 = 0.650\) and \(\sqrt{7/365} = 0.138\), so one volatility point equals a spot move of \(0.650 \times 0.138 = 0.090\%\). Dividing, \(1/0.090 = 11\): implied volatility would have to move 11 points for each 1% of spot for vanna's hedge to match gamma's.

**Step 3: at 180 days.** The same ratio is \(0.548 \times 0.702 = 0.385\%\), and the break-even falls to 2.6 points per 1% of spot.
:::

Four things follow, and none of them is a threshold at 5 or 7 days:

- **For options of the same delta, vanna's weight grows smoothly with \(\sqrt{T}\).** The vanna hedge per contract barely changes with expiry, while the gamma hedge falls like \(1/\sqrt{T}\). That is the grain of truth in the rule.
- **At a fixed strike it hardly changes.** For a put struck 5% below spot, the break-even is about 3.5 points per 1% at 5 days, 3.4 at 30 and 2.9 at 180.
- **At the money, vanna is close to zero at every expiry.** Same-day gamma sits almost entirely at the money, which is why gamma dominates same-day books, whatever the calendar says.
- **Dominance depends on a market fact.** How far implied volatility moves for each 1% of spot is not in Black-Scholes, which holds volatility constant. On an event day, when volatility falls with the price still, vanna is the whole of the change at any expiry.

Skylit also maps VEX by its position against spot, in four cells:

- "+VEX below spot (support in calm, rug-pull risk in stress)";
- "–VEX below spot (fragile floor in calm, fast bounces in stress)";
- "+VEX above spot (breakouts in calm, caps in rising vol)";
- "–VEX above spot (soft ceiling in calm, squeeze fuel during vol spike)".

Read with the signs above, the matrix is consistent with the mechanics. Positive VEX below spot is dealers short puts, who buy as volatility falls in calm and sell as it rises in stress. It is untested, and it inherits the sign convention of every cell. So does Skylit's warning that when volatility spikes, back-end positive VEX "creates significant sell flow" that is "entirely invisible in a front-month gamma map". That is the vanna loop of Chapter 9A, seen on a map.

Volland infers each trade's dealer side from the options order feed, in a 2023 working paper by DeLorenzo for the vendor. The paper claims that "option dealers are predominantly more sensitive to changes in implied volatility than to the movement in the underlying equity". It also claims that charm "yields a significant edge when applied in the right time frame relative to expiration". Neither claim comes with a test a reader can check.

## The link to the vanna loop

Chapter 9A followed the vanna loop in a sell-off: the index falls, the VIX rises, the deltas of out-of-the-money puts deepen, and dealers short them sell, which pushes the index lower. The vol-crush rally is the same loop run backward. With positive VEX from short puts, a rise in volatility makes dealers sell and a fall makes them buy. The exposure is the same, and the sign of the move in volatility decides the direction.

The measured channel sits between the two: Bangsgaard and Kokholm's VIX-futures hedging moves S&P 500 futures at the close and then partly reverses. Chapter 8H works the same arithmetic for March 2020, with the index held still while volatility rises.

It shows the sell-off loop of Chapter 9A and the vol-crush rally as one exposure meeting volatility moves of opposite sign.]

::: warn When it fails
Every flow in this chapter assumes that dealers are short the puts, and often the calls, that customers bought. Under the other sign each flow reverses. The flows are small beside news: the Lab's post-FOMC estimate is at most a few tenths of a percent. A vol crush is a tendency, not a law, and an event can raise volatility instead. Black-Scholes vanna and charm also misstate the hedges of dealers who use minimum-variance deltas, and over a day the Greeks computed separately overlap.
:::

::: rule
- **Before you lean on a calendar flow, name the sign.** State which side of the puts and calls you assume dealers hold, and what the flow does under the other side.
- **Size it to its evidence.** The expiration-week effect is documented for single stocks, at modest sizes, and the settlement bias in one working paper. The window of weakness and index vanna rallies are untested. None of them supports a position that needs a large move.
- **Close SPX monthlies on Thursday** (Chapter 12H), since the settlement print has leaned upward in one working paper's sample.
- **Read VEX beside the VIX,** not as news of its own. In the one test found, VEX carried little beyond implied volatility itself.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: "charm always buys into expiration"
Charm pulls out-of-the-money deltas toward zero. That makes dealers buy only if they are short the puts. If they are short calls too, the call wing sells, and the net can be small or negative. The direction of every charm flow is a statement about who holds what.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: adding the GEX flow and the VEX flow
A map's GEX and VEX are computed separately from Black-Scholes, but in the index volatility moves with price. A dealer hedging with a minimum-variance delta has already hedged part of the vanna, and daily convexity is contaminated by vega. Adding the two flows can count the same hedge twice.
:::

::: recap
- Summed across the market, vanna gives VEX, dollars of dealer delta per volatility point, and charm gives CHEX, dollars of delta per day. Dealers trade minus each. Short puts give positive VEX, short calls negative.
- With no rate, delta depends on \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) only, so a day is a vol crush of \(\sigma/(2T)\) points. Over a window ending at expiry, a fall in volatility changes when dealers unwind, not how much.
- Into expiration, charm grows like \(1/T\), with weekend-sized Mondays. Stivers and Sun find strong option-expiration weeks in S&P 100 stocks, and a working paper finds an upward bias at the monthly a.m. settlement that reverses by midday.
- The window of weakness and index vanna rallies are practitioner lore: plausible if dealers are short the puts, and untested on SPX.
- Black-Scholes gives vanna more weight against gamma for longer-dated options of the same delta, smoothly as \(\sqrt{T}\), with no break at 5 or 7 days.
:::

::: check
?? Dealers hold a book of short puts whose VEX is +$0.5 billion per volatility point. After an FOMC decision, implied volatility falls 4 points with the index unchanged. What do they trade?
!! They trade minus VEX times the change: \(-0.5 \times (-4) = +\$2\) billion. They buy $2 billion of index exposure, because the fall in volatility shrinks the long delta their short puts give them.

?? At 20% implied volatility with 10 days to expiry, how many volatility points is one calendar day worth to delta, with no rates?
!! By [eq:charmvanna], \(20/(2 \times 10) = 1\) point. With 2 days left it is \(20/4 = 5\) points.

?? In the charm figure, why does a 5-point fall in implied volatility leave the 20-day total at $10.40 billion?
!! The window ends at expiration, where every out-of-the-money delta is zero whatever volatility did on the way. The daily trades must add up to the hedge held at the start. The fall only brings the buying forward, $1.63 billion of it out of week 4.

?? A vendor says vanna dominates options from 7 to 180 days. For a 25-delta put with 7 days left, how far must implied volatility move per 1% of spot for vanna's hedge to equal gamma's?
!! About 11 points, from \(1/(d_2\sqrt{T}) = 1/0.090\). At 180 days it is 2.6 points. Black-Scholes gives a smooth trend with expiry, not a threshold, and whether vanna dominates depends on how far volatility actually moves, which Black-Scholes does not say.

?? Why is the window of weakness a sign-convention claim?
!! The supportive flow into expiration is dealers buying back hedges on puts they are short, as charm and falling volatility shrink the puts' deltas. If customers sold those puts, dealers are long them, and the same decay makes dealers sell into expiration instead. The story reverses with the assumed side.
:::
