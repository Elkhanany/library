Gamma told you how delta changes when spot moves. But delta also changes when implied volatility moves, with no spot move required, and when time passes, with no spot move and no volatility move. These two derivatives, **[[vanna]]** and **[[charm]]**, generate predictable hedging flows that are largely invisible to retail and relentless on a calendar-time schedule. Understanding them is the difference between knowing that dealers hedge and knowing *when* they hedge.

This chapter works the two most important of those flows with full dollar arithmetic: the post-FOMC vanna unwind and the charm-driven Monday gap. It then places the rest of the [[higher-order Greeks]] in the hedged book. Chapter 5D defines and derives each Greek, so this chapter recaps them only as far as the arithmetic needs.

::: plain
A dealer's hedge is right for one spot, one implied volatility and one moment. Change any of the three and the hedge is wrong. Gamma measures how wrong it gets when spot moves, vanna when implied volatility moves, and charm as the clock runs, weekends included. Whenever the hedge is wrong the dealer trades to fix it. Those trades reach the market you trade in.
:::

## Three ways a hedge goes stale

Over a short interval, an option's delta changes for three reasons at once:

\[ d\Delta \approx \Gamma\,dS + \text{vanna}\,d\sigma + \text{charm}\,dt \]
{#eq:ddelta}

Here \(dS\) is the move in spot, \(d\sigma\) the change in implied volatility as a decimal, and \(dt\) the calendar time that passes, in years. Each coefficient is a Greek of Chapter 5D: gamma, then vanna, \(\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma\), then charm, \(\partial\Delta/\partial t\), with \(t\) running forward.

Now put the option in a dealer's book. A dealer short \(n\) put contracts, each on 100 units of the underlying, carries \(-100n\Delta\) units of delta through them. A put's delta is negative, so that is a long position. The [[delta hedging]] of Chapter 6C offsets it with a short position of the same size. When the put's delta changes by \(d\Delta\), the dealer must trade

\[ \text{hedge trade} = 100\,n\,d\Delta \]
{#eq:trade}

units of the underlying: a purchase when the put's delta rises toward zero, and a sale when it falls further below zero.

The first term of [eq:ddelta] is the gamma chase of Chapter 6D. The other two fire with spot standing still. The vanna term fires when implied volatility moves, as it does around scheduled events. The charm term fires as the calendar turns, whether or not the market is open.

### What the first-order bet leaves out

The same Greeks also change the hedged book's profit and loss. Chapter 7A's formula kept only the gamma term. Expanding the option's value in spot and implied volatility together, one order further, gives the hedged book's gain above cash over an instant:

\[
\begin{aligned}
d\Pi - r\Pi\,dt \approx{}& \tfrac12\Gamma S^2\Big[\Big(\frac{dS}{S}\Big)^2 - \sigma^2dt\Big] \\
&+ \nu\,d\sigma + \text{vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma \\
&+ \tfrac12\,\text{volga}\,(d\sigma)^2
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:pnl2}

The first line is Chapter 7A's bet on realized against implied. The second line is the vega caveat, plus vanna's cross term, which pays or charges when spot and volatility move together. The third is volga, [[vega]]'s own curvature. For an index, where falls in spot tend to come with rises in implied volatility (Chapter 5G), the second line rarely rests.

::: worked A sell-off on a hedged put
Take Chapter 5D's example put: spot 100, strike 95, three months to expiry, implied volatility 20%, and no rates or dividends. Its gamma is 0.0340, its vega 17.02 per unit of volatility, its vanna −0.788 and its volga 22.2. Spot falls 2 and implied volatility jumps 5 points, to 25%, too fast for time to matter.

**Step 1: the gamma term.** With no time passing, the first line of [eq:pnl2] is \(\tfrac12 \times 0.0340 \times 2^2 = 0.068\) a share.

**Step 2: the vega term.** \(\nu\,d\sigma = 17.02 \times 0.05 = 0.851\).

**Step 3: the two cross-effects.** Vanna adds \(-0.788 \times (-2) \times 0.05 = 0.079\), and volga adds \(\tfrac12 \times 22.2 \times 0.05^2 = 0.028\).

**Step 4: add up and check.** The terms sum to 1.026 a share for a delta-hedged long put. Repricing the put gives 0.995, so the estimate is within about 3%. The dealer short this put, delta-hedged, loses about $1.00 a share, $100 a contract. Chapter 7A's first-order term, gamma, accounts for 7 cents of that dollar. Vanna's cross term alone is larger than gamma's.
:::

## Vanna: the post-FOMC unwind

### Vanna, briefly

Chapter 5D derived vanna's closed form, the rate at which delta changes with volatility, which equals the rate at which vega changes with spot:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{vanna} &= \frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\sigma} = \frac{\partial\nu}{\partial S} \\
&= -e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\,\frac{d_2}{\sigma}
\end{aligned}
\]
{#eq:vanna}

The intuition is that implied volatility sets how spread out the option's pricing distribution is. Higher IV puts more probability mass in the tails. For an [[out-of-the-money]] (OTM) call, that means more chance of finishing in the money, which raises its delta. For an OTM put, whose delta is negative, it does the same: the delta becomes *more* negative, further from zero.

**Vanna is large and negative for OTM puts** struck below spot, where \(d_2\) is positive (Chapter 5D). When the [[VIX]] spikes, OTM put deltas become more negative, larger in magnitude. A dealer short these puts, because customers were buying protection, becomes effectively *longer* the underlying through the option leg. To remain delta-neutral, the dealer must sell more of the underlying.

Conversely, when IV crashes, as it typically does after an event, OTM put deltas decay toward zero. The dealer's effective long exposure from the puts evaporates. The dealer was holding an extra short hedge against the puts. It now holds more of that hedge than it needs, so it buys some back.

### The unwind, with full arithmetic

The FOMC is the Federal Reserve committee that sets US interest rates. Suppose SPX is at 4,500. A dealer is short 5,000 SPX OTM puts at strike 4,400, sold to an institutional hedger who bought protection ahead of the FOMC meeting. The contracts have 30 days to expiration. Before the meeting, the IV at that strike is 25%, with a 4% rate and a 1.5% dividend yield.

::: worked The post-FOMC vanna unwind
**Step 1: the book before the meeting.** Black-Scholes with these inputs puts the put's delta at about −0.36. Through the puts, the dealer is long \(5{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.36 = 180{,}000\) share-equivalents. It hedges by being short 180,000 SPX share-equivalents, through ES futures or SPY, so its net delta is zero.

**Step 2: the vol crush.** When the announcement lands, the IV at the put's strike crushes from 25% to 18%, a fall of 7 [[volatility points|volatility point]]. Vanna at this strike is about −0.005 per volatility point, so the vanna term of [eq:ddelta] moves the put's delta by \((-0.005) \times (-7) = +0.035\).

**Step 3: the new exposure.** The put's delta moves from −0.36 to about −0.325. The dealer's long exposure through the puts falls to \(5{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.325 = 162{,}500\) share-equivalents, a drop of about 17,500.

**Step 4: the trade.** The dealer is now over-hedged by 17,500 short share-equivalents. To re-flatten, it buys 17,500 SPX-equivalents, as [eq:trade] says. At SPX 4,500 that is about $79 million of index exposure: 350 ES contracts at $50 a point, or about 175,000 SPY shares.
:::

Treat these numbers as orders of magnitude. They come from a standard BSM calculator at the stated parameters. Vanna and delta vary continuously with all inputs, so verify them with your own calculator. The load-bearing point is the scale: tens of thousands of share-equivalents per 5,000-contract book.

Running that check teaches something. Repricing at the stated inputs gives a delta of −0.352 at 25% and −0.308 at 18%, a change of 0.044 rather than 0.035. The linear estimate falls short because vanna itself grows as IV falls, from about −0.0045 per point at 25% to −0.0088 at 18%. The repriced trade is about 22,200 share-equivalents, so the vanna estimate understates the unwind by about a fifth. The order of magnitude stands.

The reader drags the post-event IV and sets the book's size in contracts, and readouts give the linear and the repriced change in delta and the dealer's trade in share-equivalents, ES contracts and SPY shares. It would show the tangent peeling away from the curve as the crush deepens.]

Across the entire dealer community, the aggregate flow scales with the size of the book. A 500,000-contract aggregate put book produces several hundred million to over a billion dollars of mechanical buying flow in the hour after the announcement. Scaled one for one from the example, 500,000 contracts would buy 1.75 million share-equivalents, about $7.9 billion at SPX 4,500. The range is well below that. It would fit an aggregate book spread over strikes and expiries whose average put carries far less vanna than a 30-day put 2% out of the money.

[FIGURE IDEA: Where the aggregate vanna flow comes from. A heatmap over strikes from 80% to 105% of spot and expiries from 1 to 90 days gives the dealer's buying per contract for a 5-point fall in IV, from repricing. The reader draws a distribution of open interest over the grid, or picks a preset, and a readout sums the book's flow in dollars. It would show how much a book's flow depends on where its puts sit, and why a whole-market total does not scale one for one from a single strike.]

**The aggregate is the post-FOMC ramp that has nothing to do with anyone reading the dot plot favorably.** (The dot plot is the Fed's chart of its policymakers' projections for rates.) It is dealers reversing their pre-event hedges into the vol crush. The rally is mechanical, not interpretive.

This is the standard FOMC pattern. Vol bids up into the event, because hedgers position for it. IV crushes after the event regardless of its direction, because the event is now in the past. Dealers then buy back hedge stock as their put deltas decay. The most this typically contributes to the SPX move after an FOMC decision is 0.3% to 0.5%. It operates, though, with high reliability across years.

## Charm: the Monday gap

### Charm, briefly

Charm is the rate of change of delta with respect to time. Even with spot and IV frozen, delta drifts, because the option's effective probability of finishing in the money (ITM) evolves as the expiration window shrinks. Chapter 5D gives the Black-Scholes formula. With no dividends, a call and a put share it:

\[ \text{charm} = -\varphi(d_1)\,\frac{2rT - d_2\,\sigma\sqrt{T}}{2T\,\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]
{#eq:charm}

The formula gives charm per year, so divide by 365 for the drift per calendar day. Measured with time running forward, an OTM put's charm is positive: its delta rises toward zero as the days pass. Charm is small far from expiration and grows like \(1/T\) as expiration approaches. **It also operates on calendar time**, including weekends, even though markets are closed. This is the technical reason for the empirical regularity of Monday gaps and the "charm bid" into [[OPEX Fridays|options expiration]], the Fridays on which listed options expire.

### The weekend, with full arithmetic

Charm scales with \(1/T\) and runs on calendar time, not trading time. Take a 30-day OTM put with a delta of −0.25 at Friday's 4:00 PM ET close. Its remaining \(T\) is measured in calendar days. Friday's close to Monday's 9:30 AM open is 65.5 hours of calendar time, 2.73 days and roughly 9% of the option's remaining life, although markets were open for none of those hours.

::: worked The charm weekend gap
**Step 1: the book on Friday.** Take 100,000 SPX OTM put contracts, a typical institutional book, struck at 97% of spot, \(K = 0.97 \times S\), with 30 calendar days to expiry and a delta of −0.25. The dealer is short them, so its long exposure through the puts is \(100{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.25 = 2{,}500{,}000\) share-equivalents. It is hedged with 2,500,000 SPX-equivalents short, in ES or SPY.

**Step 2: the weekend passes.** Spot on Monday morning is identical to Friday's close, with no overnight news. Time to expiry has shrunk from 30 to 27.3 calendar days, because 65.5 hours is 2.73 days.

**Step 3: the drift in delta.** Computed from Black-Scholes at these parameters, the put's charm is about 0.004 of delta per calendar day, toward zero. Over 2.73 days the charm term of [eq:ddelta] gives \(0.004 \times 2.73 = 0.011\). The put's delta on Monday morning is about \(-0.25 + 0.011 = -0.239\).

**Step 4: the trade.** The dealer's long exposure through the puts falls to \(100{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.239 = 2{,}390{,}000\) share-equivalents, a reduction of about 110,000. The dealer did not unwind any of its hedge over the weekend while the put deltas decayed in its favor, so it opens Monday over-hedged by 110,000 short share-equivalents. To re-flatten, it buys 110,000 SPX-equivalents. At SPX 4,500 that is about $495 million, or 2,200 ES contracts.
:::

The example does not state the implied volatility, and the result depends on it. With no rates or dividends, a put struck at 97% of spot has a delta of −0.25 at an IV of about 16%. Black-Scholes then gives a charm of about 0.0033 a day, a Monday delta of about −0.240 and a trade of about 97,000 share-equivalents. That is the same order of magnitude as the example's 0.004 a day and 110,000.

The drift is small per contract. But a dealer carrying 100,000 such contracts opens Monday over-hedged by enough share-equivalents to push the SPX morning open up by a few basis points to a tenth of a percent. Aggregated across the entire dealer community, the buying flow becomes meaningful. These flows of mechanical buying, about 100,000 share-equivalents per book, hit the tape in the first 30 minutes of Monday trading. They explain a meaningful chunk of the "Monday gap-up" anomaly that has been documented in SPX since the 1990s. **The bull does not show up on Monday. The hedger does.**

::: familiar
Charm works like a drug's elimination. The concentration falls on the clock, whether or not anyone doses or measures, so a patient's level on Monday morning reflects the whole weekend of clearance. An option's delta drifts on the calendar in the same way. A dealer who does nothing over the weekend arrives on Monday with a hedge sized for Friday's delta, with the correction due at the open.
:::

### Calendar time against trading time

There is a subtle technical issue here. Some option pricing models use calendar time for charm, with \(T\) measured in 365-day years. Others use trading time, with \(T\) measured in 252-day years of trading days. The choice affects the size of the weekend drift: calendar-time models predict larger weekend charm drift than trading-time models. On a pure trading clock, Saturday and Sunday add no time at all, so the delta at Monday's open is still Friday's. The drift then arrives only as Monday's session runs.

Empirically, the Monday-gap-up anomaly is more consistent with calendar-time charm decay than with trading-time decay. That is why most dealer risk systems use calendar-time conventions for short-dated positions. It is a pedantic detail, but the kind of pedantic detail that separates dealers who correctly anticipate Monday morning flows from those who do not.

## The rest of the family, in the book

The Greek menagerie keeps going. Chapter 5D defines each of the others. Here is the operational summary of when each matters in a hedged book:

- **[[Volga]]**, also called vomma, is the second derivative of value with respect to volatility: vega's convexity. It matters most for vol-of-vol risk and for VIX options. It played a major role in Volmageddon (Chapter 8C), when short positions in the VIX call wing had massively negative vomma. As the VIX spiked, their convex losses overwhelmed the linear vega losses.
- **[[Speed]]**, \(\partial\Gamma/\partial S\), is the third derivative of value with respect to spot. In [[0DTE]] options, speed is enormous near the strike. It explains why a 1% move can flip a book of options from heavily long to heavily short gamma within minutes, as Chapter 5D works through. That is the source of the "gamma flipping" intraday character that makes 0DTE risk management qualitatively different from longer-dated risk management (Chapter 9B).
- **[[Color]]**, \(\partial\Gamma/\partial t\), is the rate at which gamma changes over time. For at-the-money (ATM) options, color is positive near expiration: gamma compresses *into* the ATM strike as expiration approaches. By the final hour of OPEX Friday, virtually all the dealer gamma is concentrated within $5 to $10 of spot either way. This is the mathematical source of [[pin risk]] (Chapter 5F): the gamma concentration is what creates the gravitational pull toward strikes.
- **[[Veta]]**, \(\partial\nu/\partial t\), is the rate at which vega decays over time. It is mostly relevant for managing the time decay of long-dated positions where vega is the dominant exposure. A vol-targeting fund running long VIX call positions as crash insurance worries about veta in a way that retail traders rarely do.
- **[[Zomma]]**, \(\partial\Gamma/\partial\sigma\), is the rate at which gamma changes with volatility. It shows up in the risk management of large vega positions and in the dynamics of the volatility surface.

For understanding aggregate dealer flow, vanna and charm cover roughly 95% of the dealer-relevant higher-order dynamics. The rest get bookkeeping attention from risk managers but rarely move the broad tape on their own. If you understand vanna and charm, you understand most of what is published about "dealer flows" with any analytical rigor, and the rest is mostly noise.

Chapter 5D puts the same roughly 95% on five Greeks rather than two: vanna, charm, volga, speed and color. Its reason is that all five matter operationally for understanding dealer flow, since volga drives losses in a volatility spike while speed and color reshape gamma on expiry day. The reason for two here is that the other three rarely move the broad tape on their own. The two counts disagree, and both are rough estimates.

Near spot 102 at the starting settings, vanna reads about −0.48 per unit of volatility, or −0.0048 per volatility point, close to the FOMC example's −0.005. Here is what to look for as you slide. Vanna changes sign at the strike: positive on the OTM-call side, where spot is below the strike, and negative on the OTM-put side, where spot is above it. This sign asymmetry is what makes vanna flow direction-dependent. Rising IV makes a dealer short downside puts sell, but makes a dealer short upside calls buy, because an OTM call's delta rises with IV. Charm is largest a short way either side of the strike rather than at it, about −1.7 a year near spot 93 and +1.2 near 107 at the starting settings. Drag the days to expiry down to 5 and both peaks grow five to seven times larger and crowd within 3 points of the strike: charm is the mathematics of "pinning".

Although the figure does not draw gamma itself, color shows gamma's growth into the strike. Pick color: at the strike it is positive, about 0.34 a year at 30 days and about 5.0 at 5 days, so the gamma peak is growing, and faster as expiry nears. Beyond spots of about 93 and 107, color turns negative, as gamma drains out of the wings into the peak. Speed changes sign at the top of the gamma hump, just below the strike, positive below it and negative above.

The joint surface view, the heatmap, is what risk managers actually look at. A position is not risky just because of any single Greek's value. It is risky because of how all the Greeks evolve jointly as conditions change. A short [[straddle]] two days before expiration is not dangerous because of gamma alone. It is dangerous because gamma, vanna, charm and vomma are all simultaneously at their peak magnitudes. Any combination of a spot move, an IV move and time passing then activates all of them against the position at once. Chapter 5D calls the gamma, vanna and charm part of this the convexity catastrophe. It works a straddle with spot a point above the strike, because vanna and volga fade to almost nothing exactly at the strike.

::: practice
- **Watch the calendar that dealers watch.** A scheduled volatility event, a weekend and an OPEX Friday each move dealer hedges with no news at all. A ramp after an FOMC decision, or a firm Monday open, may be hedging rather than information.
- **Recompute your own Greeks after every weekend and every event.** Your short options' deltas drift by charm over a weekend and by vanna after a vol crush, exactly as a dealer's do. Adjust any hedge before Monday's open, not after it.
- **Convert share-equivalents before sizing anything.** One SPX share-equivalent is one unit of the index. At SPX 4,500 that is $4,500 of exposure, one fiftieth of an ES contract or ten SPY shares.
- **Reprice for any move in IV of more than a few points.** The FOMC example's linear vanna estimate missed a fifth of the trade.
:::

::: warn When it fails
Every flow in this chapter assumes that dealers are short the puts customers buy. If customers are net sellers of those puts, dealers are long them. Each sign then flips, so a vol crush makes dealers sell. The vol crush is also a tendency, not a law: an FOMC surprise can raise implied volatility instead. The flows are small against news, at most a few tenths of a percent after an FOMC decision and a few basis points to a tenth of a percent at a Monday open. And every number here is a Black-Scholes derivative, so for a large move in spot or volatility, reprice rather than multiply.
:::

::: rule
- **Size any trade that leans on these flows to their stated size.** A flow worth 0.3% to 0.5% at most after an FOMC decision cannot justify a position that needs a larger move to pay.
- **Before holding short options over a weekend, compute Monday's delta on Friday.** Multiply the position's charm per day by 2.73 and by its size in units. If the result matters to you, hedge the difference before the close.
- **Before holding a short straddle into its last two days,** read gamma, vanna, charm and volga together on the heatmap. Hold only a size you could carry through all of them turning against you at once.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: getting the dealer's hedge backward
A dealer short puts is long delta through them, and hedges by *selling* the underlying. When the puts' delta shrinks, after a vol crush or over a weekend, the dealer *buys*. Reading "short puts" as "short delta" flips every flow in this chapter and turns a rally into a sell-off on paper.
:::

::: warn Common mistake: trusting a linear estimate for a large move
Vanna and charm are slopes at one point. Over a 7-point vol crush vanna nearly doubled, so the linear estimate missed a fifth of the trade. Over a long weekend near expiry, charm itself grows as \(1/T\). Use the Greek to see the direction and the rough size, then reprice for the number.
:::

::: recap
- A hedge goes stale three ways: \(d\Delta \approx \Gamma\,dS + \text{vanna}\,d\sigma + \text{charm}\,dt\). The last two need no move in spot.
- One order beyond Chapter 7A, a hedged book also earns or pays vega, vanna and volga terms, which dominate a sudden sell-off that lifts implied volatility.
- A post-FOMC vol crush pulls OTM put deltas toward zero, so dealers short those puts buy back hedges. The flow is tens of thousands of share-equivalents per 5,000-contract book, with the linear estimate low by about a fifth.
- Charm runs on calendar time, so a weekend's drift in put deltas arrives as dealer buying at Monday's open.
- Vanna and charm carry most of the scheduled dealer flow. Volga, speed and color matter most in stress and near expiry.
:::

::: check
?? A dealer is short 2,000 contracts of an OTM SPX put with a delta of −0.30 and a vanna of −0.006 per volatility point. Implied volatility falls 4 points after an event. Estimate the hedge trade.
!! By [eq:ddelta], the put's delta moves by \((-0.006) \times (-4) = +0.024\). By [eq:trade], the dealer buys \(2{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.024 = 4{,}800\) share-equivalents. At SPX 4,500 that is $21.6 million, or 96 ES contracts.

?? The weekend example's book faces a three-day weekend, from Friday's 4:00 PM close to Tuesday's 9:30 AM open, with charm still 0.004 a day. What does the dealer buy at Tuesday's open?
!! The weekend is \(65.5 + 24 = 89.5\) hours, or 3.73 days, so the put's delta rises by \(0.004 \times 3.73 = 0.0149\). The dealer buys about \(100{,}000 \times 100 \times 0.0149 = 149{,}000\) share-equivalents. On a pure trading clock it would buy none of that at the open.

?? Why does the post-FOMC ramp tend to come whatever the Fed decides?
!! The vol crush follows the event regardless of its direction, because the uncertainty it priced is gone. Through negative vanna, the fall in IV pulls OTM put deltas toward zero. Dealers short those puts are then over-hedged and buy back part of their short hedge.

?? A dealer is short OTM calls, struck above spot, that customers bought. Implied volatility rises. Which way does the dealer trade?
!! It buys. An OTM call's vanna is positive, so rising IV raises the call's delta. The dealer's short calls then carry more negative delta, so it must buy the underlying to stay neutral. A put book and a call book trade in opposite directions on the same IV move, which is the sign asymmetry the figure shows.

?? In the sell-off on the hedged put, why is Chapter 7A's first-order formula a poor guide to the loss?
!! Its only term is gamma's, 0.068 a share. The sudden rise in IV adds a vega term of 0.851, while the joint move adds vanna's 0.079 and volga's 0.028. A move in which spot and volatility jump together is the case the first-order bet leaves out.
:::
