## Part 1. Feedback from dynamic hedging on the underlying

### FreyStremme1997
- Citation: Frey, R., and Stremme, A. (1997). "Market Volatility and Feedback Effects from Dynamic Hedging." Mathematical Finance 7(4): 351-374. DOI 10.1111/1467-9965.00036.
- Type: peer-reviewed article (an earlier version circulated as a Bonn SFB 303 discussion paper and as SSRN 6267).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/d23be63c103556799cf2c3c4807bff4d/); WebSearch listing of RePEc (https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v7y1997i4p351-374.html, which gives vol. 7(4), pp. 351-374, October 1997), Wiley (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9965.00036), SSRN (https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=6267). The volatility formula below was seen only in a WebSearch summary of later papers that restate the model (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.06467, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.11568); I could not open the paper itself, so the exact notation is as the summary gave it.
- Question: how does the demand generated by dynamic hedging strategies change the equilibrium price process, and in particular the volatility, of the underlying?
- Data and method: theory. Two groups: "reference traders", price takers with a demand function (the logarithmic case D(Y,S) = ln(Y^gamma/S) as the summary gives it), and "program traders" whose aggregate demand is phi(t,S), the hedge of xi written identical securities. Market clearing gives the price diffusion.
- Findings: (quoting the abstract) "We derive an explicit expression for the transformation of market volatility under the impact of such strategies. It turns out that volatility increases and becomes time and price dependent. The strength of these effects however depends not only on the share of total demand that is due to hedging, but also significantly on the heterogeneity of the distribution of hedged payoffs." In the logarithmic-demand case the volatility function is v(t,S) = sigma/(1 - rho S dphi/dS) (search summary), where rho measures how much the hedgers' trading moves the price. Hedgers who replicate convex payoffs (phi_S > 0, buying as the price rises: the portfolio-insurer or short-gamma dealer case) raise volatility; the formula gives the opposite for phi_S < 0.
- Limits: a stylized equilibrium, with exogenous reference demand and a single hedged book; the formula breaks down when rho S phi_S approaches 1. The original paper studies the volatility-raising case (hedgers replicating options they are short, in effect); the damping case is the same algebra with the sign reversed, which Jeannin, Iori and Samuel (2008) exploit for pinning.
- Use in the book: the primary published derivation of the effective-volatility formula of Chapter 8B, sigma_eff = sigma/(1 - kappa G), with kappa = rho (price impact) and G = S phi_S (the hedgers' stock-holding convexity, which is the negation of dealer gamma in the book's sign). Its heterogeneity result is also the theoretical root of strike-local effects: a hedged book concentrated at one strike makes the feedback strong near that strike and weak elsewhere, which is what a strike-by-strike heatmap tries to display.

### Frey1998
- Citation: Frey, R. (1998). "Perfect option hedging for a large trader." Finance and Stochastics 2: 115-141. DOI 10.1007/s007800050035.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/cc98507355f25ce5997668c1c1292e46/); WebSearch (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s007800050035, which gives vol. 2, pp. 115-141, 1998). The model equations were seen in a WebSearch summary (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.05661, https://arxiv.org/html/math/0604117 and a Bordag-Frey book chapter); issue number [unverified].
- Question: can a large agent whose trades move prices replicate a derivative payoff, and what does the attempt do to the price process?
- Data and method: theory, continuous time. The price follows dS_t = mu S_t dt + sigma S_t dW_t + rho S_t d(alpha_t), where alpha_t is the large trader's stock holding and rho the "market liquidity parameter" (search summary). Extends Jarrow's discrete-time large-trader work to continuous trading.
- Findings: the replicating strategy solves a nonlinear Black-Scholes PDE; the paper proves existence and uniqueness (abstract). Later papers restate the diffusion coefficient as sigma^2 (1 - rho lambda(S) S V_SS)^(-2), with lambda(S) = 1 in the basic model (search summary). This is exactly the book's sigma_eff^2 = sigma^2/(1 - kappa G)^2, with kappa = rho and G = S V_SS for a trader replicating (that is, short) a claim with value V.
- Limits: a single large trader and linear, permanent impact in log price; no temporary impact, no other hedgers, no strategic response by other traders. The PDE is singular where rho S V_SS reaches 1.
- Use in the book: the cleanest citation for the effective-volatility formula and for its singularity ("edge of feedback explosion"): the squared denominator is Frey's. State its assumptions when quoting it: permanent linear impact, continuous delta hedging of a known book, and feedback below the critical value.

### PlatenSchweizer1998
- Citation: Platen, E., and Schweizer, M. (1998). "On Feedback Effects from Hedging Derivatives." Mathematical Finance 8(1): 67-84. DOI 10.1111/1467-9965.00045.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/9d114d14dac65956b9b5ce28f4e4bec6/); WebSearch (Wiley table of contents https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/toc/14679965/8/1 and summaries giving vol. 8, issue 1, January 1998, pp. 67-84).
- Question: can the technical demand of option hedgers explain the smile and skew in implied volatility?
- Data and method: theory and numerical simulation of SDEs. A microeconomic equilibrium in which aggregate demand for the stock is the sum of a noise term, a term from arbitrage-based (speculative) agents and a term from dynamic hedging of options written on the stock (search summary). Volatility is then determined endogenously by the hedgers' behavior.
- Findings: the abstract: "This leads to a stochastic volatility endogenously determined by agents' trading behavior. By using numerical methods for stochastic differential equations, we quantitatively substantiate the idea that option price distortions can be induced by feedback effects from hedging strategies." A search summary of this literature (whose results included Buis and co-authors 2024) states the sign rule plainly: "a long net gamma position dampens volatility whereas a short position amplifies it"; I could not tell whether that sentence is Platen and Schweizer's or a later paper's restatement.
- Limits: calibrated illustrations, not estimates; volatility in the model is a function of the hedgers' book, which is taken as given.
- Use in the book: supports the claim that hedging feedback changes not only realized volatility but also the shape of implied volatility (smile and skew) through the hedgers' gamma. It links Part VIII to the skew chapters (5E, 5G) and to demand-based pricing.

### SircarPapanicolaou1998
- Citation: Sircar, K. R., and Papanicolaou, G. (1998). "General Black-Scholes models accounting for increased market volatility from hedging strategies." Applied Mathematical Finance 5(1): 45-82. DOI 10.1080/135048698334727.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/7583d00c70fd5fb6a53b99f85a9818d5/); WebSearch (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/135048698334727 and https://econpapers.repec.org/article/tafapmtfi/v_3a5_3ay_3a1998_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a45-82.htm, giving vol. 5, issue 1, pp. 45-82).
- Question: if Black-Scholes hedgers are a sizeable part of the market, how does their hedging feed back into the asset's price process, and from there into option prices?
- Data and method: theory. "Reference traders", the majority, trade on expected gain; "programme traders" follow a Black-Scholes-type dynamic hedging strategy to insure a derivative. Their interaction gives a price process that depends on the hedge; a Black-Scholes argument then gives a nonlinear PDE for the derivative price and the hedge. Solved by perturbation (hedgers a small fraction of the economy), numerically, and analytically.
- Findings: the abstract says these models "do predict increased implied volatilities with minimal assumptions beyond those of the Black-Scholes theory", reduce to Black-Scholes when feedback is removed, and "clearly support the observed increasing volatility phenomenon and provide a quantitative explanation for it". The motivating case is portfolio insurance, which trades with the move.
- Limits: the hedgers insure (replicate) options, so the case studied is the destabilizing one; the magnitude depends on the hedgers' share of the market and the reference traders' demand elasticity, neither estimated here.
- Use in the book: second derivation, independent of Frey-Stremme, that pro-cyclical hedging raises volatility; the perturbation result (small hedger share gives a small correction to sigma) supports the book's point that the typical-day effect is modest and that the formula only bites as the feedback term grows.

### SchonbucherWilmott2000
- Citation: Schönbucher, P. J., and Wilmott, P. (2000). "The Feedback Effect of Hedging in Illiquid Markets." SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics 61(1): 232-272. DOI 10.1137/s0036139996308534.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/fc722f8d17bc5108b274aa6022c0f663/); WebSearch (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220222855_The_Feedback_Effect_of_Hedging_in_Illiquid_Markets, https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Feedback-Effect-of-Hedging-in-Illiquid-Markets-Wilmott-Sch%C3%B6nbucher/44519ebebadbf4ce61e18e17df2573a7a257d5c5; summaries give vol. 61(1), pp. 232-272, 2000).
- Question: how do dynamic trading strategies modify the stochastic process of the underlying when the market is illiquid, and how should options be replicated then?
- Data and method: theory and simulation. Derives the modified price process in the presence of a dynamic trading strategy, the nonlinear feedback from price to strategy, and a nonlinear PDE for option replication; simulates put-option replication.
- Findings: in this literature the volatility becomes sigma_u(t,S) = sigma/(1 - rho lambda(S) S u_SS(t,S)) (WebSearch summary of the family of models; the summary attributes the form to "related work"), which rises as the replicating strategy's convexity grows. The paper illustrates, by simulation, the effect of put-option replication (portfolio insurance) on the underlying; later literature cites it for hedging negative gamma and the 1987 crash (search summary).
- Limits: models one strategy and a stylized liquidity function; I could not see the paper's own statement of when the price process jumps [unverified], so do not attribute a crash threshold to it beyond the singular denominator.
- Use in the book: third derivation of the sigma/(1 - feedback) form, and the explicit link from the mathematics to portfolio insurance and 1987 (Chapter 8C).

### Jarrow1994
- Citation: Jarrow, R. A. (1994). "Derivative Security Markets, Market Manipulation, and Option Pricing Theory." Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 29(2): 241-261. DOI 10.2307/2331224.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/76dd4f27b68b53b7809974125cc495c4/); WebSearch result listing RePEc (https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v29y1994i02p241-261_00.html, giving vol. 29, issue 2, pp. 241-261). Frey (1998) describes itself as extending "prior work of Jarrow" to continuous trading (Consensus abstract of Frey 1998).
- Question: how should options be priced when a large trader's trades move the underlying, and does a derivative market create new ways to manipulate the underlying?
- Data and method: theory, discrete time, with a stock, a money market account and a derivative.
- Findings: (abstract) "it is shown, by example, that the introduction of the derivative security generates market manipulation trading strategies that would otherwise not exist. A sufficient condition is provided on the price process such that no additional market manipulation trading strategies are introduced by a derivative security. Options are priced under this condition, where it is shown that the standard binomial option model still applies but with random volatilities." Its companion, Jarrow (1992), "Market Manipulation, Bubbles, Corners, and Short Squeezes," JFQA, DOI 10.2307/2331322 (Consensus: https://consensus.app/papers/details/6c5849abee935886a041f9e04a8ee511/), defines a large trader as "any investor whose trades change prices" and shows manipulation strategies exist under reasonable hypotheses.
- Limits: existence results, not magnitudes.
- Use in the book: the origin of the "large trader" framework on which every feedback model in this part rests, and of the point that holding options gives a large trader a reason to move the underlying (the manipulation channel of expiry-day pinning, alongside the hedging channel). Later work: Kraft and Kühn (2011), "Large traders and illiquid options: Hedging vs. manipulation," JEDC, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.1482469 (Consensus: https://consensus.app/papers/details/fb6a22c32f5355e5b3c2fa08dc94b955/), find that a large option issuer with price impact hedges only partly and "exploits his ability to manipulate the option's payoff"; Horst and Naujokat (2011), "On derivatives with illiquid underlying and market manipulation," Quantitative Finance, DOI 10.1080/14697688.2011.552517 (Consensus: https://consensus.app/papers/details/ca19d86f44635eaebf931f4bac9ab26c/), build a multi-player equilibrium of option holders who trade the underlying to raise their payoff.

### Loeper2018
- Citation: Loeper, G. (2018). "Option pricing with linear market impact and nonlinear Black-Scholes equations." The Annals of Applied Probability 28(5): 2664-2726. DOI 10.1214/17-AAP1367. Preprint arXiv:1301.6252 (2013); SSRN 2777932.
- Type: peer-reviewed article (preprint versions on arXiv and SSRN).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/fea21ceefaa857458ce990205940bb73/); WebSearch (https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-applied-probability/volume-28/issue-5/Option-pricing-with-linear-market-impact-and-nonlinear-BlackScholes-equations/10.1214/17-AAP1367.full, https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.6252, summaries giving vol. 28, no. 5, pp. 2664-2726, October 2018). The exact form of the PDE was not shown in any result [unverified]; do not quote it from memory.
- Question: how can a contingent claim be replicated exactly when every hedge trade moves the price linearly?
- Data and method: theory. In the model, an order to buy N shares moves the price by lambda N S^2, lambda >= 0 (search summary), which is a relative move of lambda per dollar traded (lambda N S^2 / S = lambda x N S dollars). Existence and regularity of solutions are proved with a Legendre-transform method.
- Findings: (abstract) "We derive a non-linear Black-Scholes Equation that provides an exact replication strategy. This equation is fully non-linear and singular, but we show that it is well posed, and we prove existence of smooth solutions for a large class of final payoffs, both for constant and local volatility." The paper links the problem to hedging with gamma constraints (Cheridito, Soner and Touzi) and to hedging under liquidity costs, and derives "a modified Black-Scholes formula valid for asymptotically small impact parameter".
- Limits: a single hedger with purely permanent linear impact; the singularity (the hedge becomes infeasible where the hedger's gamma times impact reaches a critical value) is handled by restricting payoffs, which is itself informative: exact replication of a concentrated short-gamma book is impossible when impact is large enough.
- Use in the book: the modern, rigorous version of the Frey model, and the natural citation for the simulator's "impact per dollar traded" parameterization: the simulator's lambda (points per $1 billion) is Loeper's linear impact written in index points. Related: Bouchard, Loeper and Zou (2016), "Almost-sure hedging with permanent price impact," Finance and Stochastics (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00780-016-0295-1, seen in a WebSearch result list; details [unverified]); Abergel and Loeper (2017), "Option Pricing and Hedging with Liquidity Costs and Market Impact," book chapter (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-47705-3_2, seen in a WebSearch result list; details [unverified]).

### AndereggUlmannSornette2022
- Citation: Anderegg, B., Ulmann, F., and Sornette, D. (2022). "The impact of option hedging on the spot market volatility." Journal of International Money and Finance 124: article 102627. DOI 10.1016/j.jimonfin.2022.102627. Earlier version: Anderegg, B., Sornette, D., and Ulmann, F. (2019). "Quantification of feedback effects in FX options markets." Swiss National Bank Working Paper 2019-03 (July 9, 2019).
- Type: peer-reviewed article (2022); central-bank working paper (2019).
- Verified via: Consensus for both versions (https://consensus.app/papers/details/26ae2470017f5d8c945bf61b115a874d/ and https://consensus.app/papers/details/de54b360930a5caf957bb9b0e97bcf32/); WebSearch (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261560622000304, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358837574_The_Impact_of_Option_Hedging_on_the_Spot_Market_Volatility, https://www.snb.ch/en/publications/research/working-papers/2019/working_paper_2019_03, https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/snbsnbwpa/2019-03.htm). The model is also part of Florian Ulmann's ETH Zurich doctoral thesis, Diss. ETH No. 27982 (https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/mtec/chair-of-entrepreneurial-risks-dam/documents/dissertation/PhD%20Thesis%20Florian%20Ulmann_final.pdf, seen in a result list). The closed-form volatility expression and the estimated impact coefficients were not shown in any result [unverified].
- Question: how much does delta hedging by option market makers change spot volatility in the two largest FX pairs?
- Data and method: theory plus regression. Two aggregated traders, an option market maker (OMM) and an option market taker (OMT), "whose exposures reflect the total outstanding positions of all option traders in the market". Different hedge ratios leave a net delta-hedge flow, which moves the spot through "a simple linear permanent impact model". The OMM's gamma is reconstructed from DTCC trade-repository data (said to cover about 20% of worldwide option transactions, per a search summary [unverified share]). Sample: 21 October 2017 to 30 June 2018; EURUSD and USDJPY; daily and intraday frequencies.
- Findings: spot volatility "is increased (decreased) by a negative (positive) gamma exposure of the OMM". The reconstructed OMM gamma is negative, because option takers are net long options. "Quantitatively, a negative gamma exposure of the OMM of approximately -1000 billion USD (which is around what we observe from our reconstructed OMM data) leads to an absolute increase in volatility of 0.7% in EURUSD and 0.9% in USDJPY." The 2019 version adds that, with equal hedge ratios, the larger USDJPY effect "can be directly explained by the higher market impact of a transaction in the USDJPY spot market", EURUSD being more liquid.
- Limits: eight months of data; the gamma is a reconstruction from a partial trade repository, in the same way that equity GEX is a reconstruction from open interest; "0.7%" and "0.9%" are absolute additions to annualized volatility (volatility points), and the unit of the "-1000 billion USD" gamma is as the authors define it (not stated in the abstracts) [unverified]. FX is an OTC dealer market, not listed index options.
- Use in the book: the only published study that fits the Frey-type formula to real aggregate market-maker gamma and reports the effect in volatility points: a large negative dealer gamma adds under one volatility point in the two deepest currency pairs. It supports the sign rule of Chapter 8B and its "modest on typical days" message; it also shows that the effect scales with market impact (liquidity), the book's kappa.

### SornetteUlmannWehrli
- Citation: Sornette, D., Ulmann, F., and Wehrli, A. "On the Directional Destabilizing Feedback Effects of Option Hedging." SSRN working paper 4087222. DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4087222. Year of posting [unverified].
- Type: working paper (SSRN).
- Verified via: WebSearch (https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4087222, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4087222, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360108632_On_the_Directional_Destabilizing_Feedback_Effects_of_Option_Hedging), which showed the title, the three authors and the abstract.
- Question: beyond volatility, can option hedging create directional instabilities, short-lived locally explosive trends, in the underlying?
- Data and method: a model in which delta hedging affects the drift of the underlying, coupled with a method for detecting short-lived, locally explosive trends in the expected price path.
- Findings: (search summary of the abstract) directional instabilities "indeed occur with higher or lower intensity (depending on the option parameters and its delta) when option hedging is present", and the effect on price stability "is strongly asymmetric, with a significantly more pronounced effect on the spot price when the option market maker is short compared to long the option".
- Limits: working paper; data and effect sizes not visible in the summary.
- Use in the book: theory for the "amplifying side is worse than the damping side is good" asymmetry, which matches the convex shape of sigma/(1 - kappa G) in Chapter 8B (equal steps of G do far more on the destabilizing side), and for squeezes as locally explosive trends (Chapter 8D).

### EgebjergKokholm2024
- Citation: Egebjerg, S., and Kokholm, T. (2024). "A Model for the Hedging Impact of Option Market Makers." SSRN working paper 4936978 (posted August 26, 2024). https://ssrn.com/abstract=4936978.
- Type: working paper (SSRN, 2024 version); no journal publication found.
- Verified via: WebSearch (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/4936978.pdf?abstractid=4936978&mirid=1 and https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4936978, whose summaries give the title, both authors, the date and the abstract). Coefficient sizes not shown [unverified].
- Question: what is the full price impact of option market makers' delta hedging, including hedges of changes in their option inventory, and not only the gamma-driven rebalancing that earlier models study?
- Data and method: a model with directional price impact from OMM delta hedging, split into a "gamma effect" (rebalancing an unchanged book as spot moves) and an "inventory effect" (hedging new option trades). Empirics: high-frequency SPX option trades, used to estimate changes in the OMMs' net option position, related to subsequent SPX futures returns.
- Findings: with constant fundamental volatility and drift, hedging impact makes the observed price show "both stochastic volatility and stochastic drift". Studying the gamma effect alone "can underestimate the total effect when the OMM's inventory adjustments reinforce the gamma impact and overestimate it when the adjustments move in the opposite direction". The change in OMM net delta "is closely linked to subsequent SPX futures returns", and both the gamma and inventory components "are significant and with impact in line with the model predictions".
- Limits: working paper; the imputation of OMM positions from trades carries the same trade-signing uncertainty as any GEX estimate.
- Use in the book: the model that fits the book's SPX setting most directly. It adds a term the GEX framework leaves out: customer flow that changes dealer inventory (for example a burst of 0DTE call buying) is hedged at once and moves the price even when the stock of gamma is unchanged. A heatmap of open-interest gamma shows the first effect only. It also supports the simulator's structure (a stock of options at a strike plus linear impact) while warning that inventory changes during the day are a second channel.

### BuisEtAl2024
- Citation: Buis, B., Pieterse-Bloem, M., Verschoor, W. F. C., and Zwinkels, R. C. J. (2024). "Gamma positioning and market quality." Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control 164: article 104880. DOI 10.1016/j.jedc.2024.104880. Earlier version: SSRN 4109301.
- Type: peer-reviewed article (simulation study).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/b9581f4be60754e197a9cc2d4515038b/); WebSearch (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165188924000721, https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/dyncon/v164y2024ics0165188924000721.html giving vol. 164, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4109301, https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/gamma-positioning-and-market-quality/). Initials of Verschoor and Zwinkels [unverified]; the number of simulation runs and parameter values were not shown [unverified].
- Question: how does the net gamma positioning of dynamic hedgers affect market quality (liquidity, volatility, stability, price discovery)?
- Data and method: agent-based simulation with a zero-intelligence limit order book, to which dynamic (delta) hedgers with a positive or negative net gamma are added; stressed and normal scenarios compared.
- Findings: (abstract) "the presence of dynamic hedgers enhances market liquidity under normal conditions. However, positive gamma helps sustain liquidity in stressed scenarios, while negative gamma depletes it. We find that an increase in the net gamma positioning of dynamic hedgers reduces volatility and increases market stability, whereas a negative gamma positioning increases volatility and makes the market more prone to failure. Price discovery typically worsens when dynamic hedgers become more prevalent, regardless of the sign of their positioning." A search summary adds that the price stays closest to the fundamental value with positive gamma, while negative gamma gives "a more pronounced spread around the fundamental value", and that negative gamma amplifies "both volatility and price impact".
- Limits: simulation with zero-intelligence traders, not an estimate; no strike structure is reported in what I saw, so it says nothing direct about pinning at a strike [unverified].
- Use in the book: the best modern support for the two-regime table of Chapter 8B, with two additions the book should carry: (1) the sign affects liquidity, not only volatility (negative gamma depletes liquidity in stress, which is the mechanism by which kappa rises when G does, the "kappa is not a constant" warning of 8B); (2) hedging worsens price discovery whatever its sign, so a pinned or damped market is not a better-informed one. The authors' policy remark (steering hedgers' net gamma as a tool, most useful for illiquid instruments) matches the finding that SPX, the deepest market, should show the smallest effects.

### AubertChevalierLyVath2025
- Citation: Aubert, P., Chevalier, E., and Ly Vath, V. (2025). "Option market making with hedging-induced market impact." arXiv:2511.02518 (posted November 5, 2025); published as "Option Market Making with Hedging-Induced Market Impact," Applied Mathematical Finance, DOI 10.1080/1350486X.2026.2671725 (volume and pages [unverified]).
- Type: working paper (arXiv 2025) and peer-reviewed article (Applied Mathematical Finance, 2026 online).
- Verified via: WebSearch (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02518, https://papers.cool/arxiv/2511.02518, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1350486X.2026.2671725, summaries giving the three authors and the abstract); Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/8d3f01326bb95cdabd091f4224644a9a/).
- Question: how should an option market maker quote and hedge when its own hedging moves the underlying?
- Data and method: theory and numerics. Option order flow follows Cox processes whose intensities depend on the underlying and on the market maker's quotes; hedging has both permanent and transient impact on the underlying, so inventory and price evolve jointly. A mixed control problem (continuous quoting, impulsive hedging), solved numerically by policy optimization.
- Findings: (abstract) the paper studies "market manipulation and arbitrage phenomena that may arise from the feedback between option trading and underlying impact", proves well-posedness, and illustrates "the interplay between option market liquidity, inventory risk and underlying impact". No effect sizes for real markets.
- Limits: a single market maker; numerical illustration, not estimation.
- Use in the book: shows that once impact is transient as well as permanent, a dealer hedges in bursts (impulses) rather than continuously; hedge flow is then lumpy and partly discretionary, which weakens the one-to-one link between a GEX number and the flow it implies. Also another recent statement that impact creates manipulation incentives around option positions.

### Said2019
- Citation: Said, E. (2019). "How Option Hedging Shapes Market Impact." SSRN 3470915 and arXiv:1910.05056. Co-authors, if any, [unverified]: Consensus lists Emilio Said alone.

### AlmgrenLi2016

### GennotteLeland1990
- Citation: Gennotte, G., and Leland, H. (1990). "Market Liquidity, Hedging, and Crashes." American Economic Review 80(5): 999-1021.
- Type: peer-reviewed article (a 1989 working-paper version exists).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/349fc6cc664551e59e898e05972c64cc/); WebSearch (https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v80y1990i5p999-1021.html and https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:80:y:1990:i:5:p:999-1021, giving vol. 80(5), pp. 999-1021, December 1990).
- Question: "traditional models cannot explain how a relatively small amount of selling could cause so large a price drop" (abstract): how could hedging, without significant news, produce the October 1987 crash?
- Data and method: a rational-expectations model in which prices shape expectations; some traders are informed, others infer information from price, and hedging demand (portfolio insurance) is not fully observed.
- Findings: markets "are much less liquid in their model than in traditional models. Discontinuities (or 'crashes') can occur even with relatively little hedging." - Use in the book: the theoretical case for why the invisible book matters (Chapter 8A): the danger is not hedging per se but hedging the market does not see. The book's GEX framework assumes hedging is visible from open interest; Gennotte-Leland says the damage comes from the part that is not.

### BradyReport1988
- Citation: Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (1988). Report of the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (the "Brady Report"). Washington, DC: U.S. Government Printing Office, January 1988.
- Type: government (regulator-style) report; primary source.
- Verified via: WebSearch (the report's PDF at https://www.sechistorical.org/collection/papers/1980/1988_0101_BradyReport.pdf appeared in results; secondary accounts at https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2007/200713/200713pap.pdf, https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el88-18.pdf, https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1987). I could not open the report; every number below is as a search summary gave it, and page numbers are [unverified].
- Question: what happened in the markets on October 14-20, 1987, and what role did portfolio insurance, index arbitrage and market structure play?
- Data and method: trade-level reconstruction of NYSE and index-futures activity by participant type.
- Findings (as shown in search summaries): assets under portfolio insurance were about $60 billion to $90 billion, "only 2-3% of equity assets outstanding". On October 19, sell programs by three portfolio insurers made up just under $2 billion of NYSE sales of just under $21 billion; in futures, three portfolio insurers sold the equivalent of $2.8 billion of stock; total portfolio-insurance sales were about $6 billion ($4 billion futures, $2 billion stock). - Use in the book: the primary historical record of a mechanical hedge flow that trades with the move, sized in dollars against total volume. It is the book's best anchor for "a flow that is a small share of assets can be a large share of a day's selling", and for the portfolio-insurance version of negative gamma (a synthetic long put is a short-gamma hedge program).

### Grossman1988
- Citation: Grossman, S. J. (1988). "An Analysis of the Implications for Stock and Futures Price Volatility of Program Trading and Dynamic Hedging Strategies." Journal of Business 61(3): 275-298. NBER Working Paper 2357 (1987). Companion: Grossman, S. J. (1988). "Insurance Seen and Unseen: The Impact on Markets." Journal of Portfolio Management, DOI 10.3905/jpm.1988.409164.
- Type: peer-reviewed article (and a practitioner-journal companion).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/4fefe3689c0a5bb8a2e4ec1275309eba/ and, for the companion, https://consensus.app/papers/details/98354779b6d3575d84935b75ab6c185b/); WebSearch summary giving vol. 61(3), pp. 275-298, July 1988. The companion's full title after "Insurance seen and unseen" [unverified].
- Question: is a synthetic option (a dynamic replicating strategy) really equivalent to a traded option, as Black-Scholes says?
- Data and method: theory of the informational role of real securities.
- Findings: (abstract) "In the absence of a real traded put option ... there will be less information about the future price volatility associated with current dynamic hedging strategies. There will thus be less information transmitted to those people who could make capital available to liquidity providers. It will therefore be more difficult for the market to absorb the trades implied by the dynamic hedging strategies. - Limits: qualitative.
- Use in the book: the argument, from 1987, for why listed open interest has value: a traded option reveals the hedging demand behind it, and a replicated one does not. It is the positive case for GEX (listed positions are visible, so liquidity can prepare) and the reason FLEX, OTC and structured-product hedges are the dangerous part (Chapter 8A's invisible book).

### GrossmanZhou1996
- Citation: Grossman, S. J., and Zhou, Z. (1996). "Equilibrium Analysis of Portfolio Insurance." Journal of Finance 51(4): 1379-1403. DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb04073.x.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/2f29f484249754a1b1a84567f8fb424d/); WebSearch (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb04073.x, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7555; summary giving vol. 51, issue 4, pp. 1379-1403).
- Question: what does portfolio insurance do to prices in general equilibrium?
- Data and method: a martingale approach to general equilibrium with portfolio insurers and non-insurers.
- Findings: (abstract) "Insurers sell to noninsurers in bad states and general equilibrium requires that the risk premium rises to induce noninsurers to increase their holdings. The authors show that portfolio insurance increases price volatility, causes mean reversion in asset returns, raises the Sharpe ratio and volatility in bad states, and causes volatility to be correlated with volume. - Use in the book: an important nuance for the autocorrelation claim. Pro-cyclical hedging raises volatility, but in equilibrium the price overshoots and then partly reverts because the other side demands a higher premium: momentum within the move, mean reversion afterwards. "Short gamma means momentum" holds for the immediate response, not necessarily at longer horizons. It also ties the put skew to insurance demand (Chapters 5E, 5G).

### JacklinKleidonPfleiderer1992
- Citation: Jacklin, C. J., Kleidon, A. W., and Pfleiderer, P. (1992). "Underestimation of Portfolio Insurance and the Crash of October 1987." Review of Financial Studies 5(1): 35-63 (start page 35 confirmed; end page [unverified]). DOI 10.1093/rfs/5.1.35.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/5efa65e00ef757e79238eb0f6aeeadd9/); WebSearch (https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/5/1/35/1599650).
- Question: can a market that underestimates the amount of dynamic hedging produce a crash when it learns the truth?
- Data and method: a multiperiod Glosten-Milgrom model.
- Findings: (abstract) "if the market's prior beliefs underestimate the extent of dynamic hedging strategies such as portfolio insurance, then the price will be greater than that which would be implied by fundamentals ... - Use in the book: a second information-based mechanism: mechanical buying on the way up (the insurer's replicating purchases in a rally) is mistaken for good news; when its mechanical nature is revealed, the price falls. A modern analogue is call-driven dealer buying in a squeeze (Chapter 8D).

### KrishnanNelken2001
- Citation: Krishnan, H., and Nelken, I. (2001). "The effect of stock pinning upon option prices." Risk, December 2001: S17-S20 (equity options special report). Authors' first initials [unverified].

### AvellanedaLipkin2003
- Citation: Avellaneda, M., and Lipkin, M. D. (2003). "A market-induced mechanism for stock pinning." Quantitative Finance 3(6): 417-425. DOI 10.1088/1469-7688/3/6/301. SSRN 458020.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/6436d72b30605e68bb8aeb621491db18/); WebSearch (the journal PDF header "QUANTITATIVE FINANCE VOLUME 3 (2003) 417-425" at https://math.nyu.edu/inmemoriam/avellaneda//qf3601.pdf, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=458020, https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-market-induced-mechanism-for-stock-pinning-Avellaneda-Lipkin/28dfc8297bb73f9ab32aa172f57e7e1f18c8ba68). Issue 6 is from the DOI. The explicit SDE was not shown in any summary [unverified]; the structure below is as the summaries and the Jeannin-Iori-Samuel abstract describe it.
- Question: can delta hedging by market makers who hold a large position at one strike drive the stock to that strike at expiry?
- Data and method: theory. A linear "price elasticity" impact model (a trade of size Q moves the price in proportion, with a stock-specific constant E); floor market makers hold unusually large open interest at one strike and hedge it; an SDE for the stock price with a "singular drift" from the impact of their hedging; the pinning probability solved from the Fokker-Planck equation, analytically and numerically.
- Findings: (abstract) "if the open interest on a particular contract is unusually large, delta-hedging in aggregate by floor market-makers can impact the stock price and drive it to the strike price of the option ... the stock price has a finite probability of pinning at a strike. We calculate analytically and numerically this probability in terms of the volatility of the stock, the time-to-maturity, the open interest for the option under consideration and a 'price elasticity' constant that models price impact." The mechanism requires hedgers "who are net long options, hence long Gamma" (search summary of the authors' slides and later paper); the "strength" of the pinning force, beta, is "proportional to the open interest n and the price elasticity constant, E" (search summary). Pinning results from "a subtle interaction between the singular drift ... which drives the price to the strike, and independent diffusive shocks".
- Limits: one strike, one expiry, linear impact, continuous hedging by a single aggregate hedger whose position is known; no other flows.
- Use in the book: this is the published model the book's simulator (figures/node-pin-sim.html) implements. The simulator's hedge H = -N x 100 x delta, with each dollar traded moving the index lambda points per $1 billion, gives dS = (news - c)/(1 + w), w = lambda N 100 gamma S/1e9 and c = lambda N 100 charm dt S/1e9. That is the Avellaneda-Lipkin construction with their elasticity E written as lambda/1e9 per index unit (a relative move of lambda x units/1e9); c/(1 + w) is their charm-driven "singular drift" toward the strike, and 1/(1 + w) is the gamma term that damps volatility near the strike (the two effects Jeannin, Iori and Samuel name). I checked the book's library: its bsCharm is the calendar-time derivative of delta (dDelta/dt), so for N > 0 the drift points toward the strike on both sides, as the model requires [derived here]. Its conclusions agree with the literature: long gamma pins (Avellaneda-Lipkin; Jeannin-Iori-Samuel; pinning "much higher" when market makers are net long, Avellaneda-Kasyan-Lipkin 2012 citing Ni-Pearson-Poteshman); zero impact changes nothing (the pinning strength is proportional to n x E, so with E = 0 the hedging has no effect, in every Frey-type model); short gamma repels (the same algebra with N < 0 reverses the drift and turns the damping into amplification near the strike; the published models concentrate on the long case, and the empirical support is indirect: less clustering when market makers are net short, and anti-pinning in S&P 500 futures before SPX expiries, Golez-Jackwerth 2012). Two cautions for the text: pinning also arises without dealer impact (manipulation by holders of short options, Ni-Pearson-Poteshman 2005; a Brownian-bridge description, Krishnan-Nelken 2001), and the simulator's cap on 1 + w at 0.1 hides the Frey singularity that a large short position reaches near the strike.

- Citation: Ni, S. X., Pearson, N. D., and Poteshman, A. M. (2005). "Stock price clustering on option expiration dates." Journal of Financial Economics 78(1): 49-87. DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.08.005.
- Type: peer-reviewed article. (Already cited in Chapter 8A; included here for its two mechanisms.)
- Verified via: WebSearch (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v78y2005i1p49-87.html, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X05000577, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=519044; summary giving vol. 78(1), October 2005, pp. 49-87).
- Question: do option positions move underlying stock prices at expiry, and through what channel?
- Data and method: closing prices of optionable stocks on expiration dates, with position data by trader type (market makers, firm proprietary traders).
- Findings: closing prices cluster at strikes on expiration dates; returns of optionable stocks are altered by "an average of at least 16.5 basis points", with aggregate market-capitalization shifts "on the order of $9 billion" (search summary). Both "hedge re-balancing by option market-makers and stock price manipulation by firm proprietary traders contribute to the clustering". Per later summaries, clustering is much more frequent when market makers are net long options.
- Limits: individual stocks, pre-2005 data; the book's 8A cites the 16.5 bps figure for SPX-scale discussion, but the sample is single stocks.
- Use in the book: the evidence that pairs with Avellaneda-Lipkin: long-gamma hedging pins, and a second channel (traders short options pushing the price to the strike to minimize payouts) can also pin. Both are "attraction" mechanisms, which qualifies any claim that short-gamma strikes always repel.

### GolezJackwerth2012
- Citation: Golez, B., and Jackwerth, J. C. (2012). "Pinning in the S&P 500 futures." Journal of Financial Economics 106(3): 566-585. DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.06.010.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/79ed465239ea504e828a0b00e3462b1d/); WebSearch (https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:566-585, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X12001365, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1664261; summary giving vol. 106, pp. 566-585, December 2012).
- Question: is the S&P 500, the deepest market, pinned at expiries?
- Data and method: S&P 500 futures around expirations of serial options on the futures and of SPX index options.
- Findings: (abstract) futures "are pulled toward the at-the-money strike price on days when serial options on the S&P 500 futures expire (pinning) and are pushed away from the cost-of-carry adjusted at-the-money strike price right before the expiration of options on the S&P 500 index (anti-cross-pinning). These effects are driven by the interplay of market makers' rebalancing of delta hedges due to the time decay of those hedges as well as in response to reselling (and early exercise) of in-the-money options by individual investors. The associated shift in notional futures value is at least $115 million per expiration day."
- Limits: pre-0DTE sample; the dollar shift is small beside S&P futures volume.
- Use in the book: the index-level evidence that both attraction and repulsion occur in the S&P 500, and that the mechanism is charm (the time decay of hedges) plus customer flows, as in the simulator's c term. The $115 million a day is a useful scale check: expiry effects in the index are real but small in dollars.

### BaltussenTersteggeWhelan2023
- Citation: Baltussen, G., Terstegge, J., and Whelan, P. (2023-). "The Equity Derivative Payoff Bias" (SSRN 4562800, 2023 version); later circulated as "The Derivative Payoff Bias" (AFA meeting program, https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=98196). DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4562800. Journal publication not found.
- Type: working paper (SSRN; two versions with different titles and numbers).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/aa72c15cfc735e0088002a497b230b99/, 2023 abstract); WebSearch (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4562800, https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=98196, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374075491_The_Equity_Derivative_Payoff_Bias, and the practitioner summary https://www.quantitativo.com/p/the-derivative-payoff-bias), which gave the three authors and the later version's findings.
- Question: are S&P 500 prices at the monthly a.m. settlement (the special opening quotation, SOQ, on the third Friday) biased, and why?
- Data and method: SOQ against the preceding Thursday close, on expiry and non-expiry days; dealer option delta computed from position data; wealth transfers between option holders.
- Findings: 2023 version (Consensus abstract): unconditionally the SOQ is close to the prior close, but on settlement days "the SOQ exceeds Thursdays close by a significant margin"; the overnight return "raises (lowers) S&P 500 call (put) option payoffs, inducing a wealth transfer of around $4 billion per year in SPX options alone"; "most consistent with inventory management by option market makers and/or market manipulation by sophisticated investors", both relying on an illiquid period before settlement. Later version (search summaries): prices at the open on third Fridays "are systematically biased upward and fully reverse by midday"; call and futures payoffs are inflated and put payoffs deflated, transferring about $2.5 billion a year; "The aggregate delta of dealers' option positions drifts down into 3rd Friday derivative expiry, generating an incentive to buy equities, and dealer delta drift predicts overnight equity returns into 3rd Friday expirations - and on no other days"; SPX calls paid $10.46 billion on the average third-Friday morning against $10.23 billion at Thursday's close, $230 million a month; retail investors are net delta negative and lose at least $3.8 billion. Mechanism named by one of the authors: Julian Terstegge's Copenhagen Business School PhD thesis (2025), "Essays in Financial Intermediation and Climate Economics," DOI 10.22439/phd.14.2025 (Consensus: https://consensus.app/papers/details/78662a3e3b145712a35aee7273f06101/), summarizes this chapter as documenting "a novel channel through which option market makers' inventory hedging creates price pressure in the underlying equities: Charm. Charm spikes immediately before derivative payoffs are calculated and thus creates a derivative payoff bias, which exceeds $3bn a year." Its first chapter, "Intermediary Option Pricing," argues that intermediaries' option inventory and overnight equity illiquidity ("gap risk" when hedging is impeded) explain most of the option risk premium.
- Limits: working paper whose headline numbers changed between versions; quote the version. The a.m.-settled monthly contract is a particular design; PM-settled SPXW and 0DTE expiries work differently.
- Use in the book: the clearest modern evidence of a charm-type flow: dealers' delta drifting as expiry nears forces hedge trades on a known calendar, and the index moves in the illiquid window before settlement and then reverts. It supports the simulator's c term and a "calendar of hedge unwinds" figure, and shows the effect is attraction toward payoffs that favor the hedged side, not a general law of nodes. Context: Stoll and Whaley's studies of "triple witching" found expiration-day price effects of about 0.4% of index value, largely reversed the next morning, in 1984-85 ("Program Trading and Expiration-Day Effects," Financial Analysts Journal 1987, DOI 10.2469/faj.v43.n2.16, Consensus https://consensus.app/papers/details/6b88508c9e98564398982e8d84573380/), and smaller effects after the 1987 move to open settlement ("Expiration-Day Effects: What Has Changed?", FAJ 1991, DOI 10.2469/faj.v47.n1.58, Consensus https://consensus.app/papers/details/cabc6f74cb7f533f908a73aa1757f4de/); co-author initials of these two [unverified: Consensus lists "H. Stoll et al."].

## Part 4. Demand-based option pricing and who is net long or short

### GarleanuPedersenPoteshman2009
- Citation: Garleanu, N., Pedersen, L. H., and Poteshman, A. M. (2009). "Demand-Based Option Pricing." Review of Financial Studies 22(10): 4259-4299. https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/22/10/4259/1590158 (SSRN DOI 10.2139/ssrn.676501, as Consensus gives it). NBER Working Paper 11843 (December 2005).
- Type: peer-reviewed article. (Already cited in Chapter 8A.)
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/3a2733421bef5d8b8bf5842734221d8a/); WebSearch (https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i10p4259-4299.html, https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/22/10/4259/1590158, https://www.nber.org/papers/w11843; summary giving vol. 22(10), pp. 4259-4299, October 2009). Sample years [unverified].
- Question: when dealers cannot hedge options perfectly, how does end-user demand move option prices?
- Data and method: an equilibrium model with risk-averse dealers who cannot hedge perfectly; empirical tests with a dataset that identifies the aggregate positions of dealers and end users in index and single-stock options.
- Findings: (abstract) "demand pressure in one option contract increases its price by an amount proportional to the variance of the unhedgeable part of the option. Similarly, the demand pressure increases the price of any other option by an amount proportional to the covariance of their unhedgeable parts." Empirically, "End users are net long index options, especially out-of-money puts, which helps explain their apparent expensiveness and the smirk" (search summary); demand helps explain "the overall expensiveness and skew patterns of both index options and single-stock options".
- Limits: a pre-2008 sample, before weekly options, 0DTE and the growth of retail option selling; the model is about option prices, not the underlying.
- Use in the book: the theory of why dealers are paid to warehouse options: the unhedgeable part (jumps, discrete hedging) is what they charge for. For the sign question, it is the best-documented historical answer: in its sample, end users were net long index options, so dealers were net short index options, most of all in out-of-the-money puts. That supports the put side of the JPM-era convention and says nothing about the 0DTE era. It also implies that dealer gamma and dealer pricing are linked: when dealers are short, they charge more and hedge more. Chapter 10I's narrower reading (a pricing effect, not a level-trading rule) is right.

### BollenWhaley2004
- Citation: Bollen, N. P. B., and Whaley, R. E. (2004). "Does Net Buying Pressure Affect the Shape of Implied Volatility Functions?" Journal of Finance 59(2): 711-753. DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00647.x. (An erratum exists: https://www.afajof.org/wp-content/uploads/files/clarifications/jf-bollen-whaley-errata.pdf, content [unverified].)
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/6603af4238335ff19bd20655e59dcbcd/); WebSearch (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00647.x, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=319261; summary giving vol. 59(2), pp. 711-753).
- Question: does public order flow change the shape of the implied volatility function?
- Data and method: net buying pressure from public order flow in S&P 500 index options and individual stock options; changes in implied volatility; simulated delta-neutral option-writing strategies.
- Findings: (abstract) "changes in implied volatility are directly related to net buying pressure from public order flow ... changes in implied volatility of S&P 500 options are most strongly affected by buying pressure for index puts, while changes in implied volatility of stock options are dominated by call option demand. - Limits: 1990s data; the limits-of-arbitrage reading is disputed in some other markets (a KOSPI study finds a learning, not limits-of-arbitrage, pattern: Park and co-authors 2005, SSRN 686574, Consensus https://consensus.app/papers/details/237acac1d7e25728a6ee99ddff20db02/).
- Use in the book: evidence that customer demand (put buying in index options, call buying in single stocks) is the flow that sets dealer inventory and hence the sign of dealer gamma: for the index, public put buying leaves dealers short puts; for single stocks, public call buying leaves dealers short calls (the gamma-squeeze setting of Chapter 8D). It also links vanna: when put demand raises index implied volatility, the delta of the dealers' short puts changes, which is the vanna channel of Part 6.

### ChenJoslinNi2019
- Citation: Chen, H., Joslin, S., and Ni, S. X. (2019). "Demand for Crash Insurance, Intermediary Constraints, and Risk Premia in Financial Markets." Review of Financial Studies 32(1): 228-265. DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhy004. NBER Working Paper 25573.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/b46b60802bdc5bb8a6104adc4c90b564/ and earlier versions); WebSearch (https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/32/1/228/4994715, https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v32y2019i1p228-265..html; summary giving vol. 32, issue 1, January 2019, pp. 228-265).
- Question: can the quantity of deep out-of-the-money S&P 500 puts that intermediaries sell to the public measure how constrained intermediaries are?
- Data and method: public net buying of deep out-of-the-money S&P 500 puts (the other side being intermediaries); a model of the market for disaster insurance.
- Findings: (published abstract) "A tightening of intermediary constraint according to our measure is associated with increasing option expensiveness, higher risk premia for a wide range of financial assets, deterioration in funding liquidity, and broker-dealer deleveraging." Working-paper versions report that "a one standard deviation decrease in the net public demand of deep OTM puts is associated with a 3.6% rise in the subsequent 3-month market return" (2013 version) and 3.4% (SSRN 2375476 version), with an R-squared of 19% for 3-month returns in a 2013/2016 version (Consensus abstracts of those versions; quote them as working-paper numbers).
- Limits: the headline numbers above are from working-paper versions; the published abstract gives directions only.
- Use in the book: dealers are not passive: when their risk capacity shrinks, they sell fewer crash puts and charge more. In GEX terms, the dealer's short-put position (and its short gamma) is itself a choice that shrinks in stress, and a constrained dealer hedges more aggressively. It supports the book's "kappa is not a constant" and adds that the positioning is endogenous to dealer constraints.

### Muravyev2016
- Citation: Muravyev, D. (2016). "Order Flow and Expected Option Returns." Journal of Finance 71(2): 673-708. DOI 10.1111/jofi.12380. SSRN 1963865.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/8a6c5f6b932f5cce982809c35c1f5524/); WebSearch (https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v71y2016i2p673-708.html, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12380; April 2016, vol. 71(2), pp. 673-708 from the RePEc handle).
- Question: how much of the price impact of option trades comes from market makers' inventory risk rather than from information?
- Data and method: a decomposition of the price impact of option trades into inventory-risk and asymmetric-information components; the full panel of daily option returns.
- Findings: (abstract) "the inventory risk faced by market-makers has a first-order effect on option prices ... While both components are large for option trades, the inventory risk component is larger ... option order imbalances attributable to inventory risk have five times larger impact on option prices than previously thought ... past order imbalances have greater predictive power than any other commonly used predictor of option returns."
- Limits: effects on option prices, not on the underlying.
- Use in the book: dealers do not warehouse options for free; inventory risk sets option prices. This is the option-side counterpart of the hedging feedback: the same inventory that GEX tries to measure also moves option prices (and so implied volatility), which is the entry point for vanna flows.

### FournierJacobs2020
- Citation: Fournier, M., and Jacobs, K. (2020). "A Tractable Framework for Option Pricing with Dynamic Market Maker Inventory and Wealth." Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 55(4): 1117-1162. DOI 10.1017/s0022109019000462. SSRN 2334842.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/be4b963361c15ef0b192361136f3139f/); WebSearch (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/tractable-framework-for-option-pricing-with-dynamic-market-maker-inventory-and-wealth/88560981EB04DA96D0D17C620742C708; summary giving vol. 55, issue 4, June 2020, pp. 1117-1162).
- Question: how do an index-option market maker's inventory and wealth set the variance risk premium and option prices?
- Data and method: a dynamic model of an index option market maker with limited capital, estimated on returns, option prices and inventory.
- Findings: (abstract) "The market maker absorbs end users' positive demand and requires a more negative variance risk premium when she incurs losses"; the model "performs well, especially during the financial crisis", and nested reduced-form stochastic-volatility models are "strongly rejected".

- Use in the book: the formal link between the dealer's short-option inventory (end users' "positive demand"), the dealer's losses in a sell-off, and the rise in option prices, the variance risk premium mechanism of Chapter 10C. ### ChristoffersenGoyenkoJacobsKaroui2018
- Citation: Christoffersen, P., Goyenko, R., Jacobs, K., and Karoui, M. (2018). "Illiquidity Premia in the Equity Options Market." Review of Financial Studies. DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhx113. - Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/99c0dd20c8275b548b45ecb6082ee311/).
- Question: are illiquid equity options priced at a premium, and what does that say about who holds them?
- Data and method: option illiquidity measures built from intraday effective spreads for a large panel of U.S. equity options; risk-adjusted return spreads of illiquid over liquid options.

### ChenEtAl2025OptionImbalance
- Citation: Chen, H., and co-authors (2025). "Market Maker or Informed Trader: Who Drive the Relationship Between Option Trading and Underlying Returns? - Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/0d10503d53925c9f855be3706bce08fa/); WebSearch listing (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/fut.70038).
- Question: does option order flow move the underlying through market makers' hedging or through information?

## Part 5. Rebalancing flows that look like gamma

### ChengMadhavan2009
- Citation: Cheng, M., and Madhavan, A. (2009). "The Dynamics of Leveraged and Inverse Exchange-Traded Funds." Journal of Investment Management 7(4). Pages [unverified].
- Type: practitioner-academic journal article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/f937a6025b145beb9b4b5506ea5ffd73/); WebSearch (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228231757_The_Dynamics_of_Leveraged_and_Inverse_Exchange-Traded_Funds; summary giving Journal of Investment Management 7(4), 2009). The rebalancing formula below was shown in a search summary restating the model [notation as the summary gave it].
- Question: how do daily re-leveraging rules make leveraged and inverse ETFs trade, and what does that do to markets and to investors?
- Data and method: a unified framework for leveraged and inverse ETF dynamics; product-design and suitability analysis.
- Findings: (abstract) "The daily re-leveraging of these funds creates profound microstructure effects and exacerbates volatility towards the close"; the gross return of a leveraged or inverse ETF "has an embedded path-dependent option". The daily rebalancing trade is r x delta x (delta - 1) x W, for index return r, leverage multiple delta and fund assets W (search summary). Because delta(delta - 1) is positive for every multiple in use (2, 3, -1, -2, -3), the funds always buy after up days and sell after down days.
- Limits: a note, not an estimate of price impact.
- Use in the book: the cleanest "flow as a function of the move" outside options. A fund complex with assets W and multiple delta behaves like a dealer with negative dollar gamma of about -delta(delta - 1) x W x 0.01 per 1% move, concentrated at the close. For example, $10 billion in 3x funds trades 3 x 2 x $10 billion x 1% = $600 million per 1% move [derived here], always with the move.

### Tuzun2013
- Citation: Tuzun, T. (2013). "Are Leveraged and Inverse ETFs the New Portfolio Insurers?" Finance and Economics Discussion Series 2013-48, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System. SSRN 2190708.
- Type: working paper (Federal Reserve FEDS).

- Question: are leveraged and inverse ETFs a financial-stability risk, as portfolio insurance was?
- Data and method: rebalancing flows implied by fund assets and multiples; implied price impact; regressions of end-of-day volatility.

### HullWhite2017
- Citation: Hull, J. C., and White, A. (2017). "Optimal Delta Hedging for Options." Journal of Banking and Finance 82: 180-190 (September 2017). DOI 10.1016/j.jbankfin.2017.05.006. SSRN 2658343.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/7323868486ab5f8ca88824c9cf073c56/); WebSearch (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v82y2017icp180-190.html, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2658343; summary giving both authors, vol. 82, September 2017, pp. 180-190, and the gains below).
- Question: what hedge ratio minimizes the variance of a delta-hedged option position when volatility moves with price?
- Data and method: an empirical model of the minimum-variance delta, estimated on S&P 500 options and tested also on S&P 100, Dow Jones, individual-stock, commodity-ETF and interest-rate-ETF options.
- Findings: (abstract) "the normally calculated delta does not minimize the variance of changes in the value of a trader's position ... because there is a non-zero correlation between movements in the price of the underlying asset and movements in the asset's volatility. - Limits: a hedge-ratio result, not a flow estimate; how widely dealers use such deltas is not documented here.
- Use in the book: dealers who hedge with minimum-variance deltas already fold the expected vanna effect into their hedge (their delta is Black-Scholes delta plus vega times the expected change in implied volatility per unit of price). Two consequences: a GEX figure built on Black-Scholes delta and gamma misstates the hedge flow per move; and "vanna flow" is not a separate flow on top of gamma flow when the move and the volatility change are the typical ones; it becomes separate when volatility moves without the price (a volatility crush after an event, or a VIX spike without an index move).

## Part 7. Market impact: turning a hedge flow into a price move

### Kyle1985
- Citation: Kyle, A. S. (1985). "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading." Econometrica 53(6): 1315-1335 [issue and pages unverified]. DOI 10.2307/1913210.
- Type: peer-reviewed article.
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/416bc2f33f95505496f697c2fcf2e946/).
- Question: how do prices incorporate private information when an insider trades against noise traders and competitive market makers?
- Data and method: theory: a single risk-neutral insider, random noise traders and competitive risk-neutral market makers in sequential auctions, with a continuous-trading limit.
- Findings: (abstract) in the continuous-trading limit "prices follow Brownian motion, the depth of the market is constant over time, and all private information is incorporated into prices by the end of trading". The market makers' pricing rule is linear in net order flow; the slope ("Kyle's lambda") is the price move per unit of net order flow, and depth is its inverse (standard reading of the model).
- Limits: a model of informed trading; in it, impact is permanent because flow may be informed. Hedge flow is uninformed, and the empirical literature finds its impact mostly transient.
- Use in the book: the origin of "impact per unit of flow" as a single coefficient, the book's kappa and the simulator's lambda. The book should say that in Kyle's model lambda is set by how much information the flow might carry; for mechanical hedge flow that the market can recognize, the relevant impact is smaller and decays (Brøgger 2021; Chen and co-authors 2025).

### SquareRootImpact
- Citations: (a) Tóth, B., and co-authors (2011). "Anomalous price impact and the critical nature of liquidity in financial markets." Physical Review X 1: 021006. DOI 10.1103/physrevx.1.021006. (b) Bucci, F., and co-authors (2019). "Crossover from Linear to Square-Root Market Impact." Physical Review Letters 122: 108302. DOI 10.1103/physrevlett.122.108302. (c) Sato, Y., and co-author (2024/2025). "Strict Universality of the Square-Root Law in Price Impact across Stocks: A Complete Survey of the Tokyo Stock Exchange." Cambridge University Press; chapter "The Impact of Metaorders", DOI 10.1017/9781316659335.018. (e) Zarinelli, E., and co-authors (2015). "Beyond the Square Root: Evidence for Logarithmic Dependence of Market Impact on Size and Participation Rate." Market Microstructure and Liquidity, DOI 10.1142/s2382626615500045. (f) Vasaikar, A. (2026). "Empirical Confirmation of the Square-Root Law of Market Impact in a U.S. Large-Cap Equity." arXiv:2606.24019. Co-author lists of (a), (b), (c), (e) and the book's author list in (d) [partly unverified: Consensus shows first authors; (d)'s four authors are from my knowledge and not shown in the result, so treat them as unverified].
- Type: peer-reviewed articles (a, b, c, e); book (d); working paper (f, arXiv 2026).
- Verified via: Consensus (https://consensus.app/papers/details/d862a258856b5f8eb7bdfd3608d7cb5e/, https://consensus.app/papers/details/29bbb43c60f557168302ae0d631df53b/, https://consensus.app/papers/details/a2710387ea895097a72c1771c9bc485e/, https://consensus.app/papers/details/fd0cacb97762577ebe1d0878f964ba73/, https://consensus.app/papers/details/60273f1a0caf5073a1a23fae8fd036e9/, https://consensus.app/papers/details/bc1430f2b55a5fe1858180e0080a2028/).
- Question: how does the price impact of a large order depend on its size?
- Data and method: (a) theory of a latent order book that vanishes at the price, with empirical support; (b) 8 million U.S. institutional trades; (c) all trading accounts for all liquid Tokyo Stock Exchange stocks over eight years; (e) U.S. metaorders, 2007 to 2009; (f) AAPL order-by-order Nasdaq data, 178 trading days from December 2, 2024 to August 19, 2025.
- Findings: the average price impact I of a metaorder of size Q follows I proportional to Q^delta with delta close to 1/2; (c) finds "the exponent delta is equal to 1/2 within statistical errors at both the individual stock level and the individual trader level" across all liquid Tokyo stocks over eight years. (a) explains it by a latent order book that "vanishes around the current price", which also explains why metaorders must be split. (b) shows "a clear crossover between a linear market impact regime and a square-root regime as a function of the volume of the order" in 8 million U.S. institutional trades. (e) finds the square root fits "only across about two orders of magnitude in order size", a logarithm fitting better across five, and that impact relaxes on average to "approximately 2/3 of the peak impact". (f) calibrates I/sigma_D = c (Q/V_D)^(1/2) on AAPL with c_raw = 0.69 (bias-corrected 0.34). The square-root law "also holds for option markets" (Tóth and co-authors 2016, arXiv:1602.03043, Consensus https://consensus.app/papers/details/b139736f4f515f8686f413974ffa018c/).
- Limits: most evidence is for single stocks and for discretionary metaorders; I found no published square-root calibration for aggregate dealer hedge flow in S&P 500 futures.
- Use in the book: the standard form is I = Y x sigma_D x sqrt(Q/V_D), with Y of order one, sigma_D the daily volatility and V_D daily dollar volume. A worked illustration [derived here; the inputs are assumptions, not sourced facts]: with sigma_D = 1% (16% a year), V_D = $500 billion of S&P 500 exposure traded a day, and Y between 0.5 and 1, a $1 billion flow executed over a day moves the index 2.2 to 4.5 bps (1.3 to 2.7 points at 6,000); a $10 billion flow moves it 7 to 14 bps (4 to 8.5 points), not ten times as much. Consequences for the book: the simulator's lambda of 1 point per $1 billion sits at the low end for a day-long flow; a constant lambda overstates the effect of very large flows and understates the effect of small ones relative to the square root; and because impact partly decays, the cumulative effect of a day of hedging is smaller than the sum of its minute-by-minute impacts.

At an information volatility of 15%, adding at most 3.3 points means sigma_eff/sigma of at most about 1.22, a loop gain kappa G of at most about 0.18 on a daily horizon (1 - 15/18.3) [derived here]; on 30-minute windows, +6.4 points on the same base is a gain of about 0.30. Both are far from the "edge of explosion" and consistent with Anderegg and co-authors' under-one-point FX effects. It supports the book's sign rule and its "modest on typical days" message, and it reports that dealer 0DTE gamma is often negative, which cautions against treating the positive sign found on average (Dim, Eraker and Vilkov) as a constant.

Hu's result is the counterpoint the book should state: some option-driven hedge flow carries information (it is the other side of informed option buyers), and that part of its impact does not reverse, unlike the mechanical rebalancing that GEX describes.

## S2. Where the literature agrees

1. The sign rule. In every model with price impact, hedgers who are long gamma damp moves and hedgers who are short gamma amplify them [Frey1998, FreyStremme1997, PlatenSchweizer1998 (as a search summary of this literature states it), AndereggUlmannSornette2022, BuisEtAl2024]. The measured effects have the predicted sign in FX [AndereggUlmannSornette2022], in 0DTE SPX [AmayaGarciaAresPearsonVasquez2025], in single stocks [NiPearsonPoteshmanWhite2021, BarbonBuraschi2021, Yang2015], in bitcoin options [AtanasovaEtAl2025] and across 60 futures markets [BaltussenDaLammersMartens2021].
2. The formula. The book's sigma_eff = sigma/(1 - kappa G) is Frey's result, with kappa the liquidity (impact) parameter and G the hedger's position convexity times price [Frey1998, FreyStremme1997, SchonbucherWilmott2000]; its square is the diffusion coefficient of the nonlinear Black-Scholes equation. Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette fit a model of this family to real data.
3. No impact, no effect. Hedging changes prices only through impact: pinning strength is proportional to open interest times the elasticity [AvellanedaLipkin2003], and the empirical effect appears only as gamma interacted with illiquidity [BarbonBuraschi2021]. The simulator's "zero impact changes nothing" is right for this mechanism.
4. Typical-day effects are small. A reconstructed FX market-maker gamma of about -$1,000 billion adds 0.7 and 0.9 volatility points [AndereggUlmannSornette2022]; 0DTE market-maker gamma adds at most 3.3 points to daily and 6.4 to 30-minute realized volatility [AmayaGarciaAresPearsonVasquez2025]; expiry pinning shifts returns by at least 16.5 bps in single stocks [NiPearsonPoteshman2005] and at least $115 million of S&P 500 futures notional per expiration day [GolezJackwerth2012]; hedging demand changes daily autocorrelation by about 3.4 percentage points across quintiles [Yang2015].
5. Pinning needs long gamma, size, and time. Attraction to a strike requires hedgers net long options there, open interest large relative to liquidity, and expiry near [AvellanedaLipkin2003, JeanninIoriSamuel2008]; pinning is "much higher" when market makers are net long than when net short [AvellanedaKasyanLipkin2012, citing NiPearsonPoteshman2005].
6. Asymmetry. The destabilizing side can explode; the stabilizing side cannot [Frey1998's singularity; SornetteUlmannWehrli; Dai2025's stability condition; Zhao2026AndWoo2026's loop gain]. This matches Chapter 8B's convex curve.
7. Impact is concave, partly transient, and larger when flow is fast or unexpected [SquareRootImpact, KyleObizhaeva2016, Todorov2023AndBrogger2021, GennotteLeland1990, Grossman1988]. The 1987 and 2010 declines were about 2 and 8 times the invariance prediction when selling was fast [KyleObizhaeva2016].
8. Mechanical pressure reverses. Hedge-driven moves tend to reverse: after option-order imbalances [ChenEtAl2025OptionImbalance], after intraday momentum [BaltussenDaLammersMartens2021], after VIX-ETP hedging [BangsgaardKokholm2025], after the a.m. settlement push [BaltussenTersteggeWhelan2023], after leveraged-ETF rebalancing [Zhao2026AndWoo2026], after expiration exercise selling [Chiang2014].

## S3. Where the literature disagrees

1. Does insurance-type (short-gamma) demand raise volatility in equilibrium? Yes with limited or asymmetric information [GennotteLeland1990, JacklinKleidonPfleiderer1992, Grossman1988] and in GrossmanZhou1996; no in Basak1995's frictionless general equilibrium, where portfolio insurance lowers volatility. The sign rule is a result of models with impact and limited liquidity, not of all models.
2. Do mechanical flows matter economically? Tuzun2013 and ShumEtAl2016 find leveraged-ETF rebalancing adds volatility, largest on the most volatile days; IvanovLenkey2018 and the 2024 survey find investor flows offset most of it and the economic effect insignificant. The same dispute applies to dealer hedge flow: gross mechanical flow is not net price pressure.
3. Information or mechanics? Hu (2014) finds option-induced stock order imbalance predicts returns because it carries information [OptionTradeImpact]; NiPearsonPoteshmanWhite2021, ChenEtAl2025OptionImbalance and Dong (2025) find a non-informational, reversing channel. Both exist; they predict different persistence (information stays, mechanics reverses).
4. The shape of impact. Square root with a universal exponent [SquareRootImpact (c)], logarithmic over wide ranges [SquareRootImpact (e)], linear for small orders [SquareRootImpact (b)]; permanent in Kyle1985's information model, mostly transient and "mechanical" in the econophysics literature. The simulator's constant, permanent lambda is the simplest member, and the one that matches the published pinning model; it is not the empirically best impact model.
5. The sign of dealer positions. In the 1990s-2000s index-option samples dealers were net short (end users long, especially OTM puts) [GarleanuPedersenPoteshman2009, ConstantinidesLian2021]; in single-stock options market makers are net long [ChristoffersenGoyenkoJacobsKaroui2018]; in 0DTE SPX, market-maker gamma is "typically positive" but "often negative" [AmayaGarciaAresPearsonVasquez2025]. Positions also respond to dealer constraints and prices [ChenJoslinNi2019, JacobsMaiPederzoli2026], so any fixed convention is a simplification.
6. What autocorrelation the regimes produce depends on timing. Instantaneous feedback (the Frey family) changes volatility, not autocorrelation; lagged hedging produces autocorrelation (momentum under short gamma, reversal under long); equilibrium effects produce longer-horizon mean reversion after the overshoot [GrossmanZhou1996]. Chapter 8B's "mean reversion versus momentum" row is the lagged-hedging prediction; it holds intraday and is small in measured size.

## S4. What is untested, or only claimed

1. "Nodes attract and repel price" as a general rule. The modeled and tested version is narrow: a large long-gamma position at one strike, near expiry, attracts the close [AvellanedaLipkin2003, NiPearsonPoteshman2005, GolezJackwerth2012]. Away from expiry the theory predicts only a volatility (dwell-time) effect, and I found no test of strike-level attraction or repulsion in SPX outside expiration days, nor any test of "walls" as support and resistance.
2. Repulsion. Implied by the same equations with the sign reversed, but tested only indirectly (less clustering when market makers are net short; anti-cross-pinning in futures before SPX expiries).
3. The "absolute value rule" (vendor claim, see the practice file: "size determines influence, regardless of sign"). Every model here contradicts it: the sign decides whether a node attracts (long gamma) or repels (short gamma), and size matters only relative to liquidity (the product kappa G).
4. The gamma flip as a level. In the formula nothing happens at G = 0: damping turns smoothly into amplification. The special point is kappa G = 1, which depends on liquidity, not on the zero of G [derived here from Frey1998]. No test of the flip as a price level was found.
5. Loop gains near 1 in the S&P 500. No study estimates an SPX feedback gain near the singular point. Published calibrations imply gains of at most about 0.18 daily and 0.30 on 30-minute windows for 0DTE market-maker gamma [AmayaGarciaAresPearsonVasquez2025, derived], and about 0.27 in a 2026 Korean single-stock leveraged-ETF episode [Zhao2026AndWoo2026, derived]. Near-critical behaviour appears only as crash episodes where impact was 2 to 8 times its normal prediction [KyleObizhaeva2016].
6. Index-level vanna flows. Only specific channels are tested (VIX-ETP hedging moving SPX futures at the close [BangsgaardKokholm2025]; implied-volatility decline over expiration week [StiversSun2013]). The practitioner claim that falling implied volatility after events forces dealer buying of the index (a "vanna rally") has no direct test in what I found.
7. Charm flows outside monthly expiries. Tested for the monthly a.m. settlement [BaltussenTersteggeWhelan2023] and the expiration week [StiversSun2013]; not for weekly or daily (0DTE) expiries in what I found.
8. Pinning in 0DTE SPX. No model or test specific to same-day options surfaced in this search; the 0DTE papers study volatility, not pinning.
9. The single-stock gamma squeeze as a loop. Only a new preprint models its stability condition [Dai2025]; the GameStop literature I saw treats the episode as a short squeeze driven by social-media coordination, with no estimate of a hedging loop gain.
10. A published impact coefficient for dealer hedge flow in SPX, in points or basis points per $1 billion. None found; S7 below brackets it.

## S5. Verdict on "nodes attract and repel price"

What theory supports:
- Near expiry, a large position held long by hedgers at one strike pulls the price toward that strike (a drift from charm) and damps volatility around it; the same position held short pushes the price away and amplifies volatility around it [AvellanedaLipkin2003, JeanninIoriSamuel2008].
- The effect scales with position size times impact, and so with thin liquidity; it grows as expiry nears, because gamma near the strike grows like 1/sqrt(T) and charm faster still; it vanishes if impact is zero.
- Away from expiry, only the volatility effect remains: price tends to linger where hedgers are long gamma and to move fast where they are short (a dwell-time effect, derived here from Frey-type dynamics).

What theory does not support:
- Nodes as barriers that price bounces off, or that hold regardless of time to expiry.
- Size regardless of sign: the sign decides attraction versus repulsion.
- Effects at strikes where hedgers are not the holders, or where the vendor's sign convention is wrong: a heatmap that mislabels the sign predicts the opposite behaviour.
- A fixed effect per node: the same node matters more on a thin day (higher kappa) and less on a deep one.
- Pinning as the only expiry mechanism: manipulation by holders of short options [NiPearsonPoteshman2005], exercise-and-sell pressure [Chiang2014], order-book spoofing near strikes [FigueiredoEtAl2023] and charm into settlement [BaltussenTersteggeWhelan2023] all move prices at expiry, and only some are visible on a gamma map.

## S6. The book's formula and simulator against the literature

The effective-volatility formula. sigma_eff = sigma/(1 - kappa G) is derived in Frey (1998) and Frey and Stremme (1997), restated for the family of Schönbucher and Wilmott (2000), with a relative of it in Sircar and Papanicolaou (1998) and a fitted version in Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette (2022). The assumptions to state beside it: (1) impact is linear and permanent (each dollar of hedge moves the price by a fixed amount and it stays moved); (2) hedgers rebalance continuously to the delta of a known book; (3) everyone else's demand is unchanged by the hedging (the "reference traders" are price takers); (4) kappa G < 1, beyond which the model has no solution. Each assumption fails in a known way: impact is concave and partly transient [SquareRootImpact]; hedgers trade toward delta at a finite speed [AlmgrenLi2016] and in bursts [AubertChevalierLyVath2025]; other traders pre-position against predictable flow [Todorov2023AndBrogger2021, Zhao2026AndWoo2026]; and near kappa G = 1 the continuous model is replaced by jumps and crashes [GennotteLeland1990, KyleObizhaeva2016].

Two readings of the same multiplier. Chapter 8B's grind box sums hedging rounds within an instant (the Frey reading: volatility changes, autocorrelation does not); its regime figure spreads the feedback over steps (the AR(1) reading: autocorrelation w). Both give sigma/(1 - w) for the volatility of the cumulative move, but they predict different autocorrelation, and the measured autocorrelation effects are an order of magnitude smaller than the figure's w = 0.45 [Yang2015, BaltussenDaLammersMartens2021]. The text should call 0.45 illustrative.

Units for kappa and G (derived here). If G is dollar gamma in billions of dollars per 1% move (the negation of GEX) and lambda is the impact in index points per $1 billion traded, then

  kappa G = lambda x G / (0.01 S),

the points a 1% move's hedge flow moves the index, as a fraction of the 60 points that make a 1% move at S = 6,000. So kappa = lambda/(0.01 S). The book's illustrative kappa = 0.001 corresponds to lambda = 0.06 points per $1 billion, and its G values of +800 to +990 to $800 billion to $990 billion per 1%, far above any published GEX (the book's own aggregate range is $20 billion to $100 billion). With lambda = 1 point per $1 billion (the simulator's default), kappa G = 0.8 ("acute stress" in 8B) needs a net dealer short gamma of $48 billion per 1%. Recommend restating the 8B table in these units.

A consistency check the book needs (derived here). With a constant lambda of 1 point per $1 billion, aggregate GEX of $20 billion to $100 billion per 1% would imply loop gains of 0.33 to 1.67, explosive at the top, which nothing in the evidence supports on typical days. With square-root impact (assumed daily volume $500 billion, Y between 0.5 and 1), the same GEX gives loop gains of 0.10 to 0.45. Either the impact per dollar of hedge flow is much smaller and more concave than a constant lambda, or the hedged flow is much smaller than gross GEX (partial hedging, netting, other traders on the other side), or both. The published maxima for SPX 0DTE (a gain of about 0.18 daily) fit the concave reading.

The pinning simulator (figures/node-pin-sim.html). It implements the Avellaneda-Lipkin (2003) mechanism, in the Frey-Stremme form that Jeannin, Iori and Samuel (2008) analyse: dS = (news - c)/(1 + w), with w = lambda N 100 gamma S/1e9 the gamma (volatility) term and c = lambda N 100 charm dt S/1e9 the charm (drift) term; the library's charm is dDelta/dt, so the drift points toward the strike when dealers are long [AvellanedaLipkin2003 entry]. The identity w = lambda x (the strike's GEX in $ billion per 1%)/(0.01 S) holds exactly [derived here]. At its defaults (20,000 same-day contracts at 6,000, IV 15%, lambda 1 point per $1 billion), the strike's GEX is $11.7 billion per 1% at the open and w = 0.20; w reaches 0.50 with an hour left, 0.70 with 30 minutes left, and passes 1 in the last ten or so minutes (1.22 at ten minutes, 1.73 at five) [derived here with the figure's own formulas, calendar-time T]. So for long positions the damping becomes very strong near the close, and for short positions the model crosses the Frey singularity in the final minutes, where the figure's cap (1 + w floored at 0.1) takes over. Its three conclusions agree with the literature: long gamma pins [AvellanedaLipkin2003, JeanninIoriSamuel2008, AvellanedaKasyanLipkin2012, NiPearsonPoteshman2005]; short gamma repels (same equations, indirect evidence); zero impact changes nothing (for this mechanism). What it leaves out, which the text should say: concave and transient impact (pinning survives power-law impact [AvellanedaKasyanLipkin2012]); discrete hedging and noisy impact (pinning survives them [JeanninIoriSamuel2008]); intraday changes in dealer inventory [EgebjergKokholm2024]; other strikes and other flows; and the manipulation channel, by which holders of short options can also pull price to a strike [NiPearsonPoteshman2005].

## S7. How large is lambda? Impact per dollar in the S&P 500

No published estimate gives impact of dealer hedge flow in SPX in points per $1 billion. The literature brackets it:
- Permanent, aggregate: about $5 of market value per $1 of persistent flow [GabaixKoijen2021], roughly 1 bp or half an index point per $1 billion at an assumed $55 trillion market value [derived].
- Metaorder, one day, square root: 2 to 4.5 bps (1.3 to 2.7 points at 6,000) for $1 billion, 7 to 14 bps for $10 billion, under stated assumptions (daily volatility 1%, volume $500 billion, prefactor 0.5 to 1) [SquareRootImpact, derived].
- Invariance: a $4.1 billion E-mini sale in 2010 was predicted to move the market 0.61% [KyleObizhaeva2016] (2010 market size, not today's).
- Stress: actual moves of 2 times (1987) and 8 times (2010) the invariance prediction when selling was fast [KyleObizhaeva2016]; E-mini depth fell 80% from 2012 to 2022 [DealerDepthEminis], so a dollar of flow moves the index more now than a decade ago.
- Small: per option trade, 0.56 bps over five minutes in single stocks [OptionTradeImpact]; 0DTE market-maker hedging about 0.2% of daily SPX liquidity (Cboe, May 2025, cited in [AmayaGarciaAresPearsonVasquez2025] entry).
The simulator's slider (0 to 3 points per $1 billion, default 1) spans the normal-conditions bracket for day-long flows; stress episodes would need values several times higher.

## S9. Figure ideas

1. Loop-gain calculator. The reader sets GEX (in $ billion per 1%), impact (points per $1 billion) and spot, and switches between constant and square-root impact. The figure draws kappa G = lambda x GEX/(0.01 S) and the amplification 1/(1 - kappa G), with markers for the published calibrations (0DTE maximum gains of about 0.18 daily and 0.30 on 30 minutes; 0.27 in the Korean episode; the 2x and 8x stress multipliers of 1987 and 2010). It shows why aggregate GEX of $50 billion or more with a constant impact would be explosive, and why concave impact keeps the gain moderate.
2. The two forces of pinning. The node-pin simulator with two switches, one to turn off the charm drift c and one to turn off the gamma damping w, plus a "minutes left" readout of w. Histograms of closes for each case. It shows Jeannin, Iori and Samuel's two effects and when each dominates (damping all day, drift in the last hour).
3. Instant against lagged feedback. Two paths from the same shocks with the same kappa G: one with the feedback inside each step (Frey), one with the hedge one step late (AR(1)). Readouts: volatility of the cumulative move (the same) and lag-one autocorrelation (zero against plus or minus w). The reader sets kappa G and the hedging lag. It shows why the momentum and mean-reversion row of Chapter 8B depends on hedging speed, and how small the measured autocorrelation effects are.
4. Where price lingers. For a strike-by-strike book the reader edits (size and sign at each strike), draw local volatility sigma/(1 - kappa G(S)) and the dwell-time density proportional to 1/volatility squared, with a slider for time to expiry that adds the charm drift near the close. It shows attraction as lingering in long-gamma zones and repulsion as rushing through short-gamma zones, and it makes the "absolute value rule" visibly wrong: flip one node's sign and its effect reverses.
5. Who else trades like gamma. Stacked bars of the flow per 1% move from dealers (reader-set GEX), leveraged ETFs (delta(delta - 1) x assets x 1%, with Tuzun's $1.04 billion as a marker), volatility-targeting funds (driven by the change in realized volatility) and target-date or balanced funds (contrarian), with the net total. It shows that the market's total "gamma" is not dealer GEX alone and that some of it has a fixed sign.
6. Impact curves. Linear lambda against square-root and logarithmic impact for a flow of Q dollars, with the reader setting daily volatility, volume and the prefactor. Markers show where the simulator's default lambda agrees with the square root, and the 2010 prediction (0.61% for $4.1 billion) against the actual move (5.12%).
7. The expiry calendar. A month on a timeline with the monthly a.m. settlement (the upward bias and midday reversal), the expiration week and the week after, drawing published sizes only where they exist and labelling practitioner claims (such as "vanna rallies") as untested.
8. Crashes against predictions. Bars for 1987 (linear 0.51-0.63%, invariance 16.77%, actual 32%) and 2010 (invariance 0.61%, actual 5.12%). A slider for the speed of selling shows, as an illustration, how a faster sale raises the realized impact multiplier.
