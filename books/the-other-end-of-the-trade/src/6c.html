Part VI · Dealers · Chapter 6C

Delta hedging

Neutralizing direction

Where we are

Chapter 6B found the dealer short delta, short gamma and more after selling you a call. This chapter shows how the dealer neutralizes the delta and why the neutralization holds only at the current price. It then measures the noise that hedging at intervals leaves, and finds the resulting flow in the last 30 minutes of the trading day.

What you need first
  • Delta as a hedge ratio, counted in share-equivalents (Chapters 5C and 6B).
  • Gamma, the change in delta for a $1 move (Chapter 5C).
  • The P&L of a delta-hedged option over a short interval (Chapter 5C).
  • Variance, standard deviation and the fourth moment of the normal curve (Chapter 1B), and the Sharpe ratio (Chapter 1D).

1 · The first hedge is one multiplication

The dealer is short your one call, so its delta exposure is \(-\Delta\), where \(\Delta\) is the call's delta. Scale it up: a dealer that has sold \(n\) contracts, each on a multiplier of \(m = 100\) shares, is short \(n\,m\,\Delta\) share-equivalents, and its hedge is that many shares:

\[ \text{hedge shares} = n \times m \times \Delta \]
Worked example: Hedging 100 calls

Step 1: the exposure. A dealer that has sold 100 calls with a delta of 0.50 is short \(100 \times 100 \times 0.50 = 5{,}000\) share-equivalents, so it behaves as if it were short 5,000 shares of the underlying.

Step 2: the hedge. The fix is mechanical: it buys 5,000 shares, which leaves it short 100 calls and long 5,000 shares with a net delta of \(-5{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 0\). At this instant the book has no directional exposure, and the hedge has done its job.

This is delta hedging: holding the underlying in the amount that offsets a position's delta. The calculator below does the multiplication for a dealer that has sold SPY-like calls struck at $600, with 30 days to expiry and priced at 18% implied volatility. Its two sliders set the contracts the dealer sold (1 to 5,000, starting at 100) and the spot price ($500 to $700, starting at $600). The table walks the two steps: the delta per contract and the net exposure, then the shares the dealer buys and their dollar value. The curve shows the hedge the same position needs at every spot on the slider.

At the opening settings the table reads a delta of 0.5357, a net exposure of −5,357 shares, a purchase of 5,357 shares and a notional of $3,214,200. The delta is a little above the round 0.50 of the worked example, because an at-the-money call's delta sits above one half (Chapter 6B). Now move spot to $606, 1% higher: the delta rises to 0.6112 and the hedge to 6,112 shares. Move it to $590 and the hedge falls to 4,066 shares: the shares bought at one spot are the wrong hedge at the next.

2 · Why the hedge is local, not global

Here is the catch that every dealer has known since 1973, when the Black-Scholes model was published. The hedge is correct only at the current spot. The moment spot moves, the option's delta changes by its gamma, which leaves the existing share position the wrong hedge. The dealer must re-hedge, and after the next move it must re-hedge again. This catch drives everything from Chapter 6D through Part VIII.

Worked example: A 1% move, re-hedged

Step 1: the drift and the re-hedge. In the calculator, a rise from $600 to $606 takes the 100 calls' delta from 5,357 share-equivalents to 6,112. The dealer, still holding 5,357 shares, is now net short 755 share-equivalents, so it buys 755 more shares after the price has risen.

Step 2: the estimate from gamma. The call's gamma at $600 is 0.0128 per dollar, so a $6 move should change the book's delta by about \(0.0128 \times 6 \times 100 \times 100 = 768\) share-equivalents, close to the 755 the full formula gives.

In idealized continuous time this re-hedging never stops, and that continuous re-hedging is exactly what makes Black-Scholes pricing work mathematically (Chapter 7A). In practice dealers re-hedge at discrete intervals instead: when the residual delta exceeds some threshold, on a fixed time grid, or when market events demand it.

3 · The noise of hedging at intervals

The choice of when to re-hedge leaves a residual risk. Suppose the dealer re-hedges at intervals \(\Delta t\) apart, measured in years, where \(\Delta\) in front of a letter means "a change in", as in Chapter 5C. Between hedges the option's value moves with its full Greek profile while the share hedge sits still. The hedging error \(\varepsilon\) over one interval has variance approximately

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) \approx \tfrac12\,\Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2 \]

where \(\Gamma\) is the option's gamma, \(S\) the price of the underlying and \(\sigma\) its volatility. The expression encodes a key insight: the more frequently you re-hedge, the smaller the noise, but the more transaction costs you pay.

Where the variance comes from

Step 1: the error over one interval. By Chapter 5C, a delta-hedged option gains or loses about \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2 \left[(\Delta S/S)^2 - \sigma^2 \Delta t\right]\) over a short interval. The sign flips for the dealer, which is short, but the variance does not.

Step 2: the size of the move. Over a short interval, model the return as \(\Delta S/S = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\), where \(Z\) is a standard normal variable and \(\sigma\) is the volatility the option was priced at, so that \((\Delta S/S)^2 = \sigma^2 \Delta t\,Z^2\) and the error becomes

\[ \varepsilon = \tfrac12\,\Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 \Delta t\,(Z^2 - 1) \]

Step 3: no bias. Since \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = 1\), the error averages zero: hedging at intervals adds noise, not a systematic loss. If realized volatility differs from the priced one, the average becomes the realized-against-implied P&L of Chapter 5C, whatever the interval.

Step 4: the variance. \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2 - 1) = \mathbb{E}[Z^4] - 1\), and the fourth moment of a standard normal variable is 3 (Chapter 1B). So \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2 - 1) = 2\), and squaring the constant in front gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) &= \left(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\sigma^2\Delta t\right)^2 \times 2 \\ &= \tfrac12\,\Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Step 5: over a whole horizon. Over a horizon \(\tau\) there are \(\tau/\Delta t\) intervals. If their errors are independent, and gamma and spot are held fixed for simplicity, the variances add:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\text{total}) \approx \tfrac12\,\Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4\, \tau\, \Delta t \]

The standard deviation of the total error is therefore proportional to \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). Halving the interval cuts it by a factor of \(1/\sqrt{2} = 0.71\), and quartering the interval halves it.

Worked example: Daily against hourly hedging

Take the calculator's option: spot and strike $600, 30 days, 18% volatility, where the gamma is 0.0128.

Step 1: one day. With \(\Delta t = 1/252\) for one trading day, the constant in the error is \(\tfrac12\Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 \Delta t = \tfrac12 \times 0.0128 \times 360{,}000 \times 0.0324/252 = 0.296\). The error's standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2} \times 0.296 = 0.42\), about $0.42 a share, or $4,200 of noise from one day's re-hedge of the dealer's 100 contracts.

Step 2: hourly. A trading day of 6.5 hours hedged every hour has intervals 6.5 times shorter, so each interval's error is 6.5 times smaller, $0.0646 a share. Over 6.5 independent intervals the day's total is \(\sqrt{6.5} \times 0.0646 = 0.165\), about $1,650 for 100 contracts.

Step 3: the ratio. Hourly hedging cuts the day's noise to \(1/\sqrt{6.5} = 0.39\) of the daily figure, at the price of six or seven trades instead of one.

There is an optimal frequency, and it depends on the relative sizes of gamma, volatility and the cost of crossing the spread. For institutional dealers with very low transaction costs, it is high: every few seconds or even faster. For retail-scale operations with higher costs it is lower, a trade-off that Chapter 6E works with numbers.

Familiar ground

Delta hedging at intervals resembles dosing warfarin against the INR. The dose is right for today's reading, and the reading drifts between checks. Checking more often keeps the patient closer to the target, but each check costs a visit. A dealer's hedge is right for today's delta, delta drifts with spot between re-hedges, and each re-hedge costs a spread.

For this book's purposes, the key point is that dealers re-hedge continuously enough that the aggregate hedge flow is observable in the market. The continuous-time idealization is good enough to predict where flow appears. When spot moves through strikes with high open interest (OI), the dealer book's net delta shifts, and re-hedging activity appears in the underlying market.

4 · How often, and at what threshold

Different dealer business models re-hedge at different frequencies:

  • Equity wholesalers, such as Citadel Securities, re-hedge essentially continuously through aggregated order flow. Their hedging is interleaved with their market making and is largely invisible as discrete flow.
  • Options market makers on Cboe re-hedge when a threshold is breached. Common thresholds are a residual delta above 5% to 10% of the original position size, or a move of 0.25% to 0.50% in spot since the last hedge.
  • Structured product desks re-hedge less often, hourly or even less. They run larger residual deltas and accept the variance, because their books are larger and their cost per trade is higher.
Worked example: When a 5% threshold fires

Step 1: the threshold. The calculator's dealer holds 5,357 shares against its 100 calls. A threshold of 5% of the position fires when the residual delta passes \(0.05 \times 5{,}357 = 268\) share-equivalents.

Step 2: the move that crosses it. The book's delta changes by about \(0.0128 \times 100 \times 100 = 128\) share-equivalents per dollar of spot. It takes \(268/128 = 2.09\) dollars to reach the threshold, a move of 0.35% on $600.

Step 3: the two rules agree. The 0.35% lies inside the 0.25% to 0.50% range of the spot rule, so for this option the two kinds of threshold fire at about the same point.

Across the dealer ecosystem, hedge flow scales with realized volatility: on calm days hedging is small, and on volatile days it is large. This is the structural reason why volatility itself becomes self-reinforcing during stress regimes, and Chapter 8D returns to it with a formal feedback model.

5 · The last 30 minutes

The single most important empirical paper on dealer hedging at intraday horizons is Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens (2021), "Hedging Demand and Market Intraday Momentum", Journal of Financial Economics, Volume 142, Issue 1, October 2021, pages 377 to 403.

The authors used intraday returns on over 60 futures contracts around the world, on equities, bonds, commodities and currencies, from 1974 to 2020. They document a robust intraday momentum pattern: the return over the rest of the day, from the previous close to the last 30 minutes, positively predicts the return over those last 30 minutes. The predictive power is economically and statistically highly significant, and the move it predicts reverts over the next several days.

"Hedging short gamma exposure requires trading in the direction of price movements, thereby creating price momentum." Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens (2021), JFE

The authors' explanation of the mechanism is that dealers rebalance toward the close to limit their overnight residual exposure. When dealers are net short gamma in aggregate, their re-hedging trades go in the direction of price moves. They sell more as the price falls and buy more as it rises, as the 1% move above showed. This creates intraday momentum that concentrates in the final 30 minutes.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab adds that dealers were typically net short gamma in the past. In the paper, the sign of dealer gamma is an assumption, not an observation. Its measure for the S&P 500, net gamma exposure computed from index option open interest from 1996 to May 2020, assumes that market makers are long calls and short puts. By that measure dealer gamma was negative on about 2,930 of 6,088 days, slightly under half. The momentum appeared when the measure was negative, growing stronger the more negative it was. It was weak or absent when the measure was positive.

The effect sizes are economically meaningful. The Dealer Mechanics Lab puts the Sharpe ratios of simple intraday momentum strategies, built on the rest-of-day return's power to predict the close, in the 0.5 to 1.0 range. Summaries of the paper report higher figures: annualized Sharpe ratios of about 0.87 to 1.73 for its timing strategy at the level of an asset class, before costs. Either range is strong by intraday standards, though insufficient to support running the strategy after transaction costs.

The mechanism extends beyond options, because leveraged ETFs (LETFs) and inverse ETFs also generate short-gamma hedging needs as their leverage drifts from its target during the day. They rebalance toward the close in the same direction as the day's move.

Worked example: A leveraged fund's rebalance

Step 1: the fund. A fund that aims for two times the daily return of an index holds $200 of index exposure for each $100 of assets.

Step 2: a 5% move either way. After a 5% rise the exposure is $210 and the assets $110, so getting back to two times $110, or $220, means buying $10 of the index at the close. After a 5% fall the exposure is $190 and the assets $90, so getting back to two times $90, or $180, means selling $10.

Step 3: the general rule. For leverage \(k\), assets \(A\) and a day's return \(R\), the rebalance is \(k(k-1)R\,A\). With \(k = 2\) and \(R = 5\%\) it is \(2 \times 1 \times 0.05 \times 100 = \$10\). For an inverse fund, \(k = -1\), the factor \(k(k-1) = 2\) is positive too, so both kinds of fund trade with the day's move.

Other rule-based players trade the same way at the end of the day. The hedging of variance swaps, and the rebalancing of risk parity portfolios and of volatility-targeting funds, all go in the same direction as the recent move. A volatility-targeting fund is one that scales its exposure to hold its volatility near a target. All of these hedging activities contribute to the documented intraday momentum.

This is why the close is often the most mechanical part of the day, and why moves into 4 pm ET frequently extend rather than reverse. The last 30 minutes are different, and Baltussen and his coauthors document the difference quantitatively across decades and markets.

Three findings limit that picture. First, it describes whole markets, index and other futures. Among individual stocks, Baltussen, Da and Soebhag (2024) find the opposite in the last 30 minutes: the day's losers bounce against its winners. They find that neither liquidity nor gamma hedging explains the reversal. They point instead to attention-driven retail buying and to short sellers managing their risk at the end of the day.

Second, the rebalancing rule above overstates what leveraged funds actually trade. Ivanov and Lenkey (2018) find that investors' money moving into and out of leveraged and inverse equity ETFs, from 2006 to 2014, offset much of the rebalancing. Its effect on late-day returns was then economically insignificant.

Third, the effect shrinks where it is predictable. Barbon, Beckmeyer, Buraschi and Moerke (2022) find that leveraged-fund rebalancing moved stocks' closing prices more than option hedging did. It moved them less over time, though, as liquidity providers learned to anticipate it. Both effects reversed at the next day's open.

In practice
  • Hedging your own options follows the same trade-off at retail costs. If you delta-hedge a position yourself, hedge less often than a dealer would, because each re-hedge costs you a wider spread (Chapter 6E).
  • Intraday momentum is a description, not a strategy. Its Sharpe ratio, 0.5 to 1.0 on the Lab's figure and higher in summaries of the paper, comes before transaction costs. After costs it does not support running it.
  • Expect the close to be mechanical: if you must trade into 4 pm ET, do not assume a late-day move reverses, since such moves frequently extend.
When it fails

The hedge is exact only at the spot where it was set, so a large move between re-hedges leaves an error that the normal-curve variance understates. With fat tails (Chapter 1B), \(\mathbb{E}[Z^4]\) exceeds 3, and the error's variance grows with it. The intraday momentum mechanism needs dealers to be net short gamma. When they are net long gamma (Chapters 6D and 8D), their re-hedging leans against the move instead. The late-day move also reverts over the next several days. Among individual stocks the last 30 minutes tend to reverse the day's moves, for reasons hedging does not explain.

Common mistake: "a hedged book has no risk"

A delta-flat book is flat only at the instant of the hedge. Between re-hedges it carries gamma, and over any interval it carries hedging error whose size measures, so "hedged" means locally directionless, not riskless.

Decision rule
  • Re-hedge on a threshold, not on a feeling. The market makers' rules are a residual delta above 5% to 10% of the position, or a spot move of 0.25% to 0.50% since the last hedge.
  • Widen the threshold as your costs rise, since a retail account pays more per trade than a dealer and its best threshold is therefore wider (Chapter 6E).
  • Do not trade intraday momentum on its own. Treat it as context for how the close behaves, since after costs it does not support a strategy.
What to carry forward
  • A delta hedge is one multiplication: contracts times the multiplier of 100 times delta, in shares.
  • The hedge is right only at the current spot, because gamma changes delta as spot moves.
  • Hedging at intervals \(\Delta t\) adds noise with variance \(\tfrac12\Gamma^2 S^4 \sigma^4 (\Delta t)^2\) per interval, so the day's error shrinks as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) while costs climb.
  • Market makers re-hedge on thresholds, and hedge flow scales with realized volatility.
  • Short-gamma re-hedging, and the rebalancing of leveraged funds and similar players, concentrates same-direction flow into the last 30 minutes of the day. The evidence is for index and other futures: individual stocks reverse there instead.

6 · Check yourself

Question 1

A dealer has sold 250 calls with a delta of 0.40. What hedge does it hold, and what does it trade if their delta rises to 0.46?

Answer

The hedge is \(250 \times 100 \times 0.40 = 10{,}000\) shares, which must grow to 11,500 when delta reaches 0.46. The dealer buys the extra 1,500 after the price has risen.

Question 2

By what factor does hedging every 15 minutes, 26 times in a 6.5-hour day, cut the standard deviation of a dealer's daily hedging error, compared with hedging once a day?

Answer

The error's standard deviation scales with \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), so it falls to \(1/\sqrt{26} = 0.20\) of the daily figure, a fifth, while the dealer trades 26 times instead of once.

Question 3

What does a three-times leveraged fund with $100 of assets trade at the close on a day the index falls 4%?

Answer

By \(k(k-1)R\,A\), the rebalance is \(3 \times 2 \times (-0.04) \times 100 = -\$24\), a sale of $24. Its exposure fell from $300 to $288 while its assets fell to $88, and three times $88 is $264.

Question 4

Why does short-gamma re-hedging push the close in the direction of the day's move?

Answer

A short-gamma dealer's delta moves against it: after a rise it is short more share-equivalents, so it buys, and after a fall it sells. When dealers rebalance toward the close to limit overnight exposure, those trades arrive together and go with the move.
