Part XII · Strategy atlas · Chapter 12D

Advanced strategies

Ratios, diagonals and synthetic positions

Where we are

Chapters 12B and 12C kept every loss covered or capped. The advanced tier gives that up somewhere: naked options, short straddles and strangles, ratios, ladders and lizards all leave at least one side where the loss can far exceed the premium collected. Each entry says where that side is, and what it takes to hold it.

What you need first
  • Short options, their breakevens and their unbounded or near-unbounded losses (Chapter 5A).
  • Margin, margin expansion and forced liquidation (Chapters 1E and 2E), and the margin on each structure (Chapter 12G).
  • The slope rule (Chapter 12A), and credit spreads, condors and butterflies (Chapter 12C).

1 · How to read this tier

The cards and the notation are those of Chapter 12C, with strikes numbered from lowest to highest, \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3 < K_4\). A structure collects a net credit \(C\) or pays a net debit \(D\), the price at expiry is \(S_T\), and dollars are points times $100. For positions that hold shares, \(B\) is the cost basis per share. The premiums in the examples are illustrative, below what a pricing model gives, and the formulas hold at any premium.

Every entry here but the long condors has an unbounded or very large loss on at least one side. Find that side in the card before you read anything else.

2 · Naked structures

Short put, naked

Naked short putAt a glance
LegsSell 1 put at \(K\) for a premium \(P\), held on margin rather than cash
P&L at expiry\(P - \max(K - S_T, 0)\)
Maximum profit\(P\)
Maximum loss\(K - P\) per share, if the underlying goes to zero
Breakeven\(K - P\)
Greeks at entryDelta +, gamma −, theta +, vega −

The naked short put has the payoff of the cash-secured put of Chapter 12B, but it uses margin, so its buying-power requirement is lower. Under Reg-T the requirement is approximately 20% of the strike value less the amount the put is out of the money. Chapter 12G gives the full formula, which uses the underlying's value and adds the premium. The requirement is subject to margin expansion in stress (Chapter 2E).

Worked example: What margin does to a short put

Step 1: the cash-secured version. Selling one 5,900 SPX put with SPX at 6,000 and securing it in cash holds \(5{,}900 \times \$100\), which is $590,000.

Step 2: the naked version. By the approximate rule, 20% of the strike value is 1,180 points. Less the 100 points the put is out of the money, that leaves 1,080 points, about $108,000.

Step 3: what changes. Filled to the same capital, the account now holds about 5 times as many puts. The loss per put is unchanged, so the account's loss in a crash is about 5 times larger too.

Short call, naked

Naked short callAt a glance
LegsSell 1 call at \(K\) for a premium \(P\), with nothing to cover it
P&L at expiry\(P - \max(S_T - K, 0)\)
Maximum profit\(P\)
Maximum lossUnbounded
Breakeven\(K + P\)
Greeks at entryDelta −, gamma −, theta +, vega −

The naked short call is pure short volatility with no upside protection. It requires significant margin and explicit broker approval. With its unbounded maximum loss, it is rarely appropriate for a retail account.

The worked example is to avoid it, and to use the defined-risk call credit spread, the bear call spread of Chapter 12C, instead.

3 · Neutral structures

Short straddle

Short straddleAt a glance
LegsSell 1 call and 1 put at the same strike \(K\); collect a credit \(C\)
ViewNo move: the underlying stays near \(K\)
Maximum profit\(C\), at \(S_T = K\)
Maximum lossUnbounded above; \(K - C\) below, as \(S_T \to 0\)
Breakevens\(K - C\) and \(K + C\)
Greeks at entryDelta about 0, gamma very −, theta very +, vega very −

The short straddle takes the maximum credit for the maximum risk: pure short volatility with an unbounded loss, catastrophically dangerous in stress. Sold at the 80 points that Chapter 12C's long straddle cost, a short 6,000 SPX straddle breaks even at 5,920 and 6,080. A 10% fall to 5,400 would cost \(600 - 80 = 520\) points, or $52,000.

Short strangle

Short strangleAt a glance
LegsSell 1 out-of-the-money put at \(K_1\) and 1 out-of-the-money call at \(K_2\); collect a credit \(C\)
ViewThe underlying stays between the strikes
Maximum profit\(C\), for \(K_1 \le S_T \le K_2\)
Maximum lossUnbounded above; \(K_1 - C\) below, as \(S_T \to 0\)
Breakevens\(K_1 - C\) and \(K_2 + C\)
Greeks at entryDelta about 0, gamma −, theta +, vega −

The short strangle is a wider, higher-probability version of the short straddle, and a common vehicle for harvesting the variance risk premium (VRP). Used systematically with cash-secured collateral, as XSP strangles, it can be operationally viable.

Worked example: A short strangle on SPX

Step 1: the trade. With SPX at 6,000 and 45 DTE, sell the 5,900 put for $30 and the 6,100 call for $30, a total credit of 60 points, or $6,000.

Step 2: the odds. The strangle wins if SPX settles between 5,840 and 6,160. Its probability of profit is about 70%, but if either tail is hit, the loss-to-credit ratio can be 5 to 10 times.

Step 3: a tail. At 5,400, a 10% fall, the put pays 500 points, and the loss is \(500 - 60 = 440\) points, or $44,000, about 7 times the credit.

Long call condor and long put condor

Long call condorAt a glance
LegsBuy 1 call at \(K_1\), sell 1 call at \(K_2\), sell 1 call at \(K_3\), buy 1 call at \(K_4\), with equal gaps \(W\) between the lower two and the upper two; pay a debit \(D\)
ViewThe underlying stays between the inner strikes
Maximum profit\(W - D\), for \(K_2 \le S_T \le K_3\)
Maximum loss\(D\), below \(K_1\) or above \(K_4\)
Breakevens\(K_1 + D\) and \(K_4 - D\)
Greeks at entryAs an iron condor's: delta about 0, gamma −, theta +, vega −

The long call condor has the same payoff shape as the iron condor, built from calls only; the long put condor builds it from puts. Traders use one when the puts are illiquid, or when skew makes one type cheaper. By put-call parity, a long call condor bought for \(D\) is the iron condor with the same strikes sold for \(W - D\), apart from interest (Chapter 5F). The CNDR evidence and the Edge Book's rejection of systematic condors therefore apply to it unchanged (Chapters 12C and 10I).

4 · Ratio spreads

Call ratio backspread

Call ratio backspreadAt a glance
LegsSell 1 call at \(K_1\), in or at the money (ITM or ATM); buy 2 (or 3) calls at \(K_2\), out of the money (OTM). Often opened at zero cost or a small credit \(C\)
ViewBullish, with little at stake below \(K_1\): it profits big on a strong rally
Maximum profitUnbounded above
Maximum loss\((K_2 - K_1) - C\), at \(S_T = K_2\)
Breakevens (1 by 2)\(K_1 + C\), if \(C > 0\), and \(2K_2 - K_1 - C\)
Greeks at entryDelta near 0 or slightly +, gamma +, theta −, vega +, since it holds more options than it sells

The ratio backspread's P&L is flat at \(C\) below \(K_1\). It falls with slope −1 to its low at \(K_2\), then rises with slope +1, or +2 with three calls bought, without limit.

Worked example: A call ratio backspread on SPX

Step 1: the trade. With SPX at 6,000, sell one 6,000 call for $40 and buy two 6,100 calls for $20 each, $40 in all, for a net cost of zero.

Step 2: a rally. If SPX explodes to 6,400, the short call loses \(400 \times \$100\), or $40,000, and the two long calls gain \(2 \times 300 \times \$100\), or $60,000, for a profit of $20,000.

Step 3: the trough. At 6,100 the short call loses 100 points and the long calls pay nothing, so the maximum loss is $10,000. The position breaks even at \(2 \times 6{,}100 - 6{,}000 = 6{,}200\), and below 6,000 it neither gains nor loses.

The put ratio backspread is the mirror image and the bearish backspread: sell 1 put at \(K_2\) and buy 2 puts at \(K_1\) below it. It profits big on a crash, which makes it an effective tail-hedge structure. Its maximum loss is \((K_2 - K_1) - C\), at \(S_T = K_1\). It breaks even below at \(2K_1 - K_2 + C\), and its profit reaches \(2K_1 - K_2 + C\) if the underlying goes to zero.

Broken-wing butterflies

A broken-wing butterfly, often opened for a credit, is a butterfly with one wing wider than the other, which eliminates one side's tail risk. In the call version, you buy \(K_1\), sell two \(K_2\) and buy \(K_3\), with the upper gap wider: \(K_3 - K_2 > K_2 - K_1\). Opened for a credit \(C\), it keeps \(C\) below \(K_1\) and makes its maximum, \((K_2 - K_1) + C\), at \(K_2\). Above \(K_3\) it loses \((K_3 - K_2) - (K_2 - K_1) - C\), and it breaks even at \(2K_2 - K_1 + C\).

Suppose you buy the 5,950 call, sell two 6,000 calls and buy the 6,100 call for a hypothetical credit of 5. Below 5,950 you keep 5 points, and at 6,000 you make \(50 + 5 = 55\). Above 6,100 you lose \(100 - 50 - 5 = 45\) points, and you break even at 6,055. The put version widens the lower wing instead, which removes the upside risk.

5 · Income variants

Covered short straddle

Covered short straddleAt a glance
LegsOwn 100 shares at cost basis \(B\); sell 1 call and 1 put at an at-the-money strike \(K\); collect a credit \(C\)
ViewHeavy income on a stock you want to own
Maximum profit\((K - B) + C\) per share, above \(K\)
Maximum loss\(B + K - C\) per share, if the stock goes to zero
Breakeven\((B + K - C)/2\)
Greeks at entryDelta about +1 per share, gamma very −, theta +, vega −

Below the strike, the shares and the short put both lose, so the P&L falls with slope 2: highly negative gamma and double assignment exposure. Through put-call parity, shares plus a short call are a short put (Chapter 5F). The whole position is therefore effectively two short puts.

Suppose a stock is at $100 and you sell the 100 straddle for a hypothetical $8. The breakeven is \((100 + 100 - 8)/2 = \$96\). At $80, the shares lose $20 a share, the put costs $20 and the credit returns $8, a loss of $32 a share against $20 for the shares alone.

Covered short strangle

A covered short strangle holds 100 shares and sells an out-of-the-money put at \(K_1\) and an out-of-the-money call at \(K_2\), for a credit \(C\). It gives modest income with less assignment pressure than the covered short straddle. The out-of-the-money strikes give the underlying room to move before either short leg goes in the money. Its maximum profit is \((K_2 - B) + C\) per share, and its maximum loss \(B + K_1 - C\), if the stock goes to zero. The breakeven is \(B - C\) if that lies above \(K_1\), and otherwise \((B + K_1 - C)/2\).

6 · Other directional structures

Ladders

A ladder is a three-strike asymmetric structure: premium income with a capped profit zone and uncapped risk on one side. The bull call ladder buys a call at \(K_1\) and sells calls at \(K_2\) and \(K_3\), which leaves an unbounded loss above \(K_3\). For a net debit \(D\), or a credit if \(D\) is negative, it makes at most \((K_2 - K_1) - D\), between \(K_2\) and \(K_3\). It breaks even above at \(K_3 + (K_2 - K_1) - D\).

The bear put ladder is the mirror image: buy a put at \(K_3\) and sell puts at \(K_2\) and \(K_1\). It makes at most \((K_3 - K_2) - D\), between \(K_1\) and \(K_2\), and its loss grows below its lower breakeven, \(K_1 + K_2 - K_3 + D\). Reversing every leg of a ladder turns it into its opposite, a position that pays for a large move on the open side.

For example, a bull call ladder on 6,000, 6,100 and 6,200 bought for a hypothetical 20 points makes at most 80. It breaks even above at \(6{,}200 + 100 - 20 = 6{,}280\), and loses a point for every point beyond that.

Jade lizard

Jade lizardAt a glance
LegsSell 1 out-of-the-money put at \(K_1\); sell 1 out-of-the-money call at \(K_2\) and buy 1 call at \(K_3\) further out; collect a credit \(C\)
Defining condition\(C \ge K_3 - K_2\), the call spread's width
Maximum profit\(C\), for \(K_1 \le S_T \le K_2\)
UpsideAt least \(C - (K_3 - K_2)\), which is never below zero: no upside risk
Maximum loss\(K_1 - C\) per share or point, if the underlying goes to zero
Breakeven\(K_1 - C\)
Greeks at entryDelta +, gamma −, theta +, vega −

Provided the total credit is at least the call spread's width, a jade lizard has zero upside risk, leaving all of its risk on the downside. That condition is the defining feature of the structure. With a credit below the width, you hold a short put and a short call spread with risk on both sides, not a jade lizard.

Worked example: A jade lizard on SPX

Step 1: the trade. With SPX at 6,000, sell the 5,950 put for $42, sell the 6,080 call for $18 and buy the 6,110 call for $9. The net credit is \(42 + 18 - 9 = 51\) points, or $5,100 per contract.

Step 2: verify the condition. The call spread is \(6{,}110 - 6{,}080 = 30\) points wide. The credit of 51 is at least 30, so there is no upside risk. At any SPX print at or above 6,110 the call spread costs its full 30 points. The 51 collected exceeds that, so you keep at least \(51 - 30 = 21\) points, or $2,100.

Step 3: the downside. All the risk is on the put side, and SPX options settle in cash, so below 5,950 the put costs \((5{,}950 - S_T) \times \$100\) at settlement. The position breaks even at \(5{,}950 - 51\), which is 5,899.

Many published jade lizard examples, and some trading-platform builders, quote a call spread wider than the credit and still call the structure a jade lizard, which it is not. Verify that the credit covers the width before you rely on the no-upside-risk property.

The reverse jade lizard is the mirror image: a short out-of-the-money call and a short out-of-the-money put credit spread. It has no downside risk if the credit is at least the put spread's width. Its loss above the call strike is unbounded, from a breakeven at the call strike plus the credit.

Call and put ratio spreads, debit version

A ratio spread buys one in-the-money option and sells two out-of-the-money ones. It is a modest directional bet with premium income, but its risk is uncapped if the underlying moves through the short strikes. The call version buys \(K_1\) and sells two \(K_2\), for a net debit \(D\). It makes at most \((K_2 - K_1) - D\), at \(K_2\), and breaks even at \(K_1 + D\) and at \(2K_2 - K_1 - D\), above which the loss has no limit. The put version buys \(K_2\) and sells two \(K_1\). It makes at most \((K_2 - K_1) - D\), at \(K_1\), and loses below \(2K_1 - K_2 + D\).

In practice

Naked options need explicit broker approval and a margin account (Chapter 2E), and their requirements grow in stress. For each position, write down the loss at a 10% and a 20% move on its open side before you trade, and size so that both fit your account. Cash-settled index options remove assignment and delivered shares, but not the loss (Chapter 12H). Among these structures, the short strangle has the most followers; Chapter 10C weighs the variance risk premium it collects.

When it fails

Every structure here fails on its open side: above the short calls of a naked call, straddle, strangle, ladder or ratio spread, and below the short puts of a naked put or jade lizard. The failure arrives in the same sessions as margin expansion, so the loss and a rising requirement come together (Chapter 1E). A 70% probability of profit does not protect a position whose tail costs 5 to 10 times its credit.

Decision rule
  • Do not sell naked calls in a retail account: use a bear call spread instead.
  • Hold a naked put, straddle or strangle only with a written stress loss that fits the account, and a kill rule for margin expansion (Chapter 13C).
  • For a jade lizard, check that the credit is at least the call spread's width, or treat the position as having risk on both sides.
  • Size a ratio spread or ladder from its open side, where the loss has no cap, not from its maximum profit.
Common mistake: a jade lizard that is not one

A trader sells a put and a call spread 50 points wide for a credit of 40, and calls it a jade lizard. Above the long call, the call spread costs 50 and the credit covers only 40, so the position loses 10 points there. There is upside risk after all, because the defining condition fails.

Familiar ground

Sizing a naked short option is like setting a dose by its dose-limiting toxicity. A phase 1 trial does not choose the dose at which the typical patient does well. It chooses the highest dose whose rate of severe toxicity stays acceptable. A short strangle wins about 70% of the time. Its size has to be set by its tail loss of 5 to 10 times the credit, not by the typical month.

What to carry forward
  • Most advanced structures leave one side open: above short calls, below short puts, beyond the short strikes of ratios and ladders.
  • A naked short put has the cash-secured put's payoff on about a fifth of the capital, which multiplies the crash loss per dollar of capital.
  • Short straddles and strangles collect the most premium in the atlas and carry unbounded upside and large downside losses.
  • Backspreads hold more options than they sell, so they are long volatility and profit from a large move. Ratio spreads and ladders sell more than they hold, and lose without limit on their open side.
  • A jade lizard has no upside risk only if its credit is at least its call spread's width.

7 · Check yourself

Question 1

You sell the 5,950 put and the 6,050 call on SPX for 70 points in all. Where do you break even, and what does a move to 6,400 cost you?

Answer

The breakevens are \(5{,}950 - 70 = 5{,}880\) and \(6{,}050 + 70 = 6{,}120\). At 6,400 the call pays 350 points, a loss of \(350 - 70 = 280\) points, or $28,000.

Question 2

A call ratio backspread sells one 5,900 call and buys two 6,000 calls for a net credit of 10. What are its maximum loss and its breakevens?

Answer

The maximum loss is \((6{,}000 - 5{,}900) - 10 = 90\) points, at 6,000. The breakevens are \(5{,}900 + 10 = 5{,}910\) and \(2 \times 6{,}000 - 5{,}900 - 10 = 6{,}090\).

Question 3

A jade lizard sells a put for 30, sells a call for 12 and buys a call 25 points higher for 5. Does it have upside risk?

Answer

The credit is \(30 + 12 - 5 = 37\), which is at least the 25-point width, so there is no upside risk. Above the long call it keeps at least \(37 - 25 = 12\) points.

Question 4

Why is a covered short straddle effectively two short puts?

Answer

By put-call parity, shares plus a short call have the payoff of a short put at the same strike (Chapter 5F). Adding the straddle's own short put makes two, which is why the position loses twice as fast as the shares alone below the strike.

Question 5

A long put condor and an iron condor share the strikes 5,750, 5,800, 6,200 and 6,250, and the iron condor collects 15. What should the long put condor cost?

Answer

About \(50 - 15 = 35\) points, apart from interest. By parity the two have the same P&L, so the condor's debit is the wing width less the iron condor's credit.
