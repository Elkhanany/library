Part II · Markets · Chapter 2E

Accounts, margin and taxes

Account types and the Section 1256 advantage

Where we are

After Chapter 2D priced execution, this chapter prices the account that holds the trade: which kind of account you open, what margin rules come with it, and how its gains are taxed. The product choice of Chapter 2A turns out to be, above all, a tax decision, and the account choice a decision about how much stress you can survive.

What you need first
  • Compounding, which turns a yearly tax gap into a gap in wealth (Chapter 1A).
  • Margin, margin calls and forced liquidation, and why the path matters (Chapter 1E).
  • The six S&P 500 products, and XSP against SPY (Chapter 2A).

Two strategies with identical pre-tax profit and loss (P&L) can produce wildly different after-tax outcomes. The difference depends on the wrapper, on the account type and on whether the underlying instrument qualifies for Section 1256 treatment under American tax law. This is one of the genuinely free lunches available to American retail traders. Most retail strategies fail to capture it, because they default to the most familiar instrument (SPY) instead of the tax-optimal one (XSP, ES or SPX).

In plain terms

Your account decides two things: how much you can borrow against your positions, and how the broker's demands for collateral change when markets turn. Your instrument decides a third: how the gains are taxed. Index options and futures get a tax rate well below the one on short-term stock and ETF trades, whatever the holding period. A portfolio-margin account lends the most in calm markets and can reclaim it quickest in a crash.

1 · The four account tiers

American brokerage accounts come in four primary capacity tiers. Each has different rules about what can be traded and how much leverage is available. Capacity escalates upward: you cannot trade naked short calls in a cash account, but you can do everything a cash account permits in a portfolio-margin account. Chapter 1E teaches how margin, margin calls and forced liquidation work in general. This section gives the rules of each tier, and Chapter 12G the margin charged on each options structure.

Cash account

A cash account trades with settled funds only, with no borrowing and no naked short option. Cash-secured puts, covered calls, long options and long stock are all permitted in this default retail tier.

A trade settles when the cash and the securities actually change hands. As of May 2024, when the SEC moved the standard from T+2 to T+1, equities settle T+1, one business day after the trade, and options settle T+1 as well.

The cash account is structurally safe, because you cannot lose more than you have. There is no margin extension to draw down, so there is no margin call and no forced liquidation. The price is capital inefficiency: cash-secured strategies tie up capital dollar for dollar against potential assignment. A cash-secured XSP put struck at 600 holds \(600 \times \$100 = \$60{,}000\) of cash for as long as it is open.

Reg-T margin account

A Reg-T margin account follows fixed-percentage margin rules under Federal Reserve Regulation T. The initial margin on a stock purchase is 50%: you can borrow half the price. The maintenance margin is 25%: your equity must stay above a quarter of the position's value, or you get a margin call. Options buying power is generally limited.

Those two percentages fix how far a position bought on margin can fall before the call. Suppose you buy stock worth \(V_0\) with initial margin \(i\), so you borrow \(L = (1 - i)\,V_0\). When the stock is worth \(V\), your equity is \(V - L\), and the maintenance rule requires \(V - L \ge m\,V\). The call comes when the value falls to

\[ \frac{V}{V_0} = \frac{1 - i}{1 - m} \]

With \(i = 50\%\) and \(m = 25\%\), that is \(0.50/0.75 = 0.667\): a fall of one third. Buy $20,000 of stock with $10,000 of your own money, and the call arrives when the holding is worth $13,333, where your $3,333 of equity is exactly 25% of it.

Pattern day trader rules cover active accounts under $25,000. A day trade opens and closes the same position on the same day. An account with less than $25,000 of equity that makes four or more of them in a rolling window of five trading days is flagged and restricted. Most retail accounts with $2,000 or more of equity qualify for Reg-T margin. The permission to sell naked short options requires specific approval and demonstrated experience.

Portfolio margin account

A portfolio margin (PM) account sets margin from stress-tested theoretical losses. It requires $125,000 or more of equity, the minimum set by FINRA, the regulator of American brokers, and the broker's approval. Chapter 12G adds that brokers typically ask for $125,000 and sometimes $150,000 to $200,000.

The requirement is computed against a scenario of SPX moving 15% either way, combined with shifts in implied volatility. For diversified, well-hedged books, the requirement is dramatically lower than under Reg-T. For an iron condor on SPX with both wings 5% out of the money, Reg-T might require collateral equal to the full wing width. Portfolio margin might require only the loss that would occur in the ±15% scenario, perhaps a third of the Reg-T number.

The catch is that PM requirements expand non-linearly in stress, sometimes doubling or tripling overnight as the broker's house overlays kick in. This is a critical feature, not a bug, and the section on margin in stress returns to it.

Worked example: A scenario grid for a short strangle

Step 1: the position. With XSP at 600, you sell a 570 put and a 630 call, a short strangle, for a combined 12 points of premium, or $1,200. For simplicity, value each option at what it would pay at expiry.

Step 2: the grid. Portfolio margin asks what the position loses at each move in its scenario range and charges the worst. At −15%, XSP is at 510, the put pays \(570 - 510 = 60\) points, and you lose \(60 - 12 = 48\) points, or $4,800. The +15% case loses the same on the call, so the requirement is $4,800.

Step 3: a wider grid. If the broker widens the scenario to ±20%, the worst case is XSP at 480, a loss of \(90 - 12 = 78\) points, or $7,800. At ±25% it is \(120 - 12 = 108\) points, or $10,800. Widening the range by 5 and 10 percentage points raised the requirement by 63% and 125%, with no change in the position and before any shock to implied volatility.

Futures account

A futures account, or 1256 account, is separately permissioned for futures (ES, MES, ZN, CL and others) and for options on futures. ZN is a Treasury-note future and CL a crude-oil future. Futures use SPAN margin, developed by the CME and computed against a matrix of stress scenarios. It is typically 5% to 8% of notional for the major equity futures. Both gains and losses receive 1256 60/40 tax treatment, regardless of holding period.

ES at SPX 6,000 has a notional of $300,000, and its initial margin is around $15,000 to $22,000 depending on conditions, with intraday margin sometimes lower. Chapter 12G quotes roughly $13,000 to $15,000 of initial SPAN margin for one contract. At $15,000 the position is \(300{,}000/15{,}000 = 20\) times the margin, and at $22,000 about 14 times. This is genuinely powerful leverage with clean tax treatment. At 20 times, a 5% fall in the index takes the whole $15,000.

2 · Section 1256: the 60/40 rule

Under Internal Revenue Code Section 1256, certain financial contracts receive a uniquely favorable tax treatment that applies regardless of holding period. A Section 1256 contract has its gains split:

1256 split: 60% long-term + 40% short-term

A long-term capital gain comes from a position held more than a year, and a short-term capital gain from one held a year or less. Short-term gains are taxed at the ordinary income rate, long-term gains at a lower rate. For a 1256 contract the split ignores the holding period, so even a same-day trade is 60% long-term. The blended rate is therefore

\[ t_{1256} = 0.6\,t_{\text{LT}} + 0.4\,t_{\text{ST}} \]

where \(t_{\text{LT}}\) and \(t_{\text{ST}}\) are your long-term and short-term rates. Subtracting it from the short-term rate gives the saving on each dollar of gain:

\[ t_{\text{ST}} - t_{1256} = 0.6\,(t_{\text{ST}} - t_{\text{LT}}) \]

For an American individual taxpayer in the top federal bracket, the ordinary rate is 37%. The long-term rate is 23.8%, which includes the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT). The blended rate on 1256 gains is often quoted as approximately 27% to 28%, against approximately 40% to 41% on ordinary short-term gains for instruments outside the 1256 regime. That would be roughly 12 to 14 percentage points of after-tax savings on every dollar of P&L generated by 1256 instruments.

Worked example: The top-bracket arithmetic, like for like

Step 1: without the surtax. By , \(0.6 \times 20\% + 0.4 \times 37\% = 12\% + 14.8\% = 26.8\%\), against 37% for short-term gains.

Step 2: with the surtax on both. Adding the 3.8% NIIT to both rates gives \(0.6 \times 23.8\% + 0.4 \times 40.8\% = 30.6\%\), against 40.8%.

Step 3: the gap. Either way, the saving is 10.2 percentage points, as gives directly: \(0.6 \times (37\% - 20\%) = 10.2\%\).

Short-term rate1256 blended rateSaving
Without the surtax37%26.8%10.2 points
With the surtax on both40.8%30.6%10.2 points

The quoted figures set a blend without the surtax against a short-term rate with it. Compared like for like, the saving at the top bracket is about 10 points.

The saving on returns follows from the rates: an after-tax return is the pre-tax return times one minus the tax rate, and \(W_0\) compounds over \(n\) years to

\[ W_n = W_0\,\big(1 + R\,(1 - t)\big)^n \]

For a systematic monthly put-write strategy earning 8% a year gross, the quoted 12 to 14 points make approximately 100 basis points of after-tax return a year, turned on or off by instrument choice. The like-for-like 10.2 points make about 80: at the top bracket with the surtax, 8% becomes \(8\% \times (1 - 0.408) = 4.74\%\) on SPY and \(8\% \times (1 - 0.306) = 5.55\%\) on XSP.

Over a decade of compounding the gap is substantial. As a rough rule, 100 basis points a year for 10 years is roughly 10% more terminal wealth, before the compounding of the saved taxes themselves, which adds another few percent. Computed with at the like-for-like rates, $1 grows to $1.59 on SPY and $1.72 on XSP in 10 years. The XSP account ends 8% larger, a gap of 13% of the starting capital.

What qualifies

1256-qualified instruments include:

  • Cash-settled broad-based index options: SPX, XSP, NDX, RUT, VIX and NDXP.
  • Regulated futures contracts: ES, MES, ZN, ZB, CL, ZC and GC, and all CME, CBOT, NYMEX and COMEX equity, rate and commodity futures.
  • Options on regulated futures contracts: ES options, MES options and so on.
  • Broad-based index options on Cboe, whose operative legal definition is in IRC §1256(g)(3).
  • Non-equity options that meet the "broad-based" test.

1256 does not cover:

  • ETF options, such as SPY, QQQ and IWM. These are equity options, not index options, even though they track indices.
  • Single-name stock options.
  • Narrow-based index options, such as some sector ETFs and individual industry indices.
  • Stocks themselves, to which the standard short-term and long-term capital gains rules apply.

The broad-based index test for index options requires, loosely, that the underlying index include 10 or more component securities and that no single component exceed 30% of the index weight. SPX clearly qualifies, while sector ETF options such as XLE or XLF do not.

3 · SPY against XSP: the one decision that matters

This is the single highest-leverage operational choice in retail systematic options strategies, and it costs nothing to make. A retail trader running monthly cash-secured at-the-money puts on SPY or on XSP sees identical pre-tax P&L, apart from a few basis points of spread. After tax, in the top bracket, XSP keeps roughly 10 more cents of every dollar of pre-tax profit, over a multi-year horizon. Its only operational price is its slightly wider bid-ask spread, which costs perhaps 5 to 10 basis points a year and is easily outweighed by the tax saving.

Worked example: A year of puts, taxed two ways

Step 1: the strategy. Suppose you sell ten SPY at-the-money monthly puts at a strike where each contract collects $300 of premium, for $3,000 of gross premium a month. The contracts' notional equals that of ten XSP contracts. You hold the strategy for a year, collecting twelve months of premium, and win 9 months while losing 3.

Step 2: the pre-tax result. Gross premium collected is about $36,000 and losses in the losing months about $15,000, for a net pre-tax P&L of about $21,000.

Step 3: SPY in a Reg-T account. All gains and losses are short-term, since nothing is held a year. At the quoted effective rate of about 40% to 41%, the after-tax P&L is about $12,400.

Step 4: XSP under 1256. The 60/40 blend applies regardless of holding period. At the quoted effective rate of about 27% to 28%, the after-tax P&L is about $15,200, which is approximately $2,800 more after tax on the same pre-tax P&L.

Step 5: like for like. At 40.8% and 30.6%, the two results are $12,432 and $14,574, a gap of $2,142. At 37% and 26.8%, without the surtax, they are $13,230 and $15,372, the same gap. It is 10.2% of the $21,000, the 10.2 points of the top-bracket arithmetic.

Over a decade of compounding this gap is a small house. The only operational change required is opening the XSP order ticket instead of the SPY one.

4 · Wash sales

The IRS wash-sale rule disallows a loss on a security if a "substantially identical" security is bought within 30 days before or after the sale that realized the loss. For stocks and most ETFs this rule is easy to trigger accidentally. Selling SPY for a loss and buying it back two weeks later disallows the loss, deferring it into the cost basis, the tax purchase price, of the new position.

Worked example: A wash sale, and a 1256 roll

Step 1: the SPY loss. You buy 100 SPY at $600, sell them at $570 for a $3,000 loss, and buy 100 back two weeks later at $575. The loss is disallowed this year. It is added to the new shares' basis, which becomes \(57{,}500 + 3{,}000 = \$60{,}500\), so it reduces the gain, or adds to the loss, when you finally sell.

Step 2: the XSP loss. You close a losing XSP credit spread for a $3,000 loss and re-enter a similar spread the same day. The loss counts this year, in full, against your other gains.

For 1256 instruments, wash-sale rules do not apply. Losses can be realized and immediately re-established without disallowance. This matters substantially for any strategy with frequent roll activity, or one that legitimately needs to harvest losses against gains for tax planning. A trader running monthly XSP credit spreads who has a losing month can close the position, realize the loss for tax purposes and immediately re-enter a similar position. The loss is fully usable against any other gains in the same year.

The wash-sale exemption for 1256 contracts is buried in the Internal Revenue Code and rarely discussed in retail education. It interacts with the mark-to-market provision of §1256, which requires you to mark all open 1256 positions to market at year-end and treat any gain or loss as recognized. The result is a clean tax accounting in which every gain and every loss flows through to the tax computation in the year it occurred, with no carryforward complexity from disallowed losses.

Worked example: The year-end mark

Step 1: the mark. In November you open an XSP position for $2,000, and on December 31 it is worth $2,600. The $600 gain is taxed this year, 60% as long-term and 40% as short-term, although you have not closed the position.

Step 2: the close. The position's basis is now $2,600. You close it in January for $2,400, a $200 loss that belongs to the new year. Across the two years the gains and losses add up to the $400 the trade actually made.

6 · Margin in stress: the real cost of portfolio margin

Reg-T margin requirements are fixed and formulaic (50% on stock, fixed formulas on options), so they do not change in stress. Your margin requirement is the same on Monday as it was on Friday, regardless of overnight news.

Portfolio margin requirements, by contrast, are stress-tested. The broker computes what your portfolio would lose if SPX moved 15% either way along with implied-volatility shocks, and requires you to post collateral against that theoretical loss. In calm conditions, PM allows far more leverage than Reg-T: diversified, hedged portfolios can run at 5 to 10 times the leverage that Reg-T would permit. In stress, PM requirements expand violently.

The canonical worked example is March 2020, the first three weeks of the COVID drawdown:

DateSPXVIX
February 21, 2020333817
March 16, 20202386 (intraday low about 2280)82.69

That is a fall of \(2386/3338 - 1 = -28.5\%\) at the close, and nearly 32% to the intraday low, while the VIX rose almost fivefold. In the same period, four things happened at once:

  • Many PM accounts saw their margin requirements double or triple in 1 to 3 days, as brokers increased their house-overlay add-ons.
  • Brokers adjusted the ±15% scenario itself upward, some to ±20% or ±25%, as the realized-volatility regime shifted.
  • The implied-volatility shock components of the stress test grew larger as the VIX rose.
  • Volatility-targeting funds began de-grossing, cutting their positions, which fed back into the PM margin calculations as the VIX rose further.

The combination produced a wave of forced liquidations across PM accounts. Traders who had been running at "comfortable" pre-stress leverage, say 4 times, found themselves facing requirements of 8 or 10 times after the margin expansion, with no time to raise additional collateral. The broker's algorithm sells positions to bring the account back into compliance with the new margin level. That selling adds to the SPX downside, which raises the VIX further, which further expands margin requirements at other PM accounts. The feedback loop has exactly the same structure as the dealer-hedging loop of Chapter 8B, operating through margin accounting instead of options Greeks.

The strangle's scenario grid above shows the first link in that chain. Widening the scenario from ±15% to ±25% more than doubled its requirement, from $4,800 to $10,800, with no change in the position and before any volatility shock or house add-on.

The structural lesson is that cash-secured strategies cannot be force-liquidated for margin reasons, because there is no margin extension to expand. This is the operational reason cash-secured XSP put-writes are the recommended retail vehicle for harvesting the variance risk premium (VRP), even though they are capital-inefficient next to PM-leveraged versions of the VRP harvest. The capital inefficiency is the insurance premium against the path-dependent risk of margin expansion in stress (Chapter 1E). Portfolio margin is genuinely useful in calm conditions. It is genuinely dangerous in stress, and the danger comes at precisely the moments when you most want your account to survive intact.

7 · The decision tree: five products, three accounts

Cash account: XSP cash-secured puts, capital-bound but operationally the safest. Avoid SPY for any systematic strategy: the tax penalty is large and unnecessary.

Reg-T account: SPY or XSP diagonal or vertical spreads if you need defined-risk leverage, and ES futures through the 1256 route if you want clean leveraged equity exposure. The Reg-T tier is a good middle ground for traders who have outgrown cash accounts but are not ready for the margin-expansion risk of portfolio margin.

Portfolio margin account: sized XSP strangles or condors against a known stress budget, with explicit, pre-committed kill criteria for margin-expansion scenarios. Do not touch PM until you have at least 12 months of cash-account or Reg-T experience with the strategy and documented adherence to your written kill rules. The first PM account every trader opens is operated incorrectly. The second is operated more carefully, because the first one taught lessons that no amount of reading could have.

The two traditions this book draws on disagree about the condors in that line, and both positions stand. One lists XSP strangles and condors among the portfolio-margin strategies and teaches the iron condor as an intermediate structure (Chapter 12C). The other rejects systematic iron condors in a retail account, on the evidence that Chapter 1D summarizes and Chapter 10I sets out in full. Read those chapters before running one.

The choice between cash, Reg-T and PM is one of the most consequential a retail trader makes, and most retail traders make it badly. They jump to PM too early and fail to write down stress-scenario kill rules. They also do not understand that PM margin requirements are path-dependent in a way that Reg-T's are not.

Familiar ground

The twelve-month rule works like surgical credentialing. A surgeon adopting a new procedure does a set number of proctored cases before operating alone, because the learning curve is steepest in the first cases and the patients bear its cost. Portfolio margin is a new procedure with the account as the patient: earn it on the same strategy in a safer tier, with a written record, before running it unsupervised.

In practice

In a taxable account, put index exposure in 1256 instruments: XSP or SPX options, or ES and MES futures. Keep SPY for share-level execution, and remember that the tax advantage counts only in a taxable account. Size cash-secured positions from the strike's notional, since the cash is locked for as long as the position is open. If you move to margin, compute your call point with for Reg-T. For portfolio margin, compute the requirement at ±20% and ±25% as well as ±15%, and hold enough cash to meet the widest of them.

When it fails

The tax advantage is only as large as your rates. It shrinks as the gap between your short-term and long-term rates shrinks, and it counts only on gains in a taxable account. The cash-secured account's safety has a limit too: it removes the margin call, not the loss, and a cash-secured put still loses whatever the index falls below its strike, less the premium.

Decision rule
  • In a taxable account, choose the 1256 product (XSP, SPX, ES or MES) over SPY for any systematic strategy on the index.
  • Start in a cash account with cash-secured XSP puts.
  • Move to Reg-T for defined-risk spreads or futures once the strategy has a record.
  • Move to portfolio margin only after 12 months with the strategy in a lower tier, with written kill rules for margin expansion, and sized so that the ±25% requirement still fits.
Common mistake: running a systematic strategy in SPY

SPY's options are the most familiar and the most liquid, so they are where most retail put-writing starts. In a taxable account, that choice gives away about 10 points of tax on every dollar of gain. In a top bracket that is some $2,000 on every $21,000 of pre-tax profit, in exchange for a spread a few cents narrower.

Common mistake: sizing portfolio margin from the calm requirement

A portfolio-margin requirement measured in calm markets is about as small as it gets. Sizing to it, at "comfortable" leverage, is what left traders facing requirements of 8 or 10 times in March 2020. Size to the requirement in the stress you are guarding against.

What to carry forward
  • There are four account tiers. A cash account borrows nothing, so it gets no margin calls. Reg-T sets fixed rules: 50% initial and 25% maintenance margin. Portfolio margin is stress-tested, cheapest in calm markets and expanding in stress, and futures accounts use SPAN.
  • Section 1256 contracts are taxed 60% long-term and 40% short-term whatever the holding period. At the top bracket that saves about 10 points of tax per dollar of gain, like for like. The often-quoted 12 to 14 mixes rates.
  • On the same pre-tax P&L, XSP keeps about 10 more cents per dollar than SPY, and the gap compounds.
  • 1256 contracts escape the wash-sale rule and are marked to market at year-end.
  • Portfolio margin can double or triple in days, as it did in March 2020. Cash-secured strategies cannot be force-liquidated for margin.

8 · Check yourself

Question 1

Your ordinary rate is 32% and your long-term rate 20%, with no surtax. What is your blended 1256 rate, and how much does it save on each dollar of gain?

Answer

By , \(0.6 \times 20\% + 0.4 \times 32\% = 12\% + 12.8\% = 24.8\%\). The saving is \(32\% - 24.8\% = 7.2\) points, which is \(0.6 \times (32\% - 20\%)\), as says.

Question 2

You buy $30,000 of stock with $15,000 of your own money in a Reg-T account. At what value does the maintenance call come?

Answer

You borrowed $15,000, so by the call comes at \(0.50/0.75 = 2/3\) of the starting value, $20,000, where your $5,000 of equity is 25% of the holding.

Question 3

A strategy makes $10,000 before tax for a top-bracket taxpayer. What does it keep in SPY and in XSP, like for like with the surtax?

Answer

In SPY it keeps \(10{,}000 \times (1 - 0.408) = \$5{,}920\), and in XSP \(10{,}000 \times (1 - 0.306) = \$6{,}940\). The difference is $1,020, 10.2% of the pre-tax profit.

Question 4

You sell SPY at a $3,000 loss in November and buy it back ten days later. What happens to the loss? What if the trade had been an XSP spread?

Answer

In SPY it is a wash sale: the loss is disallowed and added to the basis of the new shares. In XSP, a 1256 contract, the wash-sale rule does not apply, and the loss counts in full this year.

Question 5

Your short XSP strangle is margined against ±15% and needs $4,800. What happens to the requirement if the broker widens the scenario to ±20%?

Answer

The worst case moves to XSP at 480, where the 570 put pays 90 points. Less the 12 points of premium, the loss is 78 points, or $7,800, a 63% rise with no change in the position.

Question 6

One ES contract at SPX 6,000 carries $15,000 of initial margin. What leverage is that, and what index fall wipes out the margin?

Answer

The notional is \(\$50 \times 6{,}000 = \$300{,}000\), 20 times the margin, so a fall of \(15{,}000/300{,}000 = 5\%\) loses the whole $15,000.
