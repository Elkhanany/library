1 · The claim this part tests

The Dealer Mechanics Lab states the central claim of its swing-trading material narrowly, so that it can be tested and found false:

SPY exhibits conditional short-horizon mean reversion embedded within a longer-run positive equity drift, and that edge weakens or flips in trend-dominant, negative-gamma, or macro-shock regimes. This is not "SPY mean reverts." It is "SPY mean reverts when specific measurable conditions hold." The Dealer Mechanics Lab

The when is the entire framework. Without it, what remains is a contrarian habit that works for a year and blows up in the next volatility regime. With it, you have a system in which you do nothing on most days. On a handful of days you take a bet that compounds. The bets you skip are at least as valuable as the bets you take.

2 · Four questions before any trade

The thesis of Chapter 11B says that different markets have different statistical properties. So before you apply any tactical setup, whether mean reversion, a breakout, a pullback or premium selling, you need a current read of the regime. Every swing decision begins with four questions, answered from the dashboard:

  1. What is the trend? Is the market in a Stage 2 uptrend, a Stage 4 downtrend, a Stage 1 base or a Stage 3 distribution? Chapter 11E defines the four stages.
  2. What is the volatility regime? Is volatility compressed, normal, expanding or stressed?
  3. What is the dealer positioning? Is gamma exposure (GEX) positive, negative or near the gamma flip?
  4. What is the flow signature? Is the day's options flow bullish premium buying, a hedge unwind or defensive positioning?

If any of the four reads is inconclusive, the right action is usually to wait, not to size down on a half-formed thesis. The dashboard's main job is filtering: it keeps you out of trades on days when the regime is ambiguous.

In plain terms

The dashboard does not tell you what to buy. It tells you what kind of market today is and so which families of trade have a positive expectation today and which do not. On most days at least one question honestly comes back "unclear", which makes the trade no trade.

Familiar ground

The dashboard plays the part of the workup before a regimen starts. Blood counts, organ function and performance status do not choose the drug. They decide whether any drug is given today. An equivocal result earns a repeat test, not a half dose. An inconclusive dashboard read earns a wait for the same reason: a smaller position on a half-formed thesis is still a position on a guess.

The rest of this chapter walks through the six groups of markers on the dashboard, then the table that turns a dashboard state into a family of trades.

3 · Trend and return markers

The first read asks where the market sits relative to its own structure. Most of its markers compare the index with moving averages, so those come first.

Moving averages

A bar is one period of a price chart, such as a day, with its open, high, low and close. Write \(C_t\) for the close of bar \(t\), which is \(S_t\) at the close in this book's notation. A moving average is the average of the price over a fixed number of recent bars, recomputed as each new bar arrives.

The simple moving average (SMA) of the last \(n\) closes weights each of them equally:

\[ M_t = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} C_{t-i} \]

Each new bar adds one close and drops the one from \(n\) bars back. So the SMA changes by \((C_t - C_{t-n})/n\): it moves on the close it drops as much as on the close it adds.

The exponential moving average (EMA) weights recent bars more heavily. At each bar it moves a fraction \(\alpha\) of the way from its last value toward the new close:

\[ E_t = \alpha\,C_t + (1-\alpha)\,E_{t-1} \]

For an \(n\)-bar EMA the fraction is \(\alpha = 2/(n+1)\). Unrolled, the weight on the close \(k\) bars back is \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^k\), so old closes fade away geometrically instead of dropping out on a fixed day.

Worked example: One close, two averages

Step 1: the EMA. Yesterday the 20-day EMA of SPX stood at 5,900, and today the index closes at 5,963. With \(\alpha = 2/21 = 0.0952\), gives \(5{,}900 + 0.0952 \times 63 = 5{,}906.0\).

Step 2: the SMA. Suppose the 20-day SMA was also 5,900 and today drops a close of 5,850 from its window. It rises by \((5{,}963 - 5{,}850)/20 = 5.65\), to 5,905.65, because the new close replaces a lower one. Had the dropped close been 5,990, the SMA would have fallen on an up day, by \((5{,}963 - 5{,}990)/20 = -1.35\).

Step 3: the distance. SPX now sits \((5{,}963 - 5{,}906)/5{,}906 = 0.97\%\) above its 20-day EMA, well inside the 3% the table below treats as overextended.

A moving average is rising when it is higher than it was a few bars earlier. The dashboard measures the 200-day SMA's slope over five days: \(M_t - M_{t-5}\), in index points.

Breadth measures how many of the index's members take part in its move. SPX weights each company by its market capitalization (Chapter 2A), so a handful of the largest stocks can carry the index while most members fall. RSP is an exchange-traded fund that holds the same stocks in equal weights. Its price divided by SPY's falls when the largest stocks outrun the rest, which is narrow leadership.

The quickest single read takes four conditions: SPX above a rising 20-day EMA, above a rising 50-day SMA, a 200-day slope above zero, and more than 55% of members above their own 50-day. If all four hold, the Dealer Mechanics Lab reads a Stage 2 environment in which pullback longs and put credit spreads have positive expectancy. If two or more fail, the regime is in transition, so tactical positioning should defer to defined-risk structures.

Worked example: A trend read

Step 1: the averages. With SPX closing at 6,050, the 20-day EMA is 6,000 and rising, the 50-day SMA is 5,900 and rising, and the 200-day SMA is 8 points higher than five days ago.

Step 2: breadth. Of the index's members, 58% close above their own 50-day average, more than the 55% the read needs.

Step 3: the verdict. All four conditions hold, so the read is Stage 2. The index is \(50/6{,}000 = 0.8\%\) above its 20-day EMA and \(150/5{,}900 = 2.5\%\) above its 50-day SMA, so it is not stretched by either threshold.

Step 4: a transition. A week later the 20-day EMA has flattened and only 51% of members are above their 50-day. Two conditions fail, so the read is a regime in transition: defined risk only, or no trade.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab does not say what a single failure means. The four-question rule covers it: an inconclusive read is a reason to wait.

The VIX percentile is the IV percentile of Chapter 5G applied to the VIX: the share of days in the past one or three years on which the VIX closed below today's level. The rule of 16 (Chapter 5E) turns the level into a daily move: a VIX of 16 prices a typical day of about 1%, while the stressed threshold of 25 prices about \(25/16 = 1.6\%\). The VVIX is the implied volatility of options on the VIX itself.

Chapter 11G folds these markers into one numerical score. For a same-day read, two conditions are enough for "normal regime" tactical positioning: the VIX/VIX3M ratio below 1.0 and the VIX percentile below the 60th. Backwardation or a top-quintile VIX, above the 80th percentile, each warrant a defensive posture. Between the 60th and the 80th percentile with the curve in contango, neither rule fires, so the composite scorecard of Chapter 11G decides.

Worked example: Two mornings

Step 1: a calm morning. The VIX is 15.2 and the VIX3M 17.6, a ratio of \(15.2/17.6 = 0.86\): contango. The VIX sits at its 45th percentile for the past year, so both conditions hold and normal positioning is allowed.

Step 2: the variance premium. SPX's 30-day realized volatility is 11%, so implied sits \(15.2 - 11 = 4.2\) volatility points above realized: a positive premium for option sellers (Chapter 10C).

Step 3: a stressed morning. The VIX is 27 and the VIX3M 23.5, a ratio of \(27/23.5 = 1.15\). That is backwardation, which calls for a defensive posture whatever the percentile says.

The Edge Book uses the same ratio as a regime filter, with a persistence rule against whipsaw (Chapter 10H). It halts new short-volatility entries once the ratio has closed above 1.00 on three closes and stops all put sales above 1.05 on three closes. The dashboard's same-day read and the filter's three-day rule answer different questions: whether to open a trade this morning, and whether a running strategy should stand down.

Two of these rows are one measurement. The risk reversal subtracts the put's IV from the call's, while the skew slope, the skew metric of Chapter 5G, subtracts the call's IV from the put's. Each is the other with its sign flipped, so reading both counts the same fact twice, the trap that Chapter 11C calls multicollinearity.

The expected move divides the price of the at-the-money straddle, a call and a put at the same strike, by spot. A straddle pays the absolute size of the move either way, so its price is the market's estimate of the average absolute move to expiry. That is about 0.8 of the one-standard-deviation move, for the reason the box below derives.

Why a straddle prices the average absolute move

Step 1: the payoff. A straddle struck at today's price \(S_0\) pays \(|S_T - S_0|\) at expiry, whichever way the index moves.

Step 2: a normal approximation. Over a short horizon, with no drift, write the move as \(S_T - S_0 \approx S_0\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\). Here \(Z\) is a standard normal variable, \(\sigma\) the implied volatility and \(T\) the time to expiry in years.

Step 3: the mean absolute value of a normal. By symmetry, \(\mathbb{E}|Z|\) is twice the integral of \(z\,\varphi(z)\) from 0 to infinity. Since \(\varphi(z) = e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) and the integral of \(z\,e^{-z^2/2}\) from 0 to infinity is 1, this gives \(\mathbb{E}|Z| = 2/\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.798\).

Step 4: the result. The straddle's value is about \(S_0\,\sigma\sqrt{T} \times 0.798\), so

\[ \frac{\text{straddle}}{S_0} \approx 0.8\,\sigma\sqrt{T} \]

The approximation ignores interest, dividends and skew, which matter little for a short-dated at-the-money straddle.

Worked example: Reading a straddle

Step 1: the expected move. SPX is at 6,000 and the 30-day at-the-money straddle costs $220. The expected move is \(220/6{,}000 = 3.67\%\), or about ±220 points to expiry.

Step 2: the implied volatility behind it. With \(\sqrt{30/365} = 0.287\), gives \(\sigma \approx 0.0367/(0.8 \times 0.287) = 0.16\), a 16% implied volatility.

Step 3: the one-standard-deviation move. That is \(0.16 \times 0.287 = 4.6\%\), about 275 points. A put credit spread with its short strike 220 points below spot sits at the average move, not at one standard deviation.

The forward volatility of Chapter 5G isolates an event. Suppose the 1-day at-the-money IV is 12% and the 5-day IV is 20%, with a report due in three days. Implied variance adds over time, so the four days after tomorrow carry a forward variance of \((0.20^2 \times 5 - 0.12^2 \times 1)/4 = 0.0464\), a forward volatility of \(\sqrt{0.0464} = 21.5\%\). The steep rise from 12% to 20% is the event premium, and the forward window prices it.

The two highest-value surface reads are the implied minus realized spread and forward volatility. The first says whether selling premium has a structural edge. The second says whether calendar spreads and diagonals make sense around upcoming events. The Edge Book's own filter for premium selling agrees on the first: contango and realized volatility below implied together give a green light (Chapter 10H).

Charm and vanna are the higher-order Greeks of Chapter 5D. The quick read takes three numbers, refreshed before the open: the GEX sign, the distance to the flip and the nearest large strike. Together they tell you whether intraday moves are likely to be damped, with GEX positive and spot well above the flip, or amplified, with GEX negative and spot near the flip.

The Edge Book keeps only the sign

The Edge Book reads these markers far more cautiously, for three reasons (Chapters 1G, 10H and 10I). Public GEX models are model-dependent, because each vendor assumes different positioning. The rise of 0DTE options since 2022 changed who holds what. And a Cboe study from 2025 estimated 0DTE market-maker gamma hedging at only about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity. From those reasons it draws four operational rules:

  • Use the sign of GEX as low-confidence regime context only.
  • Do not trade specific levels such as the call wall and the put wall: scalping around them is on its rejected list.
  • Do not size on the magnitude of GEX, which is too model-dependent.
  • If GEX disagrees with the VIX term structure or the realized-volatility filter, ignore it. If it agrees, it adds slight confidence, worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight and never a filter of its own.

It also lists open-interest concentration as descriptive, not predictive. Chapter 8A adds that the sign itself rests on an assumption about who holds what. Both sources treat the sign as regime context but part over reading the flip distance and the walls as levels.

\[ G_1 = G_0\,\frac{S_1 - S_f}{S_0 - S_f} \]

A trade that fills at the ask was started by a buyer who crossed the spread, as a taker of liquidity (Chapter 2C). Delta-adjusted premium scales each trade by its delta, so that it measures directional exposure rather than a count of contracts: a far out-of-the-money call, with its small delta, adds little.

Open interest changes only when both sides of a trade open positions, or both close them (Chapter 5B). When one side opens and the other closes, the position simply changes hands.

Worked example: Reading an open-interest change

Step 1: the data. A strike opens the day with open interest of 20,000 contracts and trades 6,000 during the session.

Step 2: new positions. If the next morning's open interest is 25,500, it rose by 5,500, so most of the 6,000 contracts opened new positions on both sides: new positioning.

Step 3: no new positions. If open interest is still 20,000, openings and closings balanced, or positions passed from one holder to another. The table reads that as closing: whichever it was, little new exposure was created.

Flow markers are noisy and prone to over-interpretation, so use them to confirm or refute a thesis you already have, never to generate one. "Unusual options activity" alone is not a signal. It becomes one only when it overlays fundamentals, technical structure and a supportive surface.

Familiar ground

A flow alert is an incidental finding on a scan ordered for something else. Read without a clinical question, most incidental findings are noise. Chasing each one harms more patients than it helps. The same finding in a patient whose history already points that way is worth acting on. Flow earns its place the same way: as a test applied to a thesis with a pretest probability, never as the source of the thesis.

An option-adjusted spread (OAS) is the extra yield a bond index pays over Treasuries of similar maturity, after allowing for options built into the bonds, such as the issuer's right to repay early. It is quoted in basis points, so 450 bps is 4.5 percentage points. HY OAS is the spread on high-yield bonds, those rated below investment grade. IG OAS is the spread on investment-grade bonds. BAML's US HY Master II and US IG Master are the bond indexes the two spreads are read from. A credit default swap pays out if a named borrower defaults, and CDX HY bundles many of them into one tradable index.

The 2Y10Y slope is the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year yield, a measure of the tilt of the yield curve. A negative slope means the curve is inverted. A real yield is a yield after inflation, read from TIPS, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. The US Dollar Index (DXY) tracks the dollar against a basket of major currencies. USDJPY is the number of yen per dollar. A sharp move in the yen can force leveraged carry trades funded in yen to unwind all at once, as on August 5, 2024, one of the stress episodes of Chapter 8C.

The quick cross-asset read takes three numbers: the direction of HY OAS, the direction of the DXY and the level of the MOVE index. Rising HY OAS with a rising dollar and the MOVE above 120 is cross-asset stress. It warrants a defensive posture whatever SPX is doing on the day.

Worked example: A cross-asset alarm

Step 1: the three readings. HY OAS has widened from 380 to 470 bps in a month, a move of 90 bps across the 450 bps line for "elevated". Over the last two weeks the DXY has risen 2.5%, and the MOVE index closed at 128.

Step 2: the verdict. All three conditions fire, so the posture is defensive, even on a morning when SPX opens higher.

The Edge Book's liquidity filter reads the same instruments with its own thresholds (Chapter 10H). For full short-volatility exposure it asks for the MOVE below 100 and HY OAS below 500 bps, with the TED spread as a third input. It also warns that these markets can be late, because the equity damage is often done before credit and funding markets show it.

10 · The morning routine

The operating pattern is a short routine: scan the dashboard before the open, between 8:00 and 9:00 AM ET, and make the regime determination with the four questions. Choose the strategy family from the translation table. Only then apply the conviction score of Chapter 13B and the kill rules of Chapter 13C to any specific trade. The Dealer Mechanics Lab calls this a 5-minute discipline that prevents most low-quality trades.

In practice
  • Write the read down: record the four answers each morning, with the markers behind them, before you look at any setup. A written read cannot quietly change to fit a trade you want.
  • Measure the filter by what it skips: the dashboard's value shows in losses avoided, so keep a log of the trades it vetoed and what they would have done.
  • Fewer trades cost less: each skipped trade saves its spread and slippage (Chapter 2D). SPX and XSP options are Section 1256 contracts, taxed more lightly than SPY's short-term gains (Chapter 2E).
  • Know your data: GEX, flow and some credit series come from vendors whose methods differ, so note the source beside every reading.
When it fails

The dashboard is a morning snapshot of a regime that can turn during the day. Its thresholds are typical values from practice, and the Dealer Mechanics Lab reports no fit or out-of-sample test behind them. Credit and funding markers can lag the equity damage, as the Edge Book warns. GEX readings rest on assumptions about who holds what (Chapter 8A), so a clean dealer read can be wrong in the direction that matters.

Decision rule

Before the open, answer the four questions.

  • If any answer is inconclusive, wait, rather than trading a smaller size.
  • Trend: all four quick-read conditions hold, so Stage 2 families are open. Two or more fail, so defined risk only, or cash.
  • Volatility: VIX/VIX3M below 1.0 and the VIX below its 60th percentile allow normal positioning. Backwardation, or a VIX above the 80th percentile, means defensive.
  • Cross-asset: rising HY OAS, a rising dollar and the MOVE above 120 together mean defensive, whatever SPX does.
  • Dealers: read the GEX sign as context only. When it disagrees with the term structure, follow the term structure.
  • Then pick the family from the translation table and size any trade with Chapters 13B and 13C.
Common mistake: counting one reading twice

The dashboard has more rows than independent facts. The risk reversal and the skew slope are one number with opposite signs, the VIX level and its percentile move together, and the MOVE index sits in two of the six tables. Count each measurement once, or a single fact looks like a consensus.

Common mistake: letting flow write the thesis

A large call sweep feels like news, yet without a trend, volatility and dealer read already pointing the same way it is only an incidental finding. Trading it is trading noise.

What to carry forward
  • The Dealer Mechanics Lab's claim is conditional: SPY mean-reverts over short horizons only when measurable conditions hold, and the dashboard measures them.
  • Four questions come before any trade: trend, volatility, dealer positioning and flow. If any answer is unclear, wait.
  • Each marker group has a quick read: the four trend conditions, the VIX/VIX3M ratio with the VIX percentile, GEX sign with the flip distance, and HY OAS with the DXY and the MOVE.
  • The Edge Book keeps only the sign of GEX, at low weight, and rejects trading the walls. Several cells of the translation table are trades it doubts.

Question 1

Question 2

The VIX is 17.5 at its 70th percentile, and the VIX3M is 19. What does the volatility quick read say?

Answer

The ratio is \(17.5/19 = 0.92\), in contango. The percentile is above the 60th, though, so the "normal regime" condition fails. It is below the 80th, so the defensive rule does not fire either. Neither rule decides, so the composite scorecard of Chapter 11G takes over.

Question 3

SPX is at 6,000 and the 7-day at-the-money straddle costs $95. What move does it price, and what implied volatility does that suggest?

Answer

The expected move is \(95/6{,}000 = 1.58\%\). With \(\sqrt{7/365} = 0.138\), gives \(\sigma \approx 0.0158/(0.8 \times 0.138) = 0.143\), about 14%. The one-standard-deviation move is \(0.143 \times 0.138 = 2.0\%\), larger than the straddle's 1.58%.

Question 4

In the forward GEX map, spot is 6,000, GEX is +3 billion and the flip is 5,900. What shock takes spot to the flip, and what is GEX after a 2% fall?

Answer

The flip is \(5{,}900/6{,}000 - 1 = -1.67\%\) away. A 2% fall takes spot to 5,880, and gives \(3 \times (5{,}880 - 5{,}900)/(6{,}000 - 5{,}900) = -0.6\) billion: the regime has changed. The Edge Book would read only the sign, and at low confidence.

Question 5

An options strike trades 8,000 contracts and its open interest rises by 7,200 overnight. Another trades 8,000 and its open interest is unchanged. What does each suggest?

Answer

The first is mostly new positions opened on both sides: new positioning. The second created no net new positions: openings and closings balanced, or contracts changed hands. The table reads it as closing, though either way it adds no new exposure.

Question 6

Answer

This is the trend or stress regime. It prefers momentum in the direction of weakness, long puts or put spreads, and hedges. It avoids mean reversion, buying dips and selling volatility.
