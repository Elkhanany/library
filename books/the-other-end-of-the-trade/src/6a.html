Part VI · Dealers · Chapter 6A

Who is on the other side

The dealer's business

Where we are

Part V taught the option contract and the Greeks that measure its risk. This part turns to the firm that sells you the option: who it is, why it takes your trade, and what it is then forced to do. This chapter meets that firm and follows one order from your click to its expiry.

What you need first
  • The bid-ask spread (Chapter 1F) and adverse selection (Chapter 1G).
  • Wholesalers, the NBBO and where an order is routed (Chapter 2B).
  • The clearinghouse between the two sides of a listed trade (Chapter 4A).
  • Calls, puts and the contract multiplier of 100 (Chapter 5A).
  • Delta as a hedge ratio, in share-equivalents (Chapter 5C).

Every option you have ever traded had someone on the other side: not a mysterious "market", but a specific business with specific obligations and specific behaviors. The mechanical decisions that business is forced to make, second by second, add up to one of the largest non-fundamental forces acting on the S&P 500 on any given day with no news.

This part builds that picture from zero, assuming only what Parts I to V provide: what an option is, and what its Greeks measure. Mathematics appears when it earns its place, and every formula is followed by what it means in plain terms.

2 · How much dealer hedging matters

Much of what circulates on social media and retail trading platforms about "dealer flows" and "gamma squeezes" is qualitatively right but quantitatively overstated. The academic literature gives effect sizes in tens of basis points, not full percentage points. Where this part cites numbers, they come from peer-reviewed work or directly from Cboe's own data, and where industry sources disagree with academia, the text says so. The goal is calibration: knowing not just that dealer hedging matters but how much it matters, and when.

Four notes that apply throughout this part
  • Dealer hedging is a real and quantifiable force. It moves markets in measurable, statistically significant ways, but it is not magic.
  • The effect sizes are modest on typical days. Most days, dealer hedging accounts for tens of basis points of intraday move at most, while the dominant force is news and information flow.
  • The effect sizes can be large on stress days. Chapter 8C describes three conditions: negative-gamma dealer positioning, mechanical rule-based hedging demand and degraded market depth. When they coexist, the linear framework can break, with effects measured in full percentage points or more.
  • Gamma exposure is positioning, not outcome. Gamma exposure (GEX), the dealers' total gamma of Chapter 8A, tells you what dealers would do given a move. It does not tell you whether a move is coming, so do not conflate the two.
Familiar ground

Clinical research has taught you to separate statistical significance from effect size. A large trial can show a hazard ratio of 0.95 with a tiny p-value while the absolute benefit is too small to change practice. Dealer hedging is in that position on most days: the effect is real and significant, and it is measured in tens of basis points. Stress days are the subgroup in which the effect becomes large.

The two traditions this book draws on weigh this force differently. The dealer-mechanics tradition of this part treats dealer hedging as a measurable force, modest most days and large in stress. The edge-by-edge case of Part X is more skeptical. It cites a 2025 Cboe study estimating 0DTE market-maker gamma hedging at about 0.2% of SPX daily liquidity, and it uses the sign of dealer gamma only as low-confidence context (Chapter 10H). It also rejects trading around gamma-exposure levels in a retail account (Chapter 10I). Read the chapters that follow with both positions in mind.

With those caveats in place, the chapters of this part are some of the most important framework material in the book.

3 · The market maker is not betting against you

Begin with a single small trade: you buy one SPY call option, strike $600, expiring in 30 days. You click "buy" in your broker's app, and a millisecond later you own the contract. Somewhere, a market maker now holds the opposite of what you hold: it is short a call. Who is it, and why did it take the other side?

This is the most important point in this part. A market maker, called the dealer in options, is not a speculator on the other side of your trade. It has no view on whether SPY goes up or down. Its entire business is to quote two prices at once, a bid at which it buys and an offer at which it sells, and to capture the spread between them.

Worked example: The spread on one contract

Step 1: the quote. Suppose the call has a fair value of $5.00, around which the market maker quotes a bid of $4.95 and an offer of $5.05.

Step 2: the round trip. You buy at the offer, $5.05, while the market maker stands ready to buy the same contract back from someone else at the bid, $4.95. If it completes that round trip, it keeps $0.10 a share.

Step 3: the contract. One contract covers 100 shares, so the round trip earns $0.10 × 100 = $10.

A $10 round trip is small until you multiply it by 100 contracts a second, across every option in every name. Then a franchise emerges, built on scale rather than on being right about direction.

"By taking the market risk to trade in this fashion, market makers can earn a 'spread' between the bid and the ask. In today's highly competitive and efficient markets, the bid-ask spread is often much less than one percent of the price of a security." Citadel Securities

4 · The problem the dealer just created

Back to the trade: you bought a call and the dealer sold it, so the dealer is now short a call. It loses money if SPY rallies and makes money if SPY falls. It holds a directional exposure that it did not want and is not paid to hold.

Carrying that exposure overnight is unthinkable, because the dealer captured a spread of about 10 cents a share while a modest move against the position costs many times that.

Worked example: One move against one contract

Step 1: the loss. With SPY at $600, a 1% rally is $6, which at a delta of 0.50 (Chapter 5C) costs the short call about 0.50 × $6 = $3 a share, or $300 a contract.

Step 2: the comparison. That is 30 times the $10 round-trip spread, so across a whole book the dealer would lose months of franchise profit on a single bad afternoon.

So the dealer neutralizes the directional exposure at once and mechanically, by delta hedging. Delta hedging, the subject of Chapter 6C, is the engine that drives nearly everything in this part and the three after it.

5 · One trade, end to end

Walk through what happens when you click "buy" on a single SPY $600 call. The chain is mechanical, fast and almost entirely invisible to you, with times measured from your click in milliseconds (ms).

  1. At 0 ms, you click "buy" at the NBBO offer of $3.05, sending a marketable limit order at $3.05 for one contract.
  2. At 1 to 3 ms, your broker's routing engine decides which venue gets the order. For SPY, an equity option, that is almost always a wholesaler: Citadel Securities, Susquehanna or Wolverine. SPX and XSP index options route directly to Cboe instead (Chapter 2B).
  3. At 3 to 5 ms, the wholesaler's internal matching engine checks its principal book, the inventory it trades for its own account, where its own mark on the call is $3.03. It fills you at the NBBO, $3.05, which gives you no price improvement against the public offer and gives it $0.02 a share over its internal mark. That $0.02 is its actual edge.
  4. At 5 to 10 ms, the wholesaler is short one SPY $600 call, and its risk system computes the delta at the current spot as 0.58. That makes it short 58 share-equivalents, so the system fires a hedge order in SPY shares, typically batched with hundreds of other flows arriving at the same moment.
  5. At 10 to 50 ms, the wholesaler buys 58 SPY shares, or proportionally fewer if opposite flow already offsets part of the exposure. The hedge typically executes at or inside the NBBO, through internalization, a lit exchange or an alternative trading system (ATS). The principal book is now delta-flat with respect to your trade.
  6. One day later, the trade settles. You hold one long call, with no shares delivered until exercise or expiry, while the wholesaler's offsetting short is registered at the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC). From then on your counterparty risk is on the OCC, not on the wholesaler.
  7. Throughout the option's life, the wholesaler delta-hedges continuously as spot moves, with gamma, vanna and charm all feeding in (Chapter 5D). Its profit and loss (P&L) over the trade's life depends on whether realized volatility along the hedge path exceeds the implied volatility (IV) built into your $3.05 fill. That comparison is the gamma-theta arithmetic of Chapter 5C.
  8. At expiry, if the call expires worthless, you lose the premium and the wholesaler keeps it, less its hedge P&L. If it expires in the money (ITM), SPX and XSP settle in cash, while SPY settles physically, with shares changing hands.

6 · The franchise at scale

One trade looks negligible: $0.02 of edge on a one-contract order is $2, so it is volume that makes the business. Citadel Securities discloses executing roughly 35% of all US retail equity and options volume. On a typical day that is billions of share-equivalents and tens of millions of options contracts running through its book.

The whole US options market traded approximately 13.8 billion contracts in 2025. If Citadel handled 35% of the retail share, and a smaller but still substantial fraction of institutional flow, its annual options volume runs to billions of contracts. The franchise's revenue, in simplified form, is the edge on each contract times the contracts traded, less what the dealer spends:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Revenue} = {} & \textstyle\sum_i e_i\, n_i \\ & - \text{hedge slippage} \\ & - \text{technology and pay} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(e_i\) is the edge per contract on product \(i\) and \(n_i\) the number of contracts traded in it. Hedge slippage is what the dealer loses between the price it expects for its hedges and the price it gets.

Worked example: A toy franchise

Suppose a wholesaler earns $1.00 of edge a contract on 1 billion contracts a year. The first term of is then $1 billion, before slippage, technology and pay, and at $2.00 a contract on 2 billion contracts it is $4 billion.

Per-contract edges run from about $0.50 to $2.00 across the retail options book. With hundreds of millions of contracts a year, the franchise produces multi-billion-dollar revenues consistently. The arithmetic is dispiriting if you imagine taking the other side, and gratifying if you are the wholesaler. Jane Street's $20.5 billion in 2024 and $39.6 billion in 2025 are not aberrations: they are the profile a high-volume franchise with a small edge per trade tends toward at scale.

7 · Risk capital and alpha capital

Two kinds of capital are at work in any market-making operation. Telling them apart shows what dealers care about and what they do not.

Risk capital is the collateral the dealer must post against its open positions, sized to cover the worst loss it could take before it can unwind them. Regulation after 2008 (Basel III, Dodd-Frank and EMIR) made this constraint binding through stress tests. A modern dealer's biggest operational constraint is not how much it can make per trade. It is how much capital each book consumes per unit of expected revenue.

Alpha capital is what a firm uses for active directional or relative-value bets: outright views the desk holds. Market makers run negligible alpha capital relative to their risk capital, since they are nominally flat in direction. In practice every dealer book carries small residual exposures that it chooses not to hedge perfectly: to skew, to the term structure, to basis and to correlation. These residuals are where the real edge of a well-run dealer book lives, and where the franchise can lose money in stress.

If you read that "Citadel Securities lost $3 billion in March 2020", the loss almost certainly refers to the residual book, not the spread franchise. The spread franchise compounds quietly through every regime, while the residual book produces the headline losses (and the headline gains in benign years).

8 · Why the dealer takes your trade

Retail traders rarely ask why the dealer is willing to take the other side of their trade in particular, and whether it worries that they might know something.

The honest answer is that, in aggregate, retail flow is informationally uninformed. A wholesaler internalizing $50 billion of retail flow a month does not worry that the median order knows tomorrow's news. It worries about the tails: the rare retail order that is informed, through insider activity or leaked earnings data. Those orders are flagged and routed to lit exchanges rather than internalized. Routing them out transfers the adverse selection risk to whichever venue takes them.

Worked example: Why the tails matter

Step 1: the mix. Suppose 999 of every 1,000 retail orders are uninformed, each leaving the wholesaler $2 of edge a contract, while the one informed order costs it $100.

Step 2: the expected edge. Per order, the wholesaler expects to earn \(0.999 \times 2 - 0.001 \times 100 = 1.998 - 0.100 = \$1.90\), a little under the $2 it makes on an uninformed order.

Step 3: the breaking point. With \(p\) for the informed fraction, the edge vanishes when \((1 - p) \times 2 = 100\,p\), which gives \(p = 2/102 = 0.0196\). At about 2 informed orders in 100 this franchise earns nothing, which is why flagging the informed few is worth so much.

For institutional flow the calculation is different, because a hedge fund placing a $200 million SPX options trade is presumed informed. The dealer quotes wider, or refuses, and the order goes through the order book directly. This is why "institutional spreads" on large index options trades are often 10 to 30 bps wider than the SIP NBBO that retail sees. The Securities Information Processor (SIP) is the public feed that computes that NBBO.

The structural lesson: retail order flow is profitable to wholesalers because it is uninformed in aggregate. The wholesaler is not paid for taking risk against a smart counterparty, because it hedges at once. It is paid for providing immediacy, and for capturing the spread against the noise that retail order flow represents in aggregate.

In practice
  • Your SPY option order goes to a wholesaler, while your SPX order goes to Cboe. Chapter 2B describes both paths and what each costs you.
  • The spread is the dealer's income and your cost. On the toy quote above, a round trip costs you $10 a contract before any move in the price.
  • After settlement, your counterparty is the OCC, so your claim on a listed option does not depend on the dealer that sold it.
  • Treat dealer-flow claims as effect sizes: on a typical day, expect hedging to move the index by tens of basis points at most.
When it fails

The picture of a flat, hedged franchise holds on ordinary days and fails in stress in two ways. The residual book, which dealers choose not to hedge, can lose heavily. And when the conditions of Chapter 8C coexist, hedging flows can move the index by full percentage points or more instead of tens of basis points.

Common mistake: "the dealer is betting against me"

The belief is that the firm on the other side profits when your trade loses, and so wants you to lose. In fact it hedges the direction away within milliseconds and earns the spread whichever way the price moves. Your P&L and the dealer's are not mirror images: the dealer's comes from spread and from realized against implied volatility, not from your forecast being wrong.

Decision rule

Before you act on a claim about dealer flows, check three things:

  • Ask for the effect size. Tens of basis points is the typical-day scale, while a claim of several percent needs the stress conditions of Chapter 8C.
  • Separate positioning from outcome. Gamma exposure says what dealers would do if the index moved, not whether it moves, so do not trade it as a forecast.
  • Read the other tradition before you trade on it. Part X uses the sign of dealer gamma only as low-confidence context for sizing and rejects trading around its levels (Chapters 10H and 10I), so weigh its reasons beside this part's.
What to carry forward
  • The dealer on the other side of your option is a market maker, which earns the bid-ask spread at scale and is not betting on direction.
  • Selling you an option leaves it with directional exposure it is not paid to hold, so it delta-hedges at once.
  • Its revenue comes from edge per contract times enormous volume, and its large losses from the residual book it chooses not to hedge.
  • Retail flow is profitable to dealers because it is uninformed in aggregate, which makes the rare informed orders the risk.
  • Dealer hedging is a real force of tens of basis points on typical days, and larger only in stress.

9 · Check yourself

Question 1

You buy 10 calls at a market maker's $5.05 offer, and it later buys them back from someone else at its $4.95 bid. What does it earn on the round trip?

Answer

It earns the $0.10 spread on each share: \(0.10 \times 100 \times 10 = \$100\) on the round trip.

Question 2

What hedge does a wholesaler put on after selling you 20 SPY calls with a delta of 0.40?

Answer

It is short \(20 \times 100 \times 0.40 = 800\) share-equivalents, so it buys 800 SPY shares, less any part that opposite flow already offsets.

Question 3

In the toy franchise of "Why the tails matter", informed orders rise from 1 in 1,000 to 3 in 100. What is the expected edge per order now?

Answer

It is \(0.97 \times 2 - 0.03 \times 100 = 1.94 - 3.00 = -\$1.06\), a loss, since 3% is above the breakeven of about 2%.

Question 4

Who owes you your option's payoff if the wholesaler that sold you the call runs into trouble a week after the trade settles?

Answer

The Options Clearing Corporation, because after settlement it is the counterparty to both sides. Your counterparty risk is on the OCC, not on the wholesaler.

Question 5

If a large market maker loses billions in a crash, has spread-based market making stopped working?

Answer

Not necessarily, because the spread franchise is hedged and compounds through every regime. Large losses almost always come from the residual book: the skew, term, basis and correlation exposures the firm chose to carry.
