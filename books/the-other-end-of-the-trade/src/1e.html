Drawdown, leverage and margin

Why the path matters as much as the destination

Where we are

Chapter 1D measured risk as volatility and showed what that number cannot see. This chapter measures risk the way an account lives through it: as the fall from its last peak, deepened by borrowed money and cut short by margin.

What you need first

1 · Drawdown: the fall from the last peak

A drawdown is the peak-to-trough loss in an account's value over a period, measured from the highest value reached so far rather than from where you started. An account that grows from $100,000 to $150,000 and then falls to $120,000 is in a 20% drawdown while still up 20% overall.

Write \(W_t\) for the account's value at time \(t\) and \(P_t = \max_{s \le t} W_s\) for its running peak, so that the drawdown at time \(t\) is the fraction of the peak lost since:

\[ D_t = 1 - \frac{W_t}{P_t} \]

Applied to the example, the formula gives \(1 - 120{,}000/150{,}000 = 0.20\), the 20% drawdown above. A whole record of drawdowns is summarized by two numbers:

  • Maximum drawdown (MaxDD) is the largest \(D_t\) in the record, the single worst peak-to-trough loss.
  • Conditional drawdown (CDaR-α) is the average drawdown beyond the α percentile, so that at α = 95% it averages the worst 5% of drawdowns.

Volatility is symmetric, counting a surprise gain as heavily as a surprise loss, while drawdown is one-sided. A strategy can have low volatility and still a 40% maximum drawdown if it crashes occasionally. For whether a portfolio survives, drawdown is the binding constraint, not volatility.

Fat tails make drawdowns deeper than a normal model predicts (Chapter 1B). For a strategy with a 5% mean return and 10% volatility, naive Gaussian math says the maximum drawdown over 10 years should be around 25%. In reality, fat-tailed strategies often see drawdowns of 40% to 50%.

From 1990 to 2024, the S&P 500 index (SPX) held long-only returned about 10% a year with a maximum drawdown of about 55%. The PUT index (Chapter 1B), which sells SPX puts monthly, returned about 9.5% with a maximum drawdown of about 33%. Their Calmar ratios (Chapter 1D), return divided by maximum drawdown, are about 0.18 and 0.29. That meaningful improvement is paid for in path dependency, the subject of this chapter's last section.

2 · What it takes to recover

A loss leaves each dollar worth \(1 - D\), so the gain \(G\) that repairs it must satisfy \((1 - D)(1 + G) = 1\), which solves to

\[ G = \frac{D}{1-D} \]

As \(D\) approaches 1, the denominator shrinks toward zero and the gain needed grows without limit. If the account then grows at a steady rate \(g\) a year, it regains its peak when \((1 - D)(1 + g)^t = 1\), which after taking logarithms (Chapter 1A) gives the years needed:

\[ t = \frac{-\ln(1-D)}{\ln(1+g)} \]
Worked example: The arithmetic of 2008

Step 1: the gain needed. By , a strategy that loses 40% needs \(0.40/0.60 = 0.667\), a 67% recovery, to break even. A strategy that loses 60% needs \(0.60/0.40 = 1.50\), a 150% recovery.

Step 2: the years needed. At a hypothetical 8% a year, gives \(0.511/0.077 = 6.6\) years to recover the 40% loss and \(0.916/0.077 = 11.9\) years for the 60% loss.

Loss \(D\)Gain needed \(G\)Years to recover at 8% a year

This asymmetry of drawdown is a real cost, not a curiosity. Recovery time is non-linear in loss size: doubling a loss from 20% to 40% more than doubles the years needed.

3 · Leverage: holding more than you own

Leverage \(k\) is the ratio of total exposure, the market value of everything your positions hold or control, to your capital:

\[ k = \frac{\text{exposure}}{\text{capital}} \]

A $1M account holding $1.4M of SPX has 1.4× leverage, with the extra $0.4M borrowed directly or through a contract. If the exposure returns \(R\) in a year and the borrowed \(k - 1\) per dollar of capital costs a rate \(r_b\), your return on capital is

\[ R_k = k\,R - (k-1)\,r_b \]

With \(k = 1.4\), \(R = 10\%\) and a hypothetical borrowing rate of 4%, that is \(14\% - 1.6\% = 12.4\%\). Leverage multiplies excess return and volatility alike, which is why it leaves the Sharpe ratio unchanged (Chapter 1D). It scales a trade, losses included, without improving it.

Worked example: A levered drawdown

Step 1: the fall. Hold $1.4M of SPX on $1M of capital through a 55% fall, the SPX maximum drawdown above, leaving financing aside. The holding falls to $630,000 while the $400,000 loan stays where it was, which leaves $230,000 of capital: a 77% loss, 1.4 times the fall.

Step 2: the repair. By , the 77% loss needs \(0.77/0.23 = 3.35\), a 335% gain, where the same fall without leverage needed \(0.55/0.45 = 1.22\), a 122% gain.

Step 3: the trough. You now hold $630,000 of SPX on $230,000 of capital, which is 2.7× leverage although you never traded.

A fall of \(1/k\) in the exposure wipes out the capital entirely: a fall of 71% at 1.4× leverage, but only 25% at 4×. Treat leverage like the dose of a cytotoxic drug: set it from the drawdown the account can survive, not from the return you hope for.

Three ways to take leverage

There are three economically distinct ways to take leverage, each with its own cost:

  1. Borrow to buy, with a margin loan or with box spread financing, at an explicit cost: the interest rate.
  2. Use futures such as ES and MES, the S&P 500 futures of Chapter 2A. The implicit cost is the basis between spot and futures, roughly SOFR + 10 to 20 bps.
  3. Use options for replication, such as LEAPS or deep in-the-money (ITM) calls. The implicit cost is embedded leverage drag, typically 50 to 150 bps annualized (Frazzini-Pedersen 2012, 2022).

A basis point (bp) is a hundredth of a percentage point, and SOFR (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate) is a benchmark rate for overnight borrowing.

The first two are usually cheap, while the third is expensive and has path-dependent properties: Greeks that shift as the market moves, margin that can expand, and assignment risk on short options.

Common mistake: the leverage trap

A common retail error is to build a complex options structure for leveraged equity exposure and believe it has "edge" beyond simple leverage. It usually does add a small positive expected return from some variance risk premium (VRP) and some skew premium. It also adds embedded leverage drag and operational complexity in rolls, assignments and margin expansion. Its third cost is path dependency, since it can be force-liquidated even if its terminal payoff is fine. Once these costs are counted, it usually offers no edge over simple ES futures or box-spread financing. Chapter 13E works one such structure through before Chapter 10I rejects it.

4 · Margin: the deposit that can be called

Margin is the capital your broker requires you to hold to keep a position open, much like a deposit. Each day the broker values your positions at market prices, which is called mark-to-market (MTM). The gains and losses reach your equity whether or not you have closed anything.

If your equity falls below the requirement, the broker issues a margin call asking you to pay in money or reduce the position. If you do not do so in time, the broker closes positions for you at whatever prices the market offers, which is forced liquidation. Brokers set the requirement with one of two main models:

  • Reg-T margin is a fixed percentage of position size, simple but capital-inefficient.
  • Portfolio margin (PM) is based on stress-tested theoretical losses, typically from a ±15% SPX move plus shifts in implied volatility (IV).

Chapter 2E gives the rules for each account type and Chapter 12G the margin on each options structure. Under PM, margin requirements expand non-linearly in stress: in March 2020, many PM accounts saw their requirements double or triple in 1 to 3 days. Brokers also raised house requirements, their own add-ons above the minimum, during the crisis.

Worked example: A margin call from two directions

Step 1: the stress. You hold $100,000 of equity against a PM requirement of $40,000. A sell-off then marks your positions down $35,000 while the requirement doubles to $80,000, as many did in March 2020.

EquityRequirementMargin call
Calm$100,000$40,000No
The loss alone$65,000$40,000No

Step 2: the call. Neither shock alone breaches the cushion, but together they leave you $15,000 short. If you cannot pay that in time, the broker sells at stressed prices.

The two shocks arrive together because one market stress drives both. This combination is what creates path-dependent risk: even if your terminal payoff is acceptable, you can be forced to close the position before the path completes.

5 · Path dependency: surviving to the payoff

A strategy is path-dependent if its MTM trajectory matters as much as its terminal payoff. Most options strategies are path-dependent in some way, because an option's payoff is fixed only at expiry while its market price, which your margin follows, moves every day until then (Chapter 5A).

Path dependency is the single most underestimated risk in retail options structures. Consider a 1-year SPX put spread sold as "defined-risk".

Worked example: The forced-liquidation trap

Step 1: the terminal risk. The strikes are $30 apart, so at expiry the spread can cost you at most $30 per contract. You sold it for $5 of premium, which makes the maximum loss $25.

Step 2: the path. Now SPX drops 25% in 30 days and IV doubles. With the index far lower and more uncertainty priced in, the spread might mark to −$22 even though its payoff at expiry is unchanged.

Step 3: the forced exit. If the position is large enough that this MTM loss exceeds your margin cushion, you are force-liquidated at −$22, locking in \(22/25 = 88\%\) of the maximum loss. Held to expiry, the spread might have recovered to −$10 or −$5.

In operational terms, "defined risk" describes the terminal loss but not the path loss. Size every options structure to survive the path, not just the terminal payoff. Path-dependent risk takes several forms in an options account:

  • Margin expansion that forces liquidation before prices revert.
  • Early assignment on short options, before the expiry you planned around.
  • Time decay (theta) that cannot be recovered if the MTM deteriorates and you have to close.
  • Forced rolling into a later expiry, which changes the strategy from what you intended.

A few strategies are not path-dependent: long stock with no margin, a cash-secured put held to expiry (no margin call is possible), and box spread financing (no MTM risk).

Familiar ground

Forced liquidation is a competing risk, in the sense of a competing event in survival analysis. A regimen with an excellent response rate at 12 months still fails the patients who die of toxicity in month 2, because they never reach the endpoint. A position closed by a margin call never reaches the expiry where its payoff is defined.

In practice

A cash account cannot receive a margin call, because nothing in it is borrowed. A cash-secured put held to expiry escapes path dependency that way, at the price of idle cash. In a margin account, sizing for the path typically means sizing 30% to 50% smaller than the terminal payoff would suggest is acceptable.

When it fails

Sizing for the path fails when the stress outgrows the one you sized for. Brokers raised their house requirements in March 2020 while the crisis ran, so a cushion sized in calm conditions can be too small when you need it.

Common mistake: "defined risk means safe"

The belief is that defined-risk spreads are safe because you know your maximum loss. Defined terminal loss does not eliminate (a) mark-to-market loss that requires margin, (b) liquidity risk on the spread, or (c) margin expansion that exceeds your cushion. The 2018 and 2020 episodes are full of "defined-risk" trades that were force-liquidated all the same.

Decision rule

Before any trade, ask whether it can be force-liquidated mid-path.

  • If it cannot, because it is cash-secured, unlevered and held to expiry, size it from the terminal loss.
  • If it can, size so that the worst mark along the path and the expanded margin requirement both fit inside your cushion.
  • For leverage, prefer futures or box-spread financing to options replication, at a level whose worst drawdown you could recover from.
What to carry forward
  • A drawdown is the fall from the running peak. Repairing a loss \(D\) takes a gain of \(D/(1-D)\), which grows without limit as \(D\) nears 1.
  • Leverage multiplies drawdowns and the gains needed to repair them, while losses push leverage higher still.
  • A margin requirement can expand just as your positions lose, so a margin call can close a "defined-risk" position before its payoff arrives. Size for the path, not only for the terminal payoff.

6 · Check yourself

Question 1

You run 2× leverage on an index position, and the index falls 30%. What is your loss, and what leverage do you now hold?

Answer

Per dollar of capital you hold $2 of index and owe $1. The holding falls to $1.40, leaving $0.40 of capital: a 60% loss, which says needs a 150% gain to repair. Holding $1.40 of index on $0.40 of capital, you now run 3.5× leverage without having traded.

Question 2

What is path dependency, and why does it make "defined-risk" trades not actually risk-defined?

Answer

A strategy is path-dependent when its mark-to-market along the way matters as much as its terminal payoff. A defined-risk spread caps only the loss at expiry. Before expiry its mark can approach that cap while margin requirements expand. A margin call can close it there, as it closed the put spread at 88% of its maximum loss.

Question 3

A $1M account holds $1.4M of SPX exposure, once through LEAPS and once through ES futures. Both have the same delta exposure, the same change in value for a small move in the index. Why is the LEAPS version riskier?

Answer

Futures cost roughly SOFR + 10 to 20 bps, while LEAPS carry embedded leverage drag of typically 50 to 150 bps a year. The options also bring path-dependent properties: Greeks that let the exposure drift from 1.4× as the market moves, margin that can expand in stress, and assignment on any short legs.
