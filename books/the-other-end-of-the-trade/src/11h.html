1 · What a setup specifies

The framework supports four primary swing setups, or archetypes, for index and large-cap single names. Each is a formal pattern with measurable entry conditions, sizing rules and invalidation criteria. Together they cover what the Dealer Mechanics Lab calls the high-probability operational surface for swing trading on horizons of 2 to 10 days. A fifth setup, pre-earnings momentum on single names, has Chapter 11I to itself, because its catalyst dynamics need the room.

Every setup answers the same questions: where its edge comes from, which regime it requires, what triggers it, and how to execute and size it. It also says in advance where it takes profit, when it gives up on time and what proves it wrong. Those last answers use units worth defining first.

R is the dollar loss you take if the stop is hit, so a profit target of 2R is a gain of twice that. A trade's R-multiple is its profit or loss divided by R. A time stop closes a trade after a set number of trading days, whatever its profit or loss. Chapter 11N gives the reason: a setup that has not paid within its typical horizon has a low expected value from there on.

Sizing from the stop

"Risk 1% of the portfolio" means the dollar loss if the stop is hit, not the notional deployed (Chapter 13C). With a risk budget \(B\) in dollars, a stop distance \(d\) per unit of the underlying and a multiplier \(m\), which is 1 for shares, the number of units is

\[ n = \left\lfloor \frac{B}{d\,m} \right\rfloor \]

where the brackets round down to a whole number. The notional is \(n\,m\,S\), so each dollar of risk carries about \(S/d\) dollars of notional: one over the stop's distance as a fraction of the price. For an option, \(d\) is the fall in the option's own price at the stop, which depends on its delta (Chapter 5C).

Worked example: Risk against notional

Step 1: the budget. A $300,000 portfolio risking 1% a trade has a budget of \(B = \$3{,}000\).

Step 2: the size. SPY trades at $600.00 and the stop sits 0.75% below, at $595.50, so \(d = \$4.50\). By , \(n = \lfloor 3{,}000/4.50 \rfloor = 666\) shares.

Step 3: the notional. The 666 shares cost $399,600, 133% of the portfolio. That is 133 times the dollar risk, as \(1/0.0075 = 133\) predicts.

Step 4: the targets. Profit targets of 1.5R and 2R sit at $606.75 and $609.00.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab puts the notional of equity swing trades at typically 20 to 100 times the dollar risk, which corresponds to stops 1% to 5% from the price. The tighter stops of the two mean-reversion setups below give more, 100 to 200 times at 0.5% to 1.0%. A long option bounds the notional by its premium.

2 · Setup 1: trend pullback and reclaim

The Trend Pullback, in full the Trend Pullback and Reclaim, buys a dip inside an uptrend.

Edge source. Mean reversion within a confirmed Stage 2 trend. In the Dealer Mechanics Lab's account, dealers with positive gamma exposure fade the pullback by buying at moving-average support, which gives the trade a mechanical tailwind (Chapter 8B).

RuleSetting
RegimeA composite signal of +30 or more. Stage 2 confirmed: the 30-bar SMA's ATR-normalized slope above +0.05, price above the 30-bar SMA, and ADX between 20 and 30. Positive net dealer GEX, or price above the gamma flip. The VIX below 22, since above it even Stage 2 setups have wider noise distributions. The VIX term structure in contango, with the VIX below VIX3M
TriggerA daily close with RSI(2) below 10 or IBS below 0.20, on a pullback that touches or breaches the 10-EMA or the 21-EMA from above
ExecutionEnter at the next trading day's open if the signal day's level holds in pre-market trading, or intraday once the price reclaims the EMA
VehicleXSP for tax efficiency, SPY or QQQ for finer sizing in shares, SPX for size
SizingRisk 1% to 1.5% of the portfolio, with the stop below the entry day's low, typically 0.5% to 1.0% below entry
Target1.5R to 2R, or a close above the prior 5-day high, whichever comes first
Time stop5 trading days: close regardless of profit or loss
InvalidationA close below the entry day's low, or the 30-bar SMA's slope turning negative, which marks a stage transition

The regime asks for more than the bull-tilt bucket of Chapter 11G, which starts at +10. It also asks for a narrower ADX window than the Stage 2 classifier of Chapter 11E, which needs only ADX above 18.

The trigger is RSI(2), the relative strength index over just two bars, or the internal bar strength (IBS). With \(\bar{U}\) and \(\bar{D}\) the Wilder averages of the gains and losses, RSI is \(100 - 100/(1 + \bar{U}/\bar{D})\). Over two bars, Wilder smoothing gives each new change half the weight, so a couple of down days drive RSI(2) toward zero. IBS says where the day closed within its own range, with high \(H\), low \(L\) and close \(C\):

\[ \text{IBS} = \frac{C - L}{H - L} \]
Worked example: RSI(2) falls fast

Step 1: the first reading. Closes run 100.00, 101.00 and 99.00. The first two changes, +1.00 and −2.00, average to a gain of 0.50 and a loss of 1.00, so RSI(2) is \(100 - 100/(1 + 0.5) = 33.3\).

Step 2: two more down days. At 97.00 the averages become \((0.50 + 0)/2 = 0.25\) and \((1.00 + 2.00)/2 = 1.50\), so RSI(2) is 14.3. At 95.50 they become 0.125 and 1.50, so RSI(2) is 7.7, below 10.

Step 3: the IBS alternative. A day with a high of 603.20, a low of 594.80 and a close of 596.10 has, by , \(\text{IBS} = 1.30/8.40 = 0.155\), below 0.20.

How ATR, ADX and the normalized slope are computed

The true range of bar \(t\) counts an overnight gap as part of its movement, using the previous close \(C_{t-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TR}_t = \max\big(&H_t - L_t,\ |H_t - C_{t-1}|, \\ &|L_t - C_{t-1}|\big) \end{aligned} \]

The average true range (ATR) smooths it the Wilder way over 14 bars, \(\text{ATR}_t = (13\,\text{ATR}_{t-1} + \text{TR}_t)/14\). Take a bar with a high of 5,950 and a low of 5,890 after a close of 5,960. Its true range is 70, the fall from the previous close to the low, not the 60 of the bar itself, and it lifts an ATR of 55 to \((13 \times 55 + 70)/14 = 56.07\).

The average directional index (ADX) measures how one-sided recent moves have been. The up-move \(H_t - H_{t-1}\) counts as \(+\text{DM}_t\) when it is positive and larger than the down-move \(L_{t-1} - L_t\), and \(+\text{DM}_t\) is zero otherwise. The down-move counts as \(-\text{DM}_t\) by the mirror rule. Smoothing \(\text{TR}\), \(+\text{DM}\) and \(-\text{DM}\) the Wilder way over 14 bars gives

\[ {+\text{DI}} = 100\,\frac{+\text{DM}}{\text{TR}}, \quad {-\text{DI}} = 100\,\frac{-\text{DM}}{\text{TR}} \]
\[ \text{DX} = 100\,\frac{|{+\text{DI}} - {-\text{DI}}|}{{+\text{DI}} + {-\text{DI}}} \]

ADX is the Wilder average of DX over 14 bars. With smoothed values of 60 for TR, 18 for \(+\)DM and 6 for \(-\)DM, the indicators are 30 and 10, and DX is \(100 \times 20/40 = 50\). An ADX of 22 then moves to \((13 \times 22 + 50)/14 = 24.0\), inside this setup's window.

Chapter 11E's normalized slope divides the 30-bar SMA's rise per bar over the last five bars by the ATR. With the SMA at 5,900 now and 5,880 five bars ago, and an ATR of 60, it is \((20/5)/60 = 0.067\), above +0.05, so Stage 2's slope condition holds.

Common failure mode. The setup fires with the composite between +10 and +30, a mediocre regime, and the price rolls over rather than bouncing. The mitigation is to respect the composite filter strictly.

3 · Setup 2: compression breakout

The Compression Breakout buys the first expansion out of a range that has been tightening.

Edge source. Range expansion after a contraction in volatility reflects supply that has been absorbed, the mechanism of the volatility contraction pattern (VCP) in Chapter 11D. In positive-GEX regimes, the Dealer Mechanics Lab adds, dealers short the upside hedge into the breakout. Because they are short calls above the range, they must buy as the price rises through them, which accelerates the continuation. Chapter 8B's regimes pull the other way on average, since positive aggregate GEX damps moves. The mechanism therefore rests on the calls dealers are short just above the range rather than on the aggregate sign.

RuleSetting
RegimeA composite of +30 or more. ADX falling to 15 to 18 or below, a range contracting. Volume contracting to 70% to 90% of its 20-day average during the base. VIX below 18. Positive GEX
TriggerThe price breaks above the range high or an identified VCP pivot, on volume of at least 130% of its 20-day average. An entry up to 0.1% above the pivot is acceptable; do not chase further
ExecutionEnter on the breakout bar with a marketable limit order
VehicleEquity directly for the cleanest execution, or a bull call spread as a defined-risk option expression
SizingRisk 1% of the portfolio, with the stop at the midpoint of the prior range, typically 1% to 2% below the breakout level. Notional often approaches the portfolio's size for equity, and the premium bounds it for options
TargetThe range's width added to the breakout level, a measured move, or 2R, whichever comes first
Time stop8 trading days. With no follow-through in that window, the breakout was likely fake
InvalidationA close back inside the prior range within 2 sessions, or a close below the range's midpoint
Worked example: A breakout from a 12-point range

Step 1: the base. SPY has traded between 590.00 and 602.00, with ADX drifting down to 15, volume at 80% of its 20-day average and the VIX at 16. The composite reads +45.

Step 2: the trigger. SPY breaks above 602.00 on volume of 145% of its average. The highest acceptable entry is \(602.00 \times 1.001 = 602.60\), and a marketable limit fills at 602.50.

Step 3: stop and size. The stop sits at the range's midpoint, 596.00, 1.0% below the breakout level, so R is 6.50 a share. A $3,000 budget buys \(\lfloor 3{,}000/6.50 \rfloor = 461\) shares, $277,753 of notional, 93% of a $300,000 portfolio.

Step 4: the targets. The measured move is \(602.00 + 12.00 = 614.00\), and 2R is \(602.50 + 13.00 = 615.50\). The measured move comes first, so it is the target, with 8 trading days to reach it.

Common failure mode. Fakeout breakouts on weak volume, which reverse within 2 days. The mitigation is to enforce the volume rule strictly. A breakout on volume below 100% of its average is statistically more often a fakeout than a continuation.

The figure draws the worked breakout with its whole plan. Drag the breakout volume below 130%, or the fill past 602.60, and the trade disappears. Then choose what the price does next, and watch which exit closes the trade.

4 · Setup 3: undercut and reclaim

The Undercut and Reclaim buys a failed breakdown through a level that everyone is watching.

Edge source. Stop-loss orders cluster below well-known technical levels, such as the 200-day moving average or prior swing lows. When the price breaks the level, those stops trigger and accelerate the decline. When the move fails to follow through and reverses, the short sellers who opened on the breakdown are forced into short covering. The reclaim above the level signals that demand has overwhelmed supply.

RuleSetting
RegimeA composite of +10 or more, since this setup is less regime-sensitive than the Trend Pullback. The index or single name in Stage 2, or moving from Stage 1 to Stage 2. A well-defined level under test: the 200-day moving average, a prior swing low or a major round number
TriggerThe intraday low penetrates the well-known level and the day closes back above it. Confirmation: volume on the reclaim bar above 110% of its average
ExecutionEnter at the next day's open if pre-market trading holds the level, or intraday once the level has held through the morning session
VehicleEquity directly for single names, XSP or SPY for the index
SizingRisk 0.5% to 1% of the portfolio, lower than the Trend Pullback because of single-name execution friction. Notional often runs 20 to 50 times the dollar risk for clean equity trades in the Mag-7
TargetThe next overhead resistance level, or 1.5R, whichever comes first
Time stop3 trading days
InvalidationA close below the undercut low, which rejects the reclaim
Worked example: Undercutting the 200-day average

Step 1: the trigger. Suppose AAPL's 200-day moving average is at $170.00. The stock trades down to $167.50 intraday, closes at $171.20, and prints 125% of its average volume.

Step 2: stop and size. The entry is the next open, $171.50, and the stop is a close below the $167.50 undercut low, $4.00 or 2.3% away. Risking 0.75% of $300,000, or $2,250, buys 562 shares, $96,383 of notional, 43 times the dollar risk.

Step 3: the target. 1.5R is \(171.50 + 1.5 \times 4.00 = 177.50\), unless overhead resistance comes first, with 3 trading days to get there.

Common failure mode. Several undercuts in a row, as the level becomes a magnet for further declines. The mitigation: if a setup undercuts a major level for a second time within 5 sessions, skip it, because the level is becoming a ceiling, not a floor.

5 · Setup 4: IBS and RSI mean reversion

IBS and RSI Mean Reversion buys a day that closes weak after a short slide.

Edge source. Combining intraday positioning, measured by IBS, with a multi-day oscillator, RSI(2), captures different aspects of selling pressure. IBS reflects today's close relative to today's range, while RSI(2) reflects a multi-day extreme of momentum. The Dealer Mechanics Lab argues that their confluence is genuine, not multicollinear.

RuleSetting
RegimeA composite of +20 or more. Stage 2 confirmed. Positive GEX above the flip. VIX below 22
TriggerA daily close with both IBS below 0.20, a close in the bottom 20% of the day's range, and RSI(2) below 15
ExecutionEnter at the next day's open
VehicleQQQ or SPY shares for the cleanest execution, or XSP if an option expression is preferred
SizingRisk 1% of the portfolio. The tight stop at the intraday low, typically 0.5% below entry, makes the risk per share small and the position large
TargetThe first close above the entry day's high, or 1R, whichever comes first
Time stop2 trading days
InvalidationA close below the entry day's intraday low
Worked example: A small stop and a large position

Step 1: the trigger. QQQ trades between 512.00 and 520.00 and closes at 513.20, so by its IBS is \(1.20/8.00 = 0.15\). RSI(2) is 12, the composite +25, the trend Stage 2 and the VIX 17.

Step 2: stop and size. The entry is the next open at 513.50, with the stop 0.5% below at 510.93, so \(d = 2.57\). A 1% budget of $3,000 buys 1,167 shares.

Step 3: the notional. The position is worth $599,255, 200% of a $300,000 portfolio, which is leveraged. Under Reg-T margin that is all the overnight buying power the account has (Chapter 2E), and an opening gap below the stop costs more than the $3,000 planned. Verify margin and gap risk before sizing this aggressively.

This setup has a high win rate but a small payoff per trade, so it lives on its win rate. By Chapter 1C, the breakeven win rate is the average loss plus costs, divided by the sum of the average win and the average loss. If wins average 0.8R and losses 1R, the breakeven is \(1/1.8 = 55.6\%\). Costs of 0.1R a trade raise it to \(1.1/1.8 = 61.1\%\).

Common failure mode. Continuation lower if the regime is genuinely deteriorating, with the composite already weakening. The mitigation is to require a composite of +20 or more strictly.

Are IBS and RSI(2) two signals or one?

The Dealer Mechanics Lab is of two minds about this pair. This setup, and Chapter 13D's worked example of it, call the pair genuine confluence, because IBS measures a single day and RSI(2) several. Chapter 11C lists RSI(2) and IBS among indicators that all compute the same latent quantity: how stretched the price is against its recent range. It warns that counting them twice is multicollinearity, and Chapter 11N repeats the warning. Both positions are the source's own.

A day that closes at its low after a fall pushes both readings down at once, so the two are at least correlated. How correlated they are is an empirical question about your instrument. Before treating them as two votes, measure the correlation between IBS and RSI(2) over your own history, and ask how often one fires without the other.

Familiar ground

The question is the one you ask before chaining two diagnostic tests. Multiplying their likelihood ratios is valid only when the tests are conditionally independent, given the disease. Two assays that measure the same analyte do not double the evidence: the second adds little once the first is known. IBS and RSI(2) both read how stretched the price is, so their joint firing is weaker evidence than two independent tests would give.

6 · The four setups side by side

The four archetypes cover the conditional swing-trading opportunity surface across three different mechanisms:

SetupMechanismBest regimeTypical hold
Trend PullbackMean reversion in a trendStrong bull, composite +30 or more2 to 5 days
Compression BreakoutRange expansionStrong bull, low VIX5 to 8 days
Undercut and ReclaimShort-covering dynamicsAny positive regime2 to 3 days

Together they capture the high-probability surface for long swing trades in positive-regime environments. They do not cover short trades, which need Stage 4 or a negative regime. Nor do they cover long-volatility trades, which need an imminent event or extremely low IV. Multi-week positions driven by a thesis are out too, since they need a fundamental view and not just a technical setup. Chapter 11I takes up pre-earnings momentum, which does lie within this surface, and Chapter 11K the catalyst overlays.

7 · How strong the evidence is

For the Trend Pullback, the Dealer Mechanics Lab grades Connors' RSI(2) parameterization Tier B on the scale of Chapter 11B: practitioner work published in trade books, not in peer-reviewed journals. The underlying short-horizon mean reversion has academic support in Lo and MacKinlay (1990) and in Jegadeesh (1990), the reversal effect. The specific threshold, below 10, and the lookback are calibrated by practitioners. Treat the signal as practitioner-grade with an academic underlying, not as a Tier A result.

The Dealer Mechanics Lab grades it differently elsewhere. Chapter 13B's scale for the evidence factor lists "Connors RSI(2) mean reversion in trending markets" among its Tier 1 examples, scored 0.8 to 1.0, and scores this setup's evidence at 0.85. Both gradings are the Dealer Mechanics Lab's own, and only the cautious one comes with its reasons: the practitioner calibration described above.

The Edge Book files short-term reversal over 1 to 5 days under microstructure and flow: pay for providing liquidity after sharp moves (Chapter 1G). It is more doubtful than the Dealer Mechanics Lab that a retail account can capture short-horizon mean reversion at all, and Chapter 11B sets the two positions side by side.

Three of the setups require positive GEX, and two lean on dealer hedging for their mechanism. The Edge Book treats the sign of GEX as low-confidence context, worth 10% to 15% of one filter's weight. It ignores the sign when it disagrees with the VIX term structure or the realized-volatility filters (Chapter 10H). It rejects trading at specific GEX levels outright for retail accounts (Chapter 10I). On its reading, the GEX conditions in these tables deserve a small weight, and the stories about dealer flow a smaller one.

The other three setups cite no study. Chapter 11B places VCP detection among its Tier C examples, practitioner pattern language that you must formalize and test yourself.

In practice
  • Choose the vehicle for tax as well as fit. XSP options are Section 1256 contracts, taxed 60% long-term whatever the holding period, while gains on SPY and QQQ held for days are short-term and subject to the wash-sale rule (Chapter 2E).
  • Check the notional against buying power and gaps. The mean-reversion setups' tight stops produce positions of 100% to 300% of the portfolio, which is leverage, and a stop does not cap an opening gap.
  • Count correlated setups as one. A Trend Pullback in SPY and an undercut in a Mag-7 stock are largely the same bet. Chapter 11L sizes such positions against a shared budget.
  • Match the option's expiry to the hold. Chapter 11M gives each setup its days to expiry: 14 to 30 for the Trend Pullback, 21 to 45 for the breakout, 14 to 21 for the undercut, and shares or 7 to 14 for IBS and RSI.
  • Work from a written card. Chapter 13F gives one page per setup. A setup taken with one condition missing, "close enough", is the setup creep of Chapter 11N.
When it fails
  • A mediocre regime. Each setup's own failure mode is a regime weaker than it looks. The Trend Pullback fires in a composite of +10 to +30, the breakout comes on thin volume, the undercut becomes a magnet and the mean reversion meets a deteriorating market.
  • A gap through the stop. Stops sized at 0.5% to 1% are small against a single overnight gap, and the loss is set by where the market opens, not by where the stop was.
  • A regime change mid-trade. A composite that falls through a bucket edge after entry does not close the trade by itself. The invalidation and the time stop do.
Decision rule
  • Regime first: check every condition in the setup's regime row, and take no trigger in a regime that fails one.
  • Trigger on the close: act only on the defined trigger at the daily close, then execute as the setup specifies.
  • Size from the stop: set the risk budget first and derive the size from , then check the notional against buying power and gap risk.
  • Pre-commit the exits: write down the target, the time stop and the invalidation before entry, and close at whichever comes first.
Common mistake: sizing 1% as 1% of the portfolio in notional

Buying $3,000 of SPY on a $300,000 portfolio with a 0.75% stop risks $22.50, about 1/133 of the intended loss, so the setup's edge never shows up in the account. The reverse error, buying a portfolio's worth of notional and forgetting the stop, turns a 5% adverse move into five times the planned loss (Chapter 13C).

Common mistake: loosening the regime to catch a clean trigger

A textbook RSI(2) reading in a composite of +15 looks like the setup and is not one. The regime conditions are part of the setup's definition, since the edge exists only where they hold.

What to carry forward
  • A setup specifies its edge, regime, trigger, execution, sizing, target, time stop and invalidation before the trade, so that nothing is decided in the moment.
  • Size from the stop: \(n = \lfloor B/(d\,m) \rfloor\). Tight stops mean large notional, often more than the portfolio.
  • Two setups, the Trend Pullback and IBS and RSI Mean Reversion, are mean reversion in a strong trend, while the Compression Breakout is range expansion and the Undercut and Reclaim is forced short covering.
  • All four are long trades for positive regimes, and all four depend on the regime conditions that open their tables.
  • The evidence is practitioner-grade. The Edge Book gives the GEX conditions low confidence and doubts that retail accounts can capture short-horizon mean reversion.

8 · Check yourself

Question 1

A $150,000 account risks 1.5% on a Trend Pullback in SPY at $590, with the stop 0.8% below entry. How many shares, and what notional?

Answer

The budget is $2,250 and the stop distance $4.72, so gives \(\lfloor 2{,}250/4.72 \rfloor = 476\) shares. They cost $280,840, 187% of the account, which is within Reg-T's overnight limit of twice the equity but leaves little room.

Question 2

A day's bar has a high of 450, a low of 440 and a close of 441.50, with RSI(2) at 18. Which setups can it trigger?

Answer

IBS is \(1.50/10 = 0.15\), below 0.20. The IBS and RSI setup needs RSI(2) below 15 as well, so it does not fire. The Trend Pullback needs only one of the two readings, so it can fire if the bar pulled back to its 10- or 21-EMA and every regime condition holds.

Question 3

SPY breaks out of a tight base on volume of 95% of its 20-day average. Do you buy?

Answer

No. The Compression Breakout needs volume of at least 130% of the average, and a breakout below 100% is statistically more often a fakeout than a continuation.

Question 4

A stock undercut its 200-day average and reclaimed it three sessions ago, and today it undercuts the same level again. What does the Undercut and Reclaim setup say?

Answer

Skip it. A second undercut of a major level within 5 sessions means the level is becoming a ceiling rather than a floor.

Question 5

Why must the IBS and RSI setup win more often than the Trend Pullback to break even?

Answer

Its target is only 1R or the first close above the entry day's high, so its wins are small against its losses. With wins of 0.8R and losses of 1R it needs 55.6% to break even before costs. A setup targeting 2R needs only \(1/3 = 33.3\%\) with the same 1R losses.
