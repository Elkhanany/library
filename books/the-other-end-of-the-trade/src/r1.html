Glossary

Every term the book defines

Every term the book defines, in alphabetical order, with the chapter that defines it. In the chapters, a marked term shows its definition when you hover over it or tap it, and this page lists them all in one place.

A definition here is the short form. The chapter linked after it gives the full one, with the numbers and the caveats.

0-9

0DTE. Options with zero days to expiry: options traded on the day they expire. Chapter 9B

10-year Treasury note future. A futures contract on US government notes with about ten years to maturity, traded under the ticker ZN. Its price rises when longer-term interest rates fall. Chapter 10G

60/40 portfolio. A portfolio holding 60% of its value in stocks and 40% in bonds, rebalanced back to those weights from time to time. Chapter 1G

A

acceleration clause. A term of an exchange-traded product that lets its issuer end the product early once its value falls past a stated threshold. Chapter 8H

accumulation. In trading, the gradual buying of a large position by institutions over weeks or months, which shows on a chart as a base in which the swings narrow. Chapter 11E

advance-decline ratio. The number of stocks that rose over a period divided by the number that fell. A high reading means the move was broad; a low one that most stocks fell. Chapter 11G

adverse selection. The loss from trading with someone who knows more than you do, so that the trades you get are the ones that are bad for you. Chapter 1G

agent-based model. A simulation in which many traders follow simple rules and prices emerge from their orders, used to study what a type of trader does to a market when real data cannot isolate it. Chapter 8D

aggregate vega. The total change in a portfolio's value for a rise of one point in implied volatility, found by adding every position's vega in dollars, each scaled by its underlying's VIX beta. It measures how much a whole book loses when volatility rises. Chapter 13H

air pocket. A vendor's name for a band of strikes where estimated dealer gamma is small, so that dealer hedging there neither damps nor amplifies moves much. Chapter 8C

allocation. How much capital, or how much of the portfolio's risk, each strategy is given. Chapter 1F

alpha. Return beyond what a model's risk factors explain, often claimed as the reward for a manager's skill. Chapter 1D

alpha capital. Capital a trading firm commits to its own directional or relative-value views. Market makers run little of it compared with their risk capital. Chapter 6A

alternative trading system. The regulatory name for a trading venue that is not a registered exchange, such as a dark pool. Abbreviated ATS. Chapter 2B

ambiguity aversion. A dislike of not knowing the probabilities themselves, over and above the risk that known probabilities describe. An investor averse to ambiguity values an uncertain premium below its point estimate. Chapter 10C

American-style option. An option that can be exercised at any time up to expiry. US-listed options on single stocks and exchange-traded funds, such as SPY, are American-style. Chapter 5A

anti-cross-pinning. Golez and Jackwerth's name for the push of S&P 500 futures away from the at-the-money strike just before SPX options expire, the opposite of pinning. Chapter 8E

AR(1) model. An autoregressive model of order one: it predicts each period's value, such as a return, as a weight times the previous period's value plus a constant. The sign of the weight separates mean reversion from momentum. Chapter 3B

arbitrage. A profit made without bearing risk, by exploiting a difference between prices that should agree. Chapter 1G

arithmetic mean. The ordinary average of a set of returns: their sum divided by how many there are. Over time it overstates what an investment compounds to. Chapter 1A

Arrow-Debreu security. A security that pays one unit in a single future state of the world and nothing in any other. Its price is that state's price. Chapter 4C

assignment. What happens to the seller of an option when its holder exercises it: the seller must buy or sell the underlying, or pay the cash difference, at the strike. Chapter 12H

at-the-money. Said of an option whose strike is at, or very close to, the underlying's current price. Chapter 5A

autocallable. A structured note that pays a coupon and is redeemed early if the index is above a set level on a check date, and exposes its holder to losses if the index falls through a lower barrier. The issuing bank hedges it by trading the index and its options. Chapter 8B

autocorrelation. The correlation of a series with its own earlier values. The lag-1 autocorrelation of returns is the correlation between each period's return and the one before. Chapter 3B

average daily volume. The number of shares or contracts of a security that trade on a typical day, averaged over a recent period. Abbreviated ADV. Chapter 2D

average directional index. Wilder's measure of trend strength from 0 to 100, regardless of direction: a smoothed average of how one-sided the recent up and down moves have been. Abbreviated ADX. Below 20 is conventionally no trend and above 25 a strong one. Chapter 11C

average true range. The true range averaged over n bars, usually 14, with Wilder smoothing: the typical size of a bar, in price units. Abbreviated ATR. Chapter 11C

B

backtest. A test of a trading rule on historical data, showing what the rule would have earned had it been followed. Chapter 3A

backwardation. A term structure in which shorter-dated prices or implied volatilities sit above longer-dated ones. For equity implied volatility it is a mark of stress. Chapter 5E

base rate. How often an outcome happens with nothing special going on, such as how often a level at the same distance from the price holds when it is not a wall. A hit rate means something only against its base rate. Chapter 8J

basis point. One hundredth of a percentage point, so 20 basis points is 0.20%. Abbreviated bp, plural bps. Chapter 1E

beta. A measure of how much an asset moves with the market. A stock with a beta of 1.5 moves, on average, 1.5% for each 1% move in the market. Chapter 1G

betting against beta. A strategy that holds low-beta stocks against high-beta ones. Its premise is that investors unable to borrow bid up high-beta stocks to get more market exposure. Chapter 10F

bias-variance tradeoff. The balance between two sources of error in an estimate: bias, being consistently wrong in one direction, and variance, the noise that comes from a finite sample. Complex models cut bias and add variance. Chapter 3A

bid-ask bounce. The alternation of the last trade price between the bid and the ask as buyers and sellers arrive in turn. It adds noise and false mean reversion to any series of last prices. Chapter 2C

bid-ask spread. The gap between the highest price a buyer offers, the bid, and the lowest price a seller asks, the ask. Crossing it is the immediate cost of trading. Chapter 1F

binding constraint. In a score built as a product of factors, the lowest factor: the one holding the whole product down, and so the first thing to fix or the reason to pass. Chapter 13B

Black-Scholes model. The option pricing model, also called Black-Scholes-Merton, that values an option by assuming the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion and can be traded continuously to hedge it. Chapter 7A

block trade. A single large trade, arranged in one piece and reported at once rather than worked through the order book in small orders. Chapter 11A

Bollinger Bands. Two lines drawn two standard deviations above and below a 20-bar simple moving average of the price, so that the band widens when prices are volatile and narrows when they are calm. Chapter 11C

Bollinger bandwidth. The distance between the upper and lower Bollinger Bands divided by the middle band. A low reading, called a squeeze, marks unusually calm prices. Chapter 11C

book-to-market ratio. The book value of a company's equity, from its accounts, divided by its market value. A high ratio marks a stock the market prices cheaply. Abbreviated B/M. Chapter 10F

borrow cost. The fee a short seller pays to borrow the shares sold, quoted as an annual rate. It can be very high for stocks in a short squeeze, and it makes puts dearer relative to calls. Chapter 5F

box spread. A combination of four options whose value at expiry is fixed whatever the underlying does. Selling one is a way to borrow money at the interest rate implied by its price. Chapter 12E

breadth. How many of an index's members take part in its move, often measured as the share of them trading above their own 50-day moving average. A rally on narrow breadth rests on a few large stocks. Chapter 11A

breakeven. The price of the underlying at expiry at which a position's profit or loss is zero. For a call, bought or sold, it is the strike plus the premium; for a put, the strike minus the premium. Chapter 5A

breakeven inflation rate. The yield on an ordinary Treasury bond minus the yield on the inflation-protected Treasury of the same maturity: the average inflation rate at which the two would pay the same. Chapter 11G

breakeven win rate. The win rate at which a bet's expected value is exactly zero: the average loss plus costs, divided by the sum of the average win and the average loss. Chapter 1C

breakout. A move of the price beyond a level that has held it, such as the top of a trading range or of a chart pattern, taken as the start of a new advance. Chapter 11D

broad-based index. For the Section 1256 test, loosely, an index of 10 or more securities in which no single one exceeds 30% of the weight. Options on such an index can qualify for 60/40 tax treatment. Chapter 2E

broken-wing butterfly. A butterfly spread with one wing wider than the other, often opened for a net credit, which removes the risk on one side and leaves a larger loss beyond the wider wing. Chapter 12D

broker call rate. The interest rate banks charge brokers for short-term loans that fund their customers' margin borrowing. Brokers set the margin rates they charge customers above it. Chapter 12E

Brownian bridge. A random path that is made to end at a given value at a given time, such as a price path forced to finish at a strike at expiry. It describes a pin without saying what causes one. Chapter 8E

Brownian motion. A random path that starts at zero, moves continuously, and whose change over any interval is normally distributed with mean zero and variance equal to the interval's length, independent of its changes over other intervals. It is the limit of a coin-flip random walk as the steps become small. Chapter 7A

bull flag. A sharp advance on heavy volume, the flagpole, followed by a brief and shallow drift lower on light volume, the flag. A move above the flag's high is the breakout. Chapter 11D

butterfly spread. A three-strike position, all calls or all puts, that buys one option at each outer strike and sells two at the middle strike, for a small net debit. It profits most if the underlying finishes at the middle strike. Chapter 12C

buying power. The value of new positions that a margin account can open, given its equity and the broker's margin requirements. Chapter 2E

C

calendar spread. A position that sells an option at one expiry and buys an option of the same type and strike at a later expiry, profiting from how the two re-price over time. Chapter 12C

call credit spread. A position that sells a call and buys a call with a higher strike on the same underlying and expiry, for a net credit. It profits if the underlying stays below the sold strike, and its loss is capped by the bought call. Also called a bear call spread. Chapter 12C

call debit spread. A position that buys a call and sells a call with a higher strike on the same underlying and expiry, for a net payment. It profits if the underlying rises, with both its gain and its loss capped. Also called a bull call spread or a long call spread. Chapter 12C

call option. A contract giving its holder the right, not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike price on or before expiry. Chapter 5A

call wall. The strike at which a gamma exposure model places the largest concentration of call gamma. Some traders read one above the price as resistance. Chapter 8C

Calmar ratio. The mean annual return divided by the size of the maximum drawdown: return per unit of the worst peak-to-trough loss. Chapter 1D

capacity. The amount of capital a strategy can absorb before its own trading erodes its edge. Chapter 1G

CAPE ratio. The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio: an index's price divided by the average of its last ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. High values have historically preceded lower 10-year returns. Chapter 10B

capital asset pricing model. The textbook model in which a stock's expected return above the risk-free rate equals its beta times the market's expected return above that rate. Abbreviated CAPM. Chapter 10F

carry. The return an asset or a futures position is expected to earn if prices do not change: roughly what holding the asset yields, less what financing it costs. High-carry assets include high-interest currencies and commodities in backwardation. Chapter 10E

carry trade. A position that earns the gap in yield between two assets, such as borrowing in a low-interest currency to lend in a high-interest one. Chapter 10E

cash account. A brokerage account that trades only with settled cash and lends nothing, so it can never receive a margin call. Options sold in it must be covered by shares or by cash. Chapter 2E

cash-secured put. A sold put option backed by enough cash to buy the underlying at the strike if it is assigned. Nothing is borrowed, so it cannot receive a margin call. Chapter 12B

cash settlement. Settling an option or future by paying the in-the-money amount in cash instead of delivering the underlying. SPX options are cash-settled. Chapter 5A

CBOE PUT Index. A Cboe benchmark that tracks a strategy of selling at-the-money SPX puts each month, fully backed by cash. It has been tracked since June 1986. Chapter 1B

charm. The rate at which an option's delta changes as time passes, with the underlying's price and implied volatility unchanged. It runs on calendar time, weekends included. Chapter 5D

charm exposure. The sum, across strikes and expiries, of the charm of dealers' estimated positions: how much their delta changes, in dollars, as one calendar day passes with price and implied volatility unchanged. Abbreviated CHEX. Chapter 8F

chart pattern. A shape in a price series, such as a cup and handle or a flag, defined by geometric rules and read as a sign of how large holders are buying or selling beneath the surface. Chapter 11D

circuit breaker. A rule that halts trading, across the whole market or in a single stock, after a large and fast price move. Chapter 2A

classification model. A model that predicts which of a finite set of categories an outcome falls into, such as up or down, usually with a probability for each. Chapter 3A

clearinghouse. An organization that stands between the two sides of every cleared trade, so that each side's counterparty is the clearinghouse rather than the other trader. Chapter 4A

close-to-close estimator. The simplest estimate of realized volatility: the average squared log return from one day's close to the next. It ignores everything that happens within each day. Chapter 5E

closing auction. The process in the last minutes of the American trading day in which the exchanges gather orders and fill them all at one price, the official close. Chapter 2C

CNDR index. Cboe's S&P 500 Iron Condor Index, a benchmark that tracks a strategy of selling SPX iron condors systematically. Its record, from a backtest starting in 1986 and live values since 2007, lags the S&P 500 by a wide margin. Chapter 12C

collar. A position that holds shares, buys a put below their price and sells a call above it, often at no net cost, so that both the loss and the gain on the shares are capped. Chapter 12C

color. The rate at which an option's gamma changes as time passes. It is positive near the strike, where gamma grows toward expiry, and negative far from it, where gamma drains away. Chapter 5D

commodity trading advisor. The US regulatory term for a manager who trades futures for clients, including managers of trend-following funds that trade far more than commodities. Abbreviated CTA. Chapter 10D

complete market. A market in which any payoff across the possible future states can be built from the securities that trade. It needs as many independent securities as there are states. Chapter 4C

composite signal. A single regime score from minus 100 to plus 100, built as a weighted average of eight dimensions of market state such as volatility, credit and trend. It permits and sizes swing trades rather than triggering them. Chapter 11G

compounding. The multiplying of growth factors over successive periods, so that each period's return is earned on the gains and losses of the periods before it. Chapter 1A

concave function. A function whose graph bends downward, such as the logarithm. The average of its values at two points lies below its value at their average. Chapter 1A

conditional drawdown at risk. The average of the drawdowns beyond a chosen percentile, such as the worst 5% of them. It describes the tail of drawdowns rather than the single worst one. Chapter 1E

confirmation bias. The tendency to seek out and favor evidence that supports what you already believe, and to discount evidence against it. Chapter 11N

Consumer Price Index. A monthly index of the prices American consumers pay for a basket of goods and services, and a headline measure of US inflation. Its release is a scheduled market event. Abbreviated CPI. Chapter 11K

contango. A term structure in which longer-dated prices or implied volatilities sit above shorter-dated ones. It is the usual shape for equity implied volatility. Chapter 5E

continuous compounding. Growth added in infinitely small, infinitely frequent amounts. At a rate r for t years, one dollar grows to e raised to the power r times t. Chapter 1A

contract multiplier. The number that converts an option's quoted price into dollars per contract: 100 for US stock and ETF options, and 100 dollars per index point for SPX options. Chapter 5A

convenience yield. The benefit of holding a physical commodity rather than a contract to receive it later, such as being able to use it during a shortage. Chapter 10E

conversion. An arbitrage that buys the underlying, sells a call and buys a put at one strike and expiry, locking in the strike at expiry. It profits only when the call is overpriced against the put by put-call parity. Chapter 12E

convex function. A function whose graph is bowl-shaped, so that the straight line between any two of its points lies on or above it. A convex loss has one minimum, which gradient descent finds from any start. Chapter 3C

convexity correction. The half-sigma-squared term in the Black-Scholes d1. It accounts for the mean of a lognormal price sitting above its median, and it lifts an at-the-money call's delta above one half. Chapter 6B

conviction score. A pre-trade test that multiplies five scores from 0 to 1, for evidence, mechanism, regime, execution and sizing survivability. A product of 0.5 or more is high conviction, and below 0.3 the trade is not taken. Chapter 13B

correlation. A number from minus 1 to plus 1 measuring how closely two series move together: their covariance divided by the product of their standard deviations. Chapter 1F

cost basis. The purchase price of a position for tax purposes, from which the taxable gain or loss on its sale is measured. Chapter 2E

cost of carry. The cost of holding the underlying until a future date, financing less any dividends received, plus storage for a commodity. It sets the gap between the futures price and the spot price. Chapter 4B

counterparty risk. The risk that the other side of a contract fails to pay what it owes. For exchange-listed options it is carried by the clearinghouse; for over-the-counter contracts it stays with each side. Chapter 5F

covariance. The average product of two variables' deviations from their means. It is positive when they tend to move the same way and negative when they tend to move opposite ways. Chapter 1F

covariance matrix. A square table holding each asset's variance on its diagonal and each pair's covariance off it. With the portfolio's weights, it gives the portfolio's variance. Chapter 1F

covered call. A sold call option backed by owning the shares it may require you to deliver. Chapter 12B

credit default swap. A contract in which one side pays a regular premium and the other pays out if a named borrower defaults, so its price quotes that borrower's credit risk directly. A CDX index bundles many of them. Chapter 11A

credit spread. A spread of options sold for a net receipt, which is the most it can make. The bought option or options cap its loss. Chapter 12C

cross-validation. Testing a model by fitting it on part of the data and scoring it on a part it was not fitted to, rotating through the data. The standard k-fold version splits the data at random, which leaks the future into training when the data are a time series. Chapter 3A

cup and handle. O'Neil's base: a rounded decline and recovery lasting weeks to months, the cup, followed by a shorter and shallower pullback, the handle, whose high is the buy point. Chapter 11D

D

dark pool. A trading venue that does not display its orders, run by a bank or broker. Its trades fill at or inside the public best bid and offer, often at the midpoint. Chapter 2B

days to expiry. The number of calendar days until an option expires, abbreviated DTE. An option with zero days to expiry expires today. Chapter 5B

debit spread. A spread of options bought for a net payment. The most it can lose is that payment. Chapter 12C

defined-outcome ETF. An exchange-traded fund that uses FLEX options to give its holders capped upside and partial protection against losses over a fixed period. Chapter 4A

deflated Sharpe ratio. The probabilistic Sharpe ratio tested against the best Sharpe ratio that luck alone would produce across all the strategies tried, which corrects a backtest for selection. Chapter 1D

delta. The change in an option's value for a one-dollar move in the underlying. A 30-delta option gains or loses about 30 cents for each dollar the underlying moves. Chapter 5C

delta hedging. Holding the underlying in the amount that offsets a position's delta, so that small moves in the underlying's price leave the combined position's value unchanged. The hedge must be reset as delta changes. Chapter 6C

demand-based option pricing. The theory that end users' net demand for options moves their prices, because dealers who cannot hedge perfectly charge for the risk left in the positions they absorb. It helps explain why index puts are expensive and the skew is steep. Chapter 8B

derivative. The slope of a curve at a point: how fast one quantity changes per unit change in another, for a very small change. At the lowest point of a smooth curve it is zero. Chapter 3C

destabilizing gamma. The negation of published gamma exposure, used in the effective-volatility formula: positive when dealers are net short gamma and their hedging amplifies moves, negative when they are net long gamma and it damps them. Chapter 8D

diagonal spread. A position that sells an option at one expiry and buys an option of the same type at a later expiry and a different strike. It is a calendar spread with the strikes moved apart. Chapter 12C

difference-in-differences. A design that compares the change in an outcome in a group that received a treatment with the change in a group that did not, over the same period. Chapter 8G

discretionary sleeve. The part of a portfolio in which a trader chooses each trade by judgment within a written framework, such as swing trades. The halts and conviction tests of a trading framework apply to it. Chapter 13A

dispersion. How widely the returns of an index's members spread around their average over a period, measured as their standard deviation across the members. High dispersion with a flat index means the members are moving, but in different directions. Chapter 11L

disposition effect. The tendency to sell winning positions too early and to hold losing positions too long. Chapter 13A

distribution. In trading, the gradual selling of a large position by institutions into strength, which shows on a chart as a top where an advance stalls. Chapter 11E

dividend discount model. A valuation that sets a stock's or an index's price equal to the present value of all the cash it is expected to pay out. Run backward, it gives the return that today's price implies. Abbreviated DDM. Chapter 10B

dollar-cost averaging. Buying a fixed dollar amount of an investment at regular intervals rather than all at once, so that the entry is spread over time and no single day's price decides it. Chapter 13F

dollar gamma. Gamma expressed as the dollar value of the hedge trade a 1% move in the underlying forces: gamma times the price squared times 0.01, per share, then times the multiplier and the open interest. It lets positions on underlyings at different prices be compared. Chapter 6D

dollar-weighted return. The return investors actually earned, weighting each period by the money they had invested in it. It is the single yearly rate that grows every deposit to the ending value, and it falls below the time-weighted return when money arrives after gains and leaves after losses. Chapter 13A

Donchian channel. The band between the highest high and the lowest low of the last n bars. A close above the top of the previous n bars' channel is a new n-bar high, the classic breakout signal. Chapter 11C

drawdown. The fall in an account's value from its highest value so far to a later low, as a fraction of that peak. Chapter 1E

E

e. The mathematical constant 2.71828, the amount one dollar grows to in a year at 100% interest compounded continuously. It is the base of the natural logarithm. Chapter 1A

earnings guidance. A company's own forecast of its coming results, such as next quarter's revenue or earnings per share, usually issued with its earnings report. Chapter 11I

earnings per share. A company's profit for a period divided by the number of its shares. Abbreviated EPS, it is the headline number of an earnings report, compared with the analysts' consensus forecast. Chapter 11I

earnings yield. Earnings per dollar of price, the inverse of a price-to-earnings ratio. The inverse of the CAPE ratio is a cyclically adjusted earnings yield. Chapter 10B

edge. The expected value of a trade or strategy, net of all costs. A trade has edge only if that number is positive. Chapter 1C

effective spread. Twice the distance between a fill price and the midpoint of the bid and ask when the order arrived: what the order paid, on the same scale as a quoted spread. Chapter 2B

effective volatility. The volatility prices show once dealer hedging feeds back into them, as opposed to the volatility news alone would cause. Feedback from short-gamma hedging raises it and feedback from long-gamma hedging lowers it. Chapter 8D

efficient market hypothesis. The claim that prices already reflect available information. In its weakest form, past prices and returns cannot predict future returns. Chapter 3B

embedded leverage. The leverage built into an option, where a small premium controls a large exposure. Buyers pay for it in the option's price, a cost called embedded leverage drag. Chapter 1E

equity factor. A trait by which stocks can be ranked, such as profitability or past return, where the ranking has predicted returns. Its return is measured as that of the top-ranked stocks minus that of the bottom-ranked ones. Chapter 10F

equity premium puzzle. The finding, named by Mehra and Prescott in 1985, that the historical equity risk premium is too large to fit standard models of investors' utility without implausibly high aversion to risk. Chapter 10B

equity risk premium. The extra return that stocks are expected to pay over safe assets, as payment for bearing the risk that they fall when times are already bad. Chapter 10B

European-style option. An option that can be exercised only at expiry. US-listed index options such as SPX and XSP are European-style. Chapter 5A

evidence tier. A grade for the evidence behind an edge: T1 for peer-reviewed, replicated, multi-decade results, T2 for strong but methodology-sensitive results, and T3 for thin or decayed ones. Chapter 10A

excess kurtosis. Kurtosis minus 3, so that a normal distribution scores zero and a fat-tailed one scores above it. Chapter 1B

exchange-traded derivative. A derivative with standardized terms that trades on a registered exchange and clears through a central clearinghouse. Abbreviated ETD. Chapter 4A

exchange-traded fund. A fund whose shares trade on an exchange like a stock, usually built to track an index. Abbreviated ETF. Chapter 2A

exchange-traded note. An exchange-traded product built as a note rather than a fund, abbreviated ETN. The VIX products VXX and UVXY are ETNs, and they decay structurally. Chapter 9A

exchange-traded product. A security that trades on an exchange like a stock and tracks an index or a strategy. Exchange-traded funds and exchange-traded notes are both kinds. Abbreviated ETP. Chapter 10A

ex-dividend date. The first day on which a buyer of a stock no longer receives its next dividend. The share price typically drops by about the dividend that morning. Chapter 2A

exercise. To use the right an option gives: for a call, to buy the underlying at the strike price, and for a put, to sell it there. A cash-settled option is exercised by paying the in-the-money amount in cash. Chapter 5A

expected move. The move the options market prices for a period, read as the price of the at-the-money straddle for that period divided by the price of the underlying. It approximates the average absolute move, about 0.8 of a one-standard-deviation move. Chapter 11A

expected shortfall. The average return in the part of the distribution beyond the value at risk, such as the worst 5% of months. Also called conditional value at risk. Chapter 1B

expected utility. The probability-weighted average of the value a person places on each possible level of wealth. Maximizing it, rather than expected dollars, gives large losses the extra weight they deserve. Chapter 1C

expense ratio. A fund's yearly charge as a percentage of the money invested in it, taken out of the fund's returns. Chapter 10F

expiry. The date on which an option stops trading and is settled or lapses. Chapter 5A

exponential moving average. A moving average updated at each bar by moving a fixed fraction of the way from its last value toward the new price, so that recent prices count most. For an n-bar average the fraction is 2 divided by n plus 1. Abbreviated EMA. Chapter 11A

extrinsic value. The part of an option's premium above its intrinsic value, also called time value. It is the price of the time left and the chance of a favorable move before expiry, and it is largest at the money. Chapter 5B

F

factor tilt. A lean within the equity sleeve toward stocks with a chosen trait, such as quality or momentum, and away from pure market-capitalization weights. Chapter 10A

factor timing. Shifting weight between equity factors according to a forecast of which will do best next, rather than holding them at fixed weights. Chapter 10F

fat tails. A distribution has fat tails when extreme outcomes are much more likely than a normal distribution with the same mean and standard deviation would make them. Chapter 1B

feature. One of the inputs to a model: a number known at the time the prediction is made, such as yesterday's return or today's volume. Chapter 3A

federal funds rate. The interest rate at which US banks lend reserves to each other overnight, which the Federal Reserve targets as its main policy rate. Often shortened to fed funds. Chapter 12E

Federal Open Market Committee. The committee of the Federal Reserve that sets US short-term interest rates. It meets about eight times a year, roughly every six weeks. Abbreviated FOMC. Chapter 10G

Fed put. The market's belief that the Federal Reserve will respond to a sharp fall in stocks by easing policy, which limits the losses investors expect. Chapter 10G

financial derivative. A contract whose value is derived from the price of another asset, its underlying. Futures, forwards, swaps and options are all derivatives. Chapter 4A

FLEX option. An option listed on Cboe whose strike, expiration, exercise style and settlement the parties choose, cleared through the OCC like a standard listed option. Chapter 4A

floor function. The rule that rounds a number down to the nearest whole number: the floor of 2.5 is 2. Rounding a position size down keeps its loss within its budget. Chapter 13C

forced liquidation. The broker's closing of your positions at market prices when you do not meet a margin call in time. Chapter 1E

forward contract. A private agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date. It has the same payoff as a futures contract but is not standardized or exchange-traded. Chapter 4B

forward guidance. A central bank's public statements about the likely future path of its policy, meant to shape expectations between its meetings. Chapter 10I

forward price. The price agreed today for buying an asset on a set future date. For an index it is the spot price grown at the risk-free rate, less dividends, and it is the expected future price under the probabilities that price options. Chapter 9A

forward volatility. The implied volatility the market prices for the window between two expiries, found from the two expiries' implied volatilities because implied variance adds over time. Chapter 5G

foundation premium. A compensated risk premium that anchors a portfolio, such as the equity risk premium, the variance risk premium, trend following or cross-asset carry. It is sized for its crashes and sized down in adverse regimes, never switched off. Chapter 10A

fractional Kelly. Sizing at a fixed fraction of the Kelly bet, usually a half or a quarter. It gives up some long-run growth for much shallower drawdowns and a buffer against an overestimated edge. Chapter 3D

free cash flow. The cash a company's operations generate after the spending needed to maintain and expand its assets. Abbreviated FCF. Chapter 10F

front-running. Trading ahead of a flow you expect, so that you hold the position before the flow moves the price. In this book it means anticipating predictable public flows, such as mandated rebalancing, not the illegal use of a client's orders. Chapter 10G

futures basis. The difference between a futures price and the price of its underlying. For an index future it reflects interest and dividends until expiry, and it shrinks to zero at expiry. Chapter 2A

futures contract. An agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date. Only a deposit is paid up front, so a future gives exposure with leverage built in. Chapter 4B

G

gamma. The change in an option's delta for a one-dollar move in the underlying, which measures the curvature of the option's value. Bought options have positive gamma, largest at the money and near expiry. Chapter 5C

gamma exposure. An estimate of the total gamma that dealers hold across all strikes, and so of how much hedging they must do as the index moves. Abbreviated GEX. Chapter 8A

gamma flip. The level of the index at which estimated net dealer gamma crosses zero, so that dealer hedging damps moves on one side of it and amplifies them on the other. Vendors give it other names, and some define nearby levels of their own that are not the same thing. Chapter 8C

gamma scalping. Earning the curvature of a long option position by re-hedging its delta: selling the underlying after rises and buying after falls. It pays when the underlying moves more than the implied volatility paid for the option. Chapter 6D

gamma squeeze. A rally driven by dealers buying the underlying to hedge calls they have sold, whose deltas rise as the price rises, so that each purchase lifts the price and forces the next. Chapter 8H

gap risk. The risk that the underlying opens at a price well away from its previous close, so that a position held overnight or over a weekend takes the whole jump with no chance to trade out. Chapter 5F

Garman-Klass estimator. A realized-volatility estimate, published in 1980, that uses each day's open, high, low and close. It is more efficient than the Parkinson estimator but still misses overnight gaps. Chapter 5E

geometric Brownian motion. The standard model of a price moving in continuous time, under which log returns over any period are normally distributed and independent of the past. Chapter 7A

geometric mean. The constant return per period that would compound to the same final value as the actual sequence of returns. It is what compounding actually delivers. Chapter 1A

GEX heatmap. A grid of estimated dealer gamma exposure with strikes as rows and expiries as columns, each cell colored by its sign and size. The sign in each cell rests on the vendor's assumption about who holds the options. Chapter 8C

gradient. The list of slopes of a loss with respect to each of a model's parameters. It points in the direction in which the loss rises most steeply. Chapter 3C

gradient descent. A way to fit a model by repeatedly moving its parameters a small step downhill on the loss, in the direction opposite to the gradient. Chapter 3C

Greeks. The measures of how an option's value changes with the underlying's price, with time, with volatility and with interest rates: delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho. Chapter 5C

growth factor. What one dollar at the start of a period became by its end: one plus the simple return. A 20% gain is a growth factor of 1.20. Chapter 1A

H

Halloween effect. The pattern in which stocks have earned more from November to April than from May to October, also known as sell in May and go away. No clean mechanism for it has been found. Chapter 10G

hedge ratio. The amount of the underlying that offsets a position's exposure to small moves in the underlying's price. For an option it is the delta, so a 0.50-delta call contract is hedged with 50 shares. Chapter 5C

hedging. Taking a position whose gains offset losses on a risk you already hold, such as selling futures against a crop or buying puts against a stock holding. Chapter 4C

higher-order Greeks. Measures of how the first-order Greeks themselves change as the underlying's price, time or volatility moves. Vanna, charm, volga, speed and color are the five that matter most for dealer flow. Chapter 5D

high-frequency trading. Trading by firms that use fast automated systems to quote and trade many times a second, typically as market makers earning small spreads on very large volumes. Abbreviated HFT. Chapter 6E

high-yield bond. A bond rated below investment grade, which pays a higher yield in return for a greater risk that its issuer defaults. Also called a junk bond. Chapter 11A

Hurst exponent. A number describing how the variance of cumulative returns grows with the horizon. It is 0.5 for a random walk, below 0.5 for a mean-reverting series and above 0.5 for a trending one. Chapter 3B

hyperparameter. A setting chosen before a model is fitted rather than learned from the data, such as the learning rate or the strength of a regularization penalty. Chapter 3C

I

immediate-or-cancel order. A limit order that fills whatever it can at once and cancels the rest instead of resting on the book. Abbreviated IOC. Chapter 2C

in-the-money. Said of an option that would pay something if exercised now: a call whose strike is below the underlying's price, or a put whose strike is above it. Chapter 5A

IV rank. Where today's implied volatility sits between its lowest and highest readings of the past 52 weeks, as a fraction of that range. Rare spikes stretch the range and pull the rank down. Chapter 5G

J

jade lizard. A position that sells an out-of-the-money put and an out-of-the-money call spread. When the total credit is at least the call spread's width it has no upside risk, and all its risk is below the put's strike. Chapter 12D

JPM collar. The quarterly index-option position of JPMorgan's Hedged Equity Fund, which holds stocks, buys a put spread below the market and sells calls above it. It is one of the few large index-option positions whose holder is public. Chapter 8B

K

King Node. Skylit's name for the cell with the largest absolute exposure on its heatmap, which it treats as the day's or week's price target. One of its own pages defines it instead as the largest positive cell. Chapter 8C

kurtosis. The fourth standardized moment of a distribution, measuring how much weight sits in its extreme tails. A normal distribution has a kurtosis of 3. Chapter 1B

L

ladder. A three-strike option position that buys one option and sells two further out of the money at different strikes, collecting premium for a capped profit zone and leaving a large loss on one side, unbounded in the call version. Chapter 12D

lasso. Regression with a penalty on the sum of the absolute values of the weights, also called L1 regularization. It sets many weights exactly to zero, so it also selects features. Chapter 3C

LEAPS. Listed options with expiries more than a year away. The name stands for Long-term Equity AnticiPation Securities. Chapter 5G

learning rate. The step size in gradient descent: how far the parameters move for each unit of slope. Too large and the fit overshoots or diverges, too small and it crawls. Chapter 3C

legging. Entering or closing a multi-leg option position one leg at a time, as separate orders, which exposes you to price moves between the fills. Chapter 2D

leverage. The ratio of a position's total exposure to the capital behind it. An account holding 1.4 million dollars of an index on 1 million dollars of its own money has 1.4 times leverage. Chapter 1E

leveraged ETF. An exchange-traded fund that aims to return a fixed multiple of an index's daily return, such as two times, or minus one times for an inverse fund. To hold the multiple it must trade near each close in the direction of that day's move. Chapter 6C

limit order. An order to buy at or below a stated price, or to sell at or above it. It promises the price but not a fill. Chapter 2C

lit exchange. A registered exchange that displays its order book publicly, so that its best bids and offers feed the National Best Bid and Offer. Chapter 2B

log contract. A payoff equal to the logarithm of the final price of the underlying. A strip of out-of-the-money options weighted by one over the strike squared builds it, and with a daily-rebalanced holding of the underlying it replicates a variance swap. Chapter 9A

log return. The natural logarithm of a period's growth factor, the end price divided by the start price. Log returns are symmetric for a round trip and add up over time. Chapter 1A

long position. Owning an asset, or holding the buyer's side of a contract, so that you gain when its value rises. Chapter 4A

long-short portfolio. A portfolio that buys one group of assets and sells short an equal dollar amount of another. Its return is the difference between the two groups' returns, which depends little on the market's direction. Chapter 10F

long-term capital gain. A gain on a position held for more than a year, taxed at a lower rate than ordinary income. Chapter 2E

loss function. The measure of how badly a model's predictions miss the data, which fitting makes as small as possible. The usual choice is the mean squared error. Chapter 3C

M

Magnificent Seven. A market nickname, abbreviated Mag-7, for seven of the largest US companies by market value: NVDA, AAPL, GOOGL, MSFT, META, AMZN and TSLA. Chapter 11H
