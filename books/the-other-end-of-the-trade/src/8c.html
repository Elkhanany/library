Part VIII · The whole market · Chapter 8C

When the framework breaks

Stress episodes

Where we are

Chapter 8B's effective-volatility formula has a singular point, where the feedback between hedging and prices explodes. This chapter visits it through four episodes: Volmageddon in February 2018, the Great Liquidity Crisis of March 2020, the yen carry unwind of August 2024 and the GME squeeze of January 2021. The aim is to learn which conditions had to coexist before the linear GEX framework stopped being a useful guide.

What you need first
  • Effective volatility, \(\sigma/(1 - \kappa G)\), and its singular point at \(G = 1/\kappa\) (Chapter 8B).
  • Vanna and volga, also called vomma: how an option's delta and vega move when implied volatility moves (Chapter 5D).
  • Portfolio margin, and how it expands in stress (Chapters 1E and 2E).
  • The VIX and its term structure (Chapters 5E and 9A).

Everything built so far assumes a functioning market: finite spreads, available depth, and dealers who can rebalance in size without moving the book against themselves. Two episodes show what the system looks like when those assumptions break: Volmageddon (February 5, 2018) and the Great Liquidity Crisis (March 16, 2020). Both are textbook cases of dealer feedback running away from the standard model.

The point of these case studies is not to predict the next one. It is to calibrate your sense of when the linear GEX framework stops being a useful guide, and to see which structural conditions had to coexist for the feedback to become explosive.

In plain terms

In a calm market, dealer hedging is one flow among many, and the market's depth absorbs it. In each episode below, three things lined up: dealers short gamma somewhere, a mechanical buyer or seller forced to trade whatever the price, and a market too thin to absorb the flow. As Chapter 8D puts it, any one of these alone is insufficient. Together they drive the feedback toward the singular point of Chapter 8B.

1 · February 5, 2018: Volmageddon

Before the event, an eight-month low-volatility regime had compressed the VIX to multi-year lows. Inverse-VIX exchange-traded products (ETPs), XIV by Credit Suisse and SVXY by ProShares, had grown to a combined approximately $3.5 billion in assets under management (AUM). These products were structurally short the front VIX future, with a rebalancing rule that required them to buy VIX futures at the close in proportion to the day's VIX move.

Worked example: Why an inverse product buys into a rise

Step 1: the target. Suppose a product aims each day for −1 times the daily change in the VIX futures it holds. It starts the day with $100 million of assets and is short $100 million of futures.

Step 2: a 10% rise. When the futures rise 10%, the short loses $10 million, so the assets fall to $90 million while the short position grows to $110 million of futures.

Step 3: the rebalance. To be short exactly its $90 million of assets, the product must cut the short from $110 million to $90 million, so it buys $20 million of VIX futures at the close. That is \(2 \times 10\% \times \$100\) million: the purchase grows with the day's rise.

Step 4: the limit. On the same arithmetic, a rise of 80% in a day would cut the product's assets by 80%, and a rise of 100% would erase them.

The rebalancing rule for any leverage

Let a product with assets \(A\) target an exposure of \(L \times A\), where \(L = -1\) for an inverse product and \(L = 2\) or 3 for a leveraged one, such as a leveraged ETF. After the underlying returns \(x\), the exposure has become \(LA(1 + x)\) and the assets \(A(1 + Lx)\). The new target is \(LA(1 + Lx)\), so the trade the product must make is

\[ \begin{aligned} &LA(1 + Lx) - LA(1 + x) \\ &\quad = L(L - 1)\,x\,A \end{aligned} \]

For \(L = -1\) the factor \(L(L - 1)\) is 2, which gives the worked example's \(2xA\), and it is also 2 for \(L = 2\) and 6 for \(L = 3\). The factor \(L(L - 1)\) is positive for every inverse or leveraged product, so each of them buys after a rise and sells after a fall. That is the same pro-trend flow as a short-gamma dealer's, which is why Chapter 8D counts leveraged-ETF rebalancing as a source of mechanical buying.

After the close, Credit Suisse's XIV acceleration clause triggered, with the product's net asset value (NAV) down more than 80% intraday. An acceleration clause lets the issuer end a product early once its value falls past a stated threshold. The ETP was liquidated, and its AUM went from $1.9 billion to effectively zero in one session.

The mechanism ran step by step, each step feeding the next:

  1. Inverse-VIX ETPs (XIV, SVXY) were structurally short the front VIX future. Their rebalance rule required buying VIX futures into the close in proportion to that day's VIX move.
  2. Each VIX uptick demanded more buying, which lifted VIX futures, which lifted spot VIX, which triggered more buying: the canonical positive feedback loop.
  3. Dealers on the other side of VIX call wings were short gamma in volatility of volatility, with negative vomma. As the VIX broke through 25, their hedges flipped from passive to forced. Vanna kicked in across SPX puts at the same time, forcing index-level selling on top of the VIX move.
  4. Credit Suisse's XIV acceleration clause, triggered by NAV down more than 80% intraday, fired after the close, and the ETP was liquidated.
  5. The combined flow into the closing auction created the 115,862-contract one-minute spike the BIS documented. It came from inverse-VIX rebalancing, dealer hedging of volatility of volatility, and panicked retail liquidations.

Three preconditions had to coexist for the linear framework to fail:

  • Dealers were deeply short gamma in the VIX wings, that is, in volatility of volatility. When the VIX broke through threshold levels, the wing hedging was no longer linear.
  • Mechanical, rule-based rebalancing demand from inverse-VIX ETPs supplied unbounded one-sided flow.
  • Market depth had degraded structurally: the calm regime had pushed market makers to thin their quotes, and there was no real depth to absorb the closing-auction sell.

When all three conditions are present, the effective-volatility equation of Chapter 8B, \(\sigma_\text{eff}^2 = \sigma^2/(1 - \kappa G)^2\), approaches its singular limit. There \(G\) is the destabilizing gamma, positive when published GEX is negative. Short dealer gamma pushes \(G\) up, and thin depth pushes \(\kappa\) up, so both push their product up toward 1. The system is then no longer described by a probabilistic prior; it is governed by a literal forcing function. This is the right tail of the dealer-hedging distribution, and it does not respond to mean-reversion bets at any size.

2 · March 16, 2020: the liquidity crisis

Before the event, COVID-19 had spread globally over February and early March 2020. The VIX rose from 14 in late January to 53 by Friday, March 13, which the Dealer Mechanics Lab calls the largest two-week VIX increase in history at that point. The term structure had inverted, into backwardation, on March 9. Risk-parity funds began publicly de-grossing, cutting their gross positions, in the same week.

On Monday, March 16, 2020, the stress peaked:

  • Before the open, ES futures, the S&P 500 futures of Chapter 2A, locked limit-down: the 5% rule triggered, and they could not trade lower.
  • At the 9:30 AM open SPX gapped down massively, and intraday it traded as low as 2,280, down approximately 12% from the prior close.
  • The VIX printed 82.69, its all-time intraday high.
  • Level 1 circuit breakers, the first tier of market-wide trading halts, triggered on several days around it: March 9, 12, 16 and 18.

The mechanism was layered across several feedback loops at once:

  1. Vol-targeting funds were de-grossing: risk-parity and volatility-targeting strategies, which size their positions to a target portfolio volatility, were forced to sell equities and bonds together as realized volatility exploded.
  2. Portfolio-margin requirements expanded: as Chapter 2E discusses, they expanded violently in the first two weeks of March. Many accounts were forced into liquidation as their stress-tested requirements doubled or tripled (Chapter 1E).
  3. Dealer vanna ran in reverse: with the VIX rising from 30 to 80 in a week, the deltas of out-of-the-money puts became dramatically more negative. Dealers short these puts had to sell increasing amounts of SPX equivalents to stay delta-neutral, and the hedging flow was overwhelmingly one-sided selling.
  4. Treasury bonds and gold sold off too, a sign that participants were raising cash through any channel that still had liquidity. The cross-asset correlation collapsed, and nothing was diversifying.
  5. The Federal Reserve intervened with several emergency announcements between March 15 and March 23. They eventually included unlimited quantitative easing (QE), which is open-ended central-bank buying of bonds, expanded repo facilities for short-term lending against collateral, and dollar swap lines with foreign central banks. This stopped the cascade, but only after market structure had been visibly damaged.
Worked example: How a volatility target forces selling

Step 1: the rule. Take a volatility-targeting fund whose exposure is its target volatility, 10%, divided by the realized volatility it measures.

Step 2: calm. With realized volatility at 10%, it holds \(10/10 = 100\%\) of its capital in the market.

Step 3: stress. When realized volatility climbs to 40%, the rule gives \(10/40 = 25\%\). The fund must sell three quarters of its holdings, whatever it thinks of prices, and it sells into the fall that raised the volatility.

Worked example: Vanna: selling with no move in price

Step 1: the put. Take an index at 100 and a put struck at 90 with 30 days to expiry, and hold the index still.

Step 2: two volatilities. By Black-Scholes with a zero interest rate (Chapter 5C), the put's delta is −0.10 at 30% implied volatility and −0.28 at 80%.

Step 3: the dealer. A dealer short 1,000 of these puts, each on 100 units, is long \(0.10 \times 100{,}000 = 10{,}000\) units of delta at 30% volatility and about 28,000 units at 80%. To stay neutral, it must sell about 18,000 more units of the index, roughly $1.8 million, although the index has not moved. In March 2020 the fall and the volatility spike came together, and both pushed dealers to sell.

The same three preconditions were present, in a different form:

  • Dealers were short gamma in the wings, in the put protection that institutions had bought as COVID-19 escalated.
  • Mechanical de-grossing from vol-targeting funds and forced portfolio-margin liquidations supplied unbounded one-sided flow.
  • Market depth had degraded acutely, as every intermediary tried to raise cash at the same time.

3 · What both cases share

When the three preconditions coexist, the effective-volatility equation approaches its singular limit. The linear GEX framework is then no longer a useful guide. It still gives a direction, but in a regime where the magnitude of the move it predicts is grossly understated. Position sizes calibrated to normal-regime volatility are catastrophically large in this regime.

The actionable lesson is asymmetric: when the three conditions coexist, your size goes down and your stops widen, regardless of whether the catalyst ever arrives. Most weeks, nothing happens. In the weeks when something does happen, the framework built across Parts II to VIII stops describing the market. The cost of being wrong then scales non-linearly with the gamma you do not see.

Familiar ground

A cytokine release syndrome after CAR-T therapy has the same shape. Activated immune cells release cytokines that activate more immune cells, and the loop feeds itself once it starts. Tocilizumab blocks the IL-6 receptor and breaks one link of the loop without removing the cells that started it. In markets the brakes also came from outside: the Federal Reserve's emergency announcements in March 2020, and a broker's restriction on purchases in the GME squeeze below. In both cases the loop stopped only after damage was done.

4 · Reading the warning signs

Both episodes had observable precursors, days or weeks before the explosion. In late January 2018, the VVIX, the VIX of the VIX, had been elevated for weeks while the VIX itself slept. That was a tell that positioning in volatility of volatility was deteriorating beneath the surface.

Before March 16, 2020, the VIX term structure inverted, and the Dealer Mechanics Lab dates the inversion in two ways: to late February, seven trading days before March 16, and to March 9. Either way the inversion came before the crash, as did the public de-grossing of risk-parity funds from March 9.

Neither reading required forecasting the catalyst. Both required noticing that the conditions for feedback were quietly being installed. The composite regime scorecard of Chapter 11G includes both the VVIX-VIX divergence and the term-structure signals as components.

5 · August 5, 2024: the yen carry unwind

A more recent example is the unwind of the yen carry trade on August 5, 2024. In a yen carry trade, a position borrows in yen, at low interest, to hold higher-yielding assets elsewhere (Chapter 10E). After the Bank of Japan unexpectedly raised rates the week before and Friday's US jobs report came in weak, leveraged yen-carry positions around the world were forced to unwind.

The Nikkei fell 12% on Monday, August 5, its largest daily decline in percentage terms since Black Monday in 1987. SPX gapped down approximately 3%. The VIX printed above 65 intraday, the third-highest reading in its history at that point. It briefly traded at levels seen only in March 2020 and in the 2008 financial crisis.

The unwind featured a similar mechanism: leveraged carry positions, which are essentially short volatility on the yen, required forced selling of yen-funded assets all at once, with no time for an orderly unwinding. SPX dealer gamma was short going into the event, and cross-asset correlations spiked toward 1. The episode resolved relatively quickly, with the VIX back to 20 within a week. It confirmed that the three-precondition framework continues to describe the right-tail behavior of modern markets.

6 · January 2021: the GME single-stock gamma squeeze

The Volmageddon, March 2020 and August 2024 cases were macro, index-level events. The 2021 episode in GME (GameStop) is the canonical single-name gamma squeeze, a rally driven by dealers buying the stock to hedge the calls that customers bought. It is the most operationally relevant case study for understanding retail-driven dealer feedback in individual stocks.

Before the event, the setup had four parts:

  • GME traded at about $20 in early January 2021.
  • Short interest was approximately 140% of the float: the stock was heavily shorted through stock loans.
  • An active retail community, WallStreetBets, coordinated a call-buying campaign.
  • The options chain showed heavy retail call buying at strikes of $20, $30, $40 and $50, with very low premiums of under $0.10.

Short interest is the number of shares sold short and not yet bought back, often quoted as a percentage of the public float. It can exceed 100%, because a borrowed share, once sold, can be lent again by its buyer and sold short again.

The mechanism ran in six stages:

  1. Retail call buying created dealer short-call inventory: Citadel Securities, Susquehanna and other wholesalers absorbed the retail call flow as its natural counterparty (Chapter 2B). The resulting dealer position was short calls with growing notional exposure.
  2. Dealers hedged by buying GME shares: each short call requires a long stock hedge equal to delta × open interest × multiplier. As GME rose, the deltas of out-of-the-money calls rose toward 1.0, so dealers had to buy progressively more shares.
  3. The feedback loop ran: rising GME moved the calls toward the money, which raised dealer deltas, which meant more share buying and more upward pressure, over and over. It was classic short-gamma feedback in single-name form.
  4. Short sellers covered: hedge funds buying back shares at progressively higher prices, which is short covering, added to the buying pressure.
  5. The run accelerated to its peak: GME hit $483 intraday on January 28, 2021, about 24 times its early-January level. Implied volatility on GME options exceeded 1,000% on a one-day basis.
  6. A brokerage liquidity crisis followed: on January 28, Robinhood restricted purchases of GME. The reason, according to later congressional testimony, was its clearing deposits at the DTCC (Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation), which clears US stock trades. They had ballooned from about $700 million to more than $3 billion because of the volatility, and Robinhood could not post enough capital to keep accepting buy orders at scale.
Worked example: A dealer's hedge as the price climbs

Step 1: the position and its hedge. Suppose a dealer is short 1,000 GME calls struck well above the price, each on 100 shares, with a delta of 0.10, so it holds \(0.10 \times 1{,}000 \times 100 = 10{,}000\) shares as its hedge.

Step 2: the rally. The price climbs to the strike and then past it. As the calls' delta rises to 0.50 and then to 0.90, the hedge grows to 50,000 shares and then to 90,000. Every one of the 80,000 extra shares is bought after the price has risen, which is what pushes it higher still.

The Robinhood restrictions broke the feedback loop: GME closed on January 28 down 44% from its intraday high. Over the following week, retail buying faded, dealer hedges unwound, and GME normalized to about $50.

The case demonstrates four things:

  • Single-name gamma squeezes require specific structural conditions: very high short interest, coordinated retail call buying, and enough dealer absorption to create meaningful delta-hedge inventory.
  • Liquidity constraints can break feedback loops: brokerage capital limits and DTCC margin act as natural shock absorbers, though the same constraints can amplify later stress.
  • The mechanism is the same as in the index cases: rising spot raises dealer delta and forces buying. It runs at single-name scale, with higher per-share gamma because of the lower notional base.
  • Implied-volatility readings during the squeeze were misleading: with spot moving 20% to 50% a day, Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) implied volatility breaks down. The model's assumptions of lognormal returns and continuous hedging are violated, so practitioners use realized-volatility measures during such episodes.

Three preconditions present in GME, mirroring the index cases:

  • Dealers were short gamma in the call wings, from retail buying.
  • Mechanical buying pressure came from retail fear of missing out (FOMO) and from short covering.
  • Liquidity was constrained at the brokerage level, through DTCC margin.

Structurally, this is the same three-precondition framework as Volmageddon and March 2020, at single-name scale. The single-name version differs in two ways: its "stress" is asymmetric, on the long side rather than both sides, and its liquidity constraint works through brokerage capital rather than market depth.

Key idea

The GME case is sometimes dismissed as a one-off retail phenomenon, but the mechanism is generic. Any single stock with high short interest, retail call-buying interest and an absorbing dealer counterparty is theoretically vulnerable. Citadel Securities' testimony after GME is one of the few public dealer-side disclosures during a gamma squeeze, and it is worth reading for operational detail.

In practice

In practice the three preconditions become a weekly check, run before any news. Is dealer gamma short in some wing? Is there a mechanical flow that must trade whatever the price, such as inverse or leveraged products, volatility-targeting funds or margin calls? Is depth thin, with wide spreads and small quotes? When all three answers are yes, cut size and widen stops before any catalyst appears. Keep the positions that lose in a feedback regime, short options above all, small enough to survive a margin requirement that doubles or triples, as portfolio-margin requirements did in March 2020 (Chapter 1E).

When it fails

The three-precondition framework is drawn from a handful of episodes, read after the fact. The conditions can coexist for weeks with nothing happening, and the next episode may run through a flow no public model sees (Chapter 8A). Liquidity constraints cut both ways, too: the brokerage limits that broke the GME loop can amplify later stress.

Decision rule
  • All three preconditions present: cut size and widen stops now, whether or not a catalyst arrives. Do not fade the move, because a feedback regime does not respond to mean-reversion bets at any size.
  • One or two present: watch them, and decide the size cut in advance, so that it does not have to be decided in the stress.
  • None present: the linear framework of Chapters 8A and 8B is a reasonable prior, with the limits Chapter 8A lists.
Common mistake: fading a feedback move

The move looks overdone, so you bet on a reversal. In a feedback regime the forced flows keep coming until the structure changes: the rebalancing is done, the margin calls are met, or an outside brake arrives. A mean-reversion bet placed before then is on the wrong side of a forcing function.

Common mistake: trusting implied volatility in a squeeze

With a stock moving 20% to 50% a day, Black-Scholes-Merton implied volatility no longer measures what it claims to, because its assumptions of lognormal returns and continuous hedging have failed. Use realized-volatility measures to size, as practitioners do in such episodes.

What to carry forward
  • Four episodes, in 2018, 2020, 2021 and 2024, share three preconditions: short dealer gamma, mechanical one-sided flow and constrained liquidity.
  • Short gamma pushes the destabilizing gamma up and thin depth pushes \(\kappa\) up, so effective volatility nears its singular limit, where mean-reversion bets fail at any size.
  • Inverse and leveraged products rebalance by \(L(L - 1)\,xA\), always with the move; volatility targets and vanna force selling as volatility rises.
  • Warning signs came days or weeks early: an elevated VVIX in 2018 and an inverted term structure in 2020. When all three preconditions are present, cut size and widen stops before any catalyst.

7 · Check yourself

Question 1

An inverse product with $50 million of assets targets −1 times the daily move of its futures. If the futures rise 20%, what must it trade at the close?

Answer

The short loses $10 million, so the assets fall to $40 million while the short grows to $60 million. Getting back to −1 times its assets means buying $20 million of futures, which is \(2 \times 20\% \times \$50\) million.

Question 2

Name the three preconditions, and say how each showed up in March 2020.

Answer

Short dealer gamma: dealers were short the put protection institutions had bought. Mechanical one-sided flow: vol-targeting funds de-grossed and portfolio-margin accounts were forced to liquidate. Degraded depth: every intermediary tried to raise cash at the same time, and even Treasury bonds and gold were sold.

Question 3

Why does a jump in implied volatility make a dealer who is short out-of-the-money puts sell the index, even with no move in price?

Answer

Higher implied volatility makes those puts more likely to finish in the money, so their deltas grow more negative, which is vanna. The dealer, short the puts, becomes longer in delta and sells the index to get back to neutral.

Question 4

A fund targets 12% volatility, and realized volatility rises from 12% to 36%. What fraction of its holdings must it sell?

Answer

Its exposure falls from \(12/12 = 100\%\) to \(12/36 = 33\%\), so it sells two thirds of its holdings, into the fall that raised the volatility.

Question 5

What broke the GME loop, and how does that differ from the way the index episodes ended?

Answer

A brokerage restriction: Robinhood restricted purchases when its clearing deposits ballooned, which removed the buyers. In the index episodes the constraint ran through market-maker capital and market depth. They resolved as volatility faded once dealers could rebalance again, helped in March 2020 by the Federal Reserve's intervention.
