## Sources
dl:2D and dl:3D, merged into one line: the book an order meets, making against taking, and the order types that choose between them. The order types and the closing auction of dl:3B (Chapter 2B's source) are merged in here too, so the three versions of each become one.

How the pieces were combined:
- dl:2D's "The order book" and dl:3D's "The book's anatomy" make "The order book" and "How deep the book is". dl:3D's "Where retail SPY orders actually go" is condensed into "Which book your order meets", since Chapter 2B covers routing in full.
- The order types appear in all three sources (dl:2D and dl:3B list five, dl:3D three). They are one list here, with dl:3B's "five order types cover 95% of retail decisions" and dl:3D's "three order types cover 90%" both kept, and dl:3D's case for marketable limits (quote dropouts, fast markets, "200 points away from the NBBO") added to the discipline paragraph.
- The closing auction appears in dl:2D and dl:3B. The two are merged, keeping both estimates of its size (see Conflicts).
- Moved to Chapter 2D, whose subtitle names market impact: dl:2D's "Slippage scaling" (the square-root law, SPY's average daily volume of 50 to 80 million shares, a 1,000-share trade at 0.001% to 0.002% of it, a 1-million-share trade at 1% to 2% with perhaps 5 to 15 bps of impact, the 100-million-share trade) and dl:3D's "Slippage and market impact" (iceberg orders, VWAP algorithms and dark pools as responses to impact; dealer hedging in ES rather than spot SPY). The facts checker reports 0.001, 80, ADV, Almgren-Chriss and ES here for this reason. Almgren and Chriss (2000) stays in this chapter, with the execution algorithms under "Walking the book".
- dl:3D's opening ("Section 2D introduced the order book to support the quant-trading strategy taxonomy. This section is the same mechanism viewed from the cash-equity execution side ...") is dropped: the merge makes it redundant.

## Conflicts
- SPY's depth. dl:3D puts 500,000 to 2 million share-equivalents at the NBBO. dl:2D's walking example has "a top-of-book ask of 1,000 shares" and a 100,000-share order that walks several levels. The text keeps both and says the example assumes a thin top of book, since at dl:3D's depth a 100,000-share order would fill at the top.
- The intraday momentum paper. dl:2D says Baltussen, Da, Lammers and Martens (2021) show "the first half-hour of return predicts the last half-hour of return". dl:6D (Chapter 6C) describes the predictor as the return over the rest of the day, from the previous close to the last 30 minutes. The text gives both and points to 6C. The writer of 6C may wish to confirm which the paper uses.
- The auction's size. dl:2D: "Approximately 10-15% of daily NYSE volume". dl:3B: about 5% to 7% in 2010, 10% to 12% in 2020, and estimates from 12% to 18% in 2025. The text gives both; it does not guess which year dl:2D's figure describes.
- SPX option spreads: "$0.10 to $0.30 wide" (dl:2D, on the bounce) and "often $0.10 to $0.50 on liquid SPX strikes" (dl:3D). Each is kept in its own context.

## Questions for the author
- Inverted markets. The source's rationale, "traders who want immediate execution badly enough are willing to pay for it explicitly, while liquidity is harder to attract", does not say which side pays for what: on an inverted venue the resting order pays and the taker is paid. The text keeps the source's sentence. Should the mechanism be spelled out?
- "When your resting order fills, the trade was almost certainly initiated by someone with marginally more information": kept at its strength, though the break-even model the text adds lets any fraction of traders be informed.
- The square-root law's attribution to Almgren and Chriss moves with it to Chapter 2D; see notes/2d.md.
