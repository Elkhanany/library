## Conflicts
- Iron condors. dl:5E lists "iron condor writing" among the systematic short-premium strategies that "have historically produced positive returns over multi-decade horizons". The Edge Book (spx:10.6, Chapter 10I) rejects systematic iron condors for retail accounts: the Cboe CNDR index returned about 690% from 1986 to 2024 against about 5,500% for SPX total return, because wing breaks erase the premium. The two claims can both be true (positive returns, far below the index), but the advice differs. The chapter states both, in the manner of Chapter 1D, and links Chapters 10I and 12C; it does not repeat the CNDR numbers, which belong to 10I.
- None on the size of the VRP: spx:6.2 (Chapter 10C) gives the same 4.2 vol points (VIX 19.3% against realized 15.1%, 1990 to 2018). Chapter 10C defines "VRP harvest" narrowly as ATM monthly cash-secured XSP puts; this chapter's broader list of short-premium strategies is the Lab's.
- Within dl:5E and dl:5G: this chapter says skew *flattening* in quiet regimes precedes vol spikes more often than chance; Chapter 5G says skew *steepens* 1 to 3 days before sell-offs. Both are kept. They may describe different horizons, but the source does not say; notes/5g.md asks.

## Questions for the author
- "If the market is quoting a 30-day ATM SPX call at $35": no index level is given. By the ATM shortcut, $35 for 30 days implies an IV near 5% at an index of 6,000, so the number reads as an illustration of the definition only. The chapter keeps it and does not compute its IV; the worked inversion uses a hypothetical $100 underlying instead.
- Which Bondarenko analysis gives the +4.2 vol points for 1990 to 2018? The Edge Book cites Bondarenko (2014, 2019).
- The efficiency figures (about five and seven to eight times close-to-close) hold for a driftless continuous price without gaps. Should the text say so? The "When it fails" callout says the range estimators assume continuous prices within the day.
- "SKEW above 145 historically has preceded mean-reverting vol spikes": over what sample, and how often did readings above 145 come with no spike? The chapter keeps the claim and adds, as teaching, that a signal's hit rate depends on the base rate of spikes.
- "Retail traders systematically overpay for upside speculation (lottery-ticket calls on meme stocks, OTM SPY calls on event days)": is there a study behind this, or is it the author's observation?
