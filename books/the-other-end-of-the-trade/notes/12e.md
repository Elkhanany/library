## Changed
- Fixed: The box's rates: "the rate is often within 5–25 bps of broker margin call rates for borrowing". Read literally as the margin rates brokers charge, this contradicts the next sentence, that the box's implied rate is "typically materially below the margin rates that retail brokers charge". The text reads "broker margin call rates" as the broker call rate, the benchmark rate at which brokers borrow to fund margin loans, which makes the two sentences agree, and adds a glossary card for it. See the questions below.
- Fixed: The box example keeps the source's $9,940 credit, 2.45% rate and "If T-bills yield 4.2%, that's a very cheap loan". It adds a step: by the source's own statement that lending rates sit within a few bps of T-bill rates, a box priced at 2.45% against 4.2% T-bills would not normally find a lender, and at 4.2% the same box would bring about $9,898 ($102 of interest). The text says to read the $9,940 as a number chosen for the arithmetic.
- Fixed (dl:11.4): the box friction arithmetic. The source says "1–4 cents per share of width. On a $10,000 box, that's $100–$400". Per share of the multiplier, 1 to 4 cents would be $1 to $4 per box; only "per dollar of width" gives $100 to $400. The text says "1 to 4 cents for each dollar of width, or $100 to $400 on a $10,000 box". See the questions below.
- Fixed (dl:11.4): the early-assignment condition. "Even SPX boxes (European) are safe" is written "SPX boxes are European-style and safe from it". The source's condition for early exercise, "when the discounted strike exceeds time value", is written as Chapter 12H's test, the interest on the strike exceeding the remaining time value.
- The Robinhood story: the source says the user was wiped out "when SPX moved". The text says "when the market moved". Widely reported accounts of the 2019 box-spread loss on Robinhood may involve SPY options and early assignment rather than SPX and margin calls; the story should be checked against a source before the instrument is named. See the questions below.
- "institutional retail-tier alternative to broker margin loans" is written "give a retail account the institutional alternative to a broker margin loan".
- "Combo = synthetic long or short stock": kept as the source defines it. Some traders use "combo" for a call and a put at different strikes; the text does not add that.
- The guts: the source says it has the "same vol exposure as straddle but more capital-intensive". Kept, with the parity identity that a long guts is the long strangle at the same strikes plus a bond paying the gap between them, which is why it costs more for the same exposure. The text says the strangle is the more exact match.
- Formulas added where the source gives none, all derived from the legs: the synthetic future's P&L and breakeven; its net premium by parity (59.7 points for a three-month 6,000 SPX synthetic at a 4% rate, no dividends); the synthetic put's P&L, maximum loss and breakeven; the strip's and strap's slopes and breakevens (worked with Chapter 12C's $40 call and put); the guts's maximum loss and breakevens; a loss bound for the double diagonal when its long options sit further out than its short ones; the box's payoff as two synthetics; the implied rate [eq:box] from \(C(1 + rn/365) = W\), which is the source's own "(60 / 9,940) × (365 / 90)"; the conversion's profit condition [eq:conv], worked with Chapter 5F's example (98.50 against 99.00).
- Parity is written in words, call − put = \(S - Ke^{-rT}\), because this tier uses \(C\) for a net credit.
- Old references translated: Section 10A.5 to Chapter 11B; the Section 10A dashboard to Chapter 11A; the 10F composite to Chapter 11G; Section 12B (conviction score) to Chapter 13B; Section 12C (sizing) to Chapter 13C.
- No "Familiar ground" callout: no clinical analogy fitted accurately. Drug generics, the obvious candidate for synthetics, do not keep price parity with the originals, so the analogy would mislead.

## Conflicts
- None between sources: the chapter draws on dl:11.4 alone. Chapter 1E (from spx:0.4) lists box-spread financing as a cheap route to leverage, consistent with this chapter.

## Questions for the author
- "Within 5–25 bps of broker margin call rates for borrowing": is the broker call rate meant, as the text reads it? If the margin rates retail brokers charge were meant, the sentence contradicts the next one.
- Box friction: is "1–4 cents per share of width" meant per dollar of width, as the $100 to $400 implies? At those costs a 90-day box's $60 of interest is lost to friction, which may understate how cheaply boxes trade. Worth checking against current quotes.
- The Robinhood box-spread story of 2019: was it SPX or SPY, and was the loss from margin calls or from early assignment? The text avoids naming the instrument until this is checked.
- The box example's 2.45% against 4.2% T-bills: should the example be re-priced to a credit near the T-bill rate (about $9,898), so that it does not show an arbitrage the source says does not exist?

## Figures
- The outline places no figure in this chapter. Five figure ideas: what a synthetic costs to hold (its net premium against time, rate and a borrow fee), a double diagonal at its near expiry with its loss bounds, strips, straps and guts against the strangle-plus-bond, a box as two synthetics whose slopes cancel, and a box-spread rate calculator with friction and the shortest holding period that beats a margin loan.
- box-spread-rate (new, from a figure idea): the annual rate of borrowing with a sold SPX box 100 points wide ($10,000 owed at expiry), before and after a round-trip cost. It uses the chapter's [eq:box], r = (W − C)/C × 365/n, with the cost taken off the credit. Sliders set the rate before costs (0.5% to 8%, default 2.45%), the days to expiry (7 to 365, default 90) and the round-trip cost ($0 to $400, default $100, the chapter's 1 to 4 cents a dollar of width). The curve is the rate after costs at every term up to a year for the chosen rate before costs. It is drawn against the example's 4.2% T-bill and a band of margin loans 100 to 400 bps above it, with the T-bill standing in for the federal funds rate. First-state readouts: credit C = $9,940 ($60 interest), rate after costs = 6.60%, beats 5.2% margin from 137 days, beats 8.2% margin from 65 days. The credit, the interest and the 2.45% reproduce the worked example. A rate of 4.2% gives step 4's $9,898 ($102 interest). A rate of 5.2% over 180 days gives the check question's $9,750 per $10,000, which is $97,500 on its 1,000-point box. At 30 days the example's box costs 14.79% a year after costs, and at 365 days 3.51%.
