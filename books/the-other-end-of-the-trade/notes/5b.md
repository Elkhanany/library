## Changed
- Fixed: the synthetic chain broke put-call parity, which the source invokes in the next paragraph. With no interest, a call and a put at the same strike differ by S − K, but the source's 148-strike call and put (4.34 and 0.34 at the mid) differ by 4.00 against S − K = 2, and its 140 pair by 11.65 against 10. Its 150 and 152 calls (2.50 and 0.34 at the mid) differ by 2.16, more than the 2 points between their strikes, so a 150/152 call spread would cost more than it could ever pay; the 148 and 150 puts have the same fault. The 140 call's $1.70 of extrinsic value was also hardly the "tiny extrinsic component" the text describes. The chain is rebuilt with the Black-Scholes model, no interest or dividend, and one implied volatility of 14.1%, chosen so that the at-the-money mid stays at the source's $2.50. Each quote is the model price ± $0.05, the source's spread width. Every sentence the source says about the chain still holds, and more exactly: the 140 call is almost all intrinsic ($10.13, with 13 cents of extrinsic), the 140 put is near zero, the 150 call and put match at $2.45/$2.55, and the 160 call is near zero ($0.12/$0.22). The text says how the chain was made.
- "Extrinsic = Premium − Intrinsic (always ≥ 0)": true for an option that can be exercised at once, and the text says so. A deep in-the-money European put can trade a little below intrinsic value, because the strike arrives only at expiry and waiting costs interest; the text adds one sentence on this, since the book's default option is European.
- Added teaching, no new facts: a coin-flip worked example showing why extrinsic value peaks at the money (strikes 90 to 110 on a stock that ends at 90 or 110); the extrinsic value of each strike in the rebuilt chain, and the equality of call and put extrinsic value at each strike (put-call parity with no interest); a table of how a trade's opening and closing sides move open interest, used for the source's 50,000-contract example and a self-check question; the spread as a share of the mid (eq:spread) with the source's two quotes worked through (3.3% and 33%); which rows of the chain would be shaded; a practice callout, a "When it fails", a decision rule, two common mistakes, a recap and five self-check questions. The 160 call's 59% spread in the second common mistake is computed from the rebuilt chain.
- A "Familiar ground" analogy: open interest and volume as prevalence and incidence, with the caveat that volume counts closings too.
- Old references translated: "Section 5F" to Chapter 5F, "Section 8" to Part VIII (Chapters 8A and 8B).

## Questions for the author
- The rebuilt chain (above) is the largest change in this chapter. If you prefer the original numbers, the text around the chain would need to say that they are stylized and do not satisfy put-call parity.
- "A sudden 50,000-contract OI increase at a specific strike, when daily volume was also 50,000 contracts, tells you a single large player took a new position at that strike." The arithmetic shows that every contract traded opened a position on both sides; that one player was behind it is an inference. The text keeps the source's wording and adds the arithmetic. Should it be hedged ("most likely")?
- "Two trades at this spread to enter and exit can wipe out three months of theta collection" assumes a rate of theta that the source does not give. Kept as the source's hedged "can".
- "The further out of the money, the higher the extrinsic value relative to the intrinsic" (kept from dl:5B). An out-of-the-money option has no intrinsic value, so the ratio is undefined there, and read literally the sentence runs against the chapter's point that extrinsic value peaks at the money. Is it meant for in-the-money options moving toward the money, where the extrinsic share of the premium grows? (Raised by the second check of the maths.)
