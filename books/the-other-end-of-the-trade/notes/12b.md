## Questions for the author
- The example premiums are far below model values at the implied volatilities they state: the 6,050 call is about $101 at 18% and the 5,950 put about $127 at 22%, against $35 each. The text keeps the numbers and says they are illustrative. Should the examples be re-priced, which would move every breakeven and profit?

- "Negative drift baked into option premium structures": is this the variance risk premium (Chapter 10C), the put skew, or both? The text points to 10C.

- long-call-life (new, from a figure idea): the P&L per contract ($100 a point) of the worked example's SPX 6,050 call, bought with 30 days to expiry at SPX 6,000 and priced by Black-Scholes with interest ignored. It is drawn against SPX on the day it is bought, on a chosen day and at expiry. Sliders set the IV at entry (5% to 40%, default 8.2%), the days left (0 to 30, default 15), an IV crush as a share of the entry IV from the chosen day on (0% to 60%, default none) and SPX then (5,900 to 6,200, default 6,150). The default IV is the volatility at which the model prices the call at the example's $35.00 (the chapter's "about 8%"); at the stated 18% the price is $100.6 (the chapter's "near $100"). First-state readouts: premium P = $35.0 ($3,500), breakeven at expiry = 6,085, breakeven with 15 days left = 6,039, P&L with 15 days left at 6,150 = +$7,370, P&L at expiry at 6,150 = +$6,500. They reproduce the example's cost, breakeven and step 2 profit; SPX then at 6,000 gives step 3's −$3,500. At 6,050 with 15 days left, a 40% crush (8.2% to 4.9%) turns +$510 into −$1,090 and moves the breakeven from 6,039 to 6,069.
