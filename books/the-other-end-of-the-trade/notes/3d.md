## Changed
- Fixed: the retail-scale worked example put dollars per share into \(f^* = p/L - (1-p)/G\): \(0.60/3 - 0.40/4 = 0.100\), read as 10% of $250,000 = $25,000 = 500 shares. The formula needs \(G\) and \(L\) per dollar staked, as the source's own explanation says ("the entire position rises or falls by G or L per unit"). Per $50 share, \(G = 0.08\) and \(L = 0.06\), so \(f^* = 5.0\): full Kelly is $1,250,000 or 25,000 shares, and quarter-Kelly 6,250 shares. A direct check agrees: maximizing \(0.6\ln(1 + 4n/W) + 0.4\ln(1 - 3n/W)\) over the share count \(n\) gives \(n = W/10 = 25{,}000\). The source's conclusion is therefore reversed: for this edge Kelly is far *more* aggressive than the 1% loss budget (1,250 shares), by a factor of 5 against quarter-Kelly and 20 against full Kelly. The text now gives the correct chain, keeps every number of the source's version inside a "Common mistake" callout that explains the unit error, and rewrites "is the loss-budget rule sufficient on its own? ... only if you don't actually have 10% Kelly worth of edge" to what the corrected arithmetic supports: the budget is sufficient for an edge this strong, and stops being so only if the true edge is much weaker than estimated (below about 52% at even payoffs it exceeds quarter-Kelly, below 50.5% full Kelly). "Most retail strategies don't [have such an edge]" is kept. This needs the author's eye; see Questions.
- Fixed: "Quarter-Kelly gives up about 44% of expected compound growth." Growth at a multiple \(c\) of Kelly is \(2c - c^2\) of the maximum, so quarter-Kelly keeps 43.75% and gives up about 56%. Half-Kelly's "gives up about 25%" is right (it keeps 75%). The text derives \(2c - c^2\) and checks both against the exact binary growth at 55% even odds (0.0038 and 0.0022 against 0.0050).
- Clarified: "the Kelly utility function is concave; over-betting hurts more than under-betting". Near the peak the growth curve is symmetric: betting 1.3 or 0.7 times Kelly both keep 91% of the growth. What is asymmetric is risk (a 69% against a 28% chance of ever halving) and errors by the same factor (twice Kelly keeps no growth, half keeps 75%). The text says both and keeps the source's conclusion that half-Kelly buffers against over-betting. A grind box adds the multiple that maximizes expected growth under a relative error \(s\), \(1/(1 + s^2)\): 0.92 at the source's 30% error, one half when the error equals the estimate.
- Clarified: "A Kelly-sized strategy has roughly a 50% chance of seeing a 50%+ drawdown at some point." Chapter 1D's ruin formula gives exactly 50% for ever falling to half the starting value at full Kelly (the exponent becomes \(2/c - 1\)). The text keeps the source's sentence and says the derivation measures the fall from the start. A 50% fall from a later peak is more likely still over a long horizon.
- Fixed: "Edward Thorp's gambling research, extended by Kelly (1956)": the text says the answer "comes from Edward Thorp's gambling research and from Kelly (1956)", without the order, because Kelly's 1956 paper preceded Thorp's gambling work, which applied it. See Questions.
- Fixed: The 12-hour example: the source compares the 24-bet day with "the once-per-12-hours version". The ratio \(\sqrt{24}\) is right against one bet a day; against two 12-hour bets a day it would be \(\sqrt{12}\). The text says "a single 12-hour bet a day", and states the condition the source leaves implicit: each hourly bet must carry the same per-bet Sharpe ratio, a fresh edge, because chopping one drift into slices adds nothing.
- "the optimal frequency ... where the marginal Sharpe gain ... equals the marginal cost in basis points" is kept. A worked example (hypothetical strategies A and B with the same gross Sharpe ratio of 1.0) shows a retail round trip of 9 bps on a $50 stock (1 cent a share each way plus 5 bps slippage) taking about a fifth of the slow strategy's Sharpe ratio and nine tenths of the fast one's.
- Added teaching, no new facts: the growth rate of a binary bet (eq:growth) and the derivation of eq:kelly (grind box); the continuous Kelly fraction from the variance drag of Chapter 1A, worked at μ = 10% and σ = 20% (2.5 times leverage at 50% volatility, where Chapter 1D says growth peaks); the chance of ever halving, \(0.5^{2/c - 1}\) (eq:halve), and a table of growth kept against that chance; the Sharpe ratio's four-year standard error as a case of an error equal to the estimate; sizing-function formulas (eq:sizing) with a worked example; the chance of ever falling 30% at full and quarter Kelly (70% and 8%); the average loser of $3 against the $2 stop (an average loss of $3,750, 1.5% rather than 1%).
- One clinical analogy: full Kelly as the maximum tolerated dose and fractional Kelly as the dose taken forward (a Familiar ground callout).
- Kelly's fraction is written \(f\), as in the source. Chapter 1D's ruin formula uses \(f\) for a fraction of starting wealth. This chapter never writes that formula with \(f\); it states the halving probability directly.

## Conflicts
- Within the Dealer Mechanics Lab: the worked example here (dl:2E) treats the Kelly fraction as the notional at stake, while dl:12C (Chapter 13C, "Kelly and loss-budget: same framework, different unit") treats it as capital at risk ("five uncorrelated strategies each at quarter-Kelly ... ~12.5% of capital at risk"). With the units corrected, this chapter uses both readings consistently (notional \(f^*\), capital at risk \(f^* L\)) and agrees with 13C's convergence claim (52% to 55% win rates at near-symmetric payoffs give Kelly fractions of 4% to 10%, and quarter-Kelly near a 1% budget). The writer of Chapter 13C should know that the old 2E example it points back to ("Section 2E's worked example shows the per-trade chain end-to-end") now reaches the opposite conclusion about which rule is more conservative.
- The Edge Book does not discuss Kelly sizing.

## Questions for the author
- The corrected worked example reverses a stated conclusion of the source. Please confirm the reading: \(G\) and \(L\) per dollar staked, Kelly far above the loss budget for this edge. If the example was meant to show Kelly as the more conservative rule, it needs a weaker edge (for instance 52% at even payoffs, where quarter-Kelly and the 1% budget coincide, as the chapter's third check question shows).
- The source says Thorp's research was "extended by Kelly (1956)". Kelly's paper came first, and Thorp's work applied it to gambling and markets. Should the text give the order, and with what dates for Thorp?
- "In any realistic behavioral model" (quarter-Kelly with a 30% discipline beating full Kelly) and "the dominant retail cost is slippage" carry no citation in the source. They are kept at its strength.
- "Most production quant strategies use either piecewise-linear or smooth-tanh sizing, calibrated so that the cap corresponds to half-Kelly": kept at the source's strength. Is there a source?
