## Changed
- Fixed (dl:11.3): the jade lizard example said that below 5,950 "you're assigned long stock at 5,950 minus the $51 credit = effective basis 5,899". The example is on SPX, whose options settle in cash, so nothing is assigned as stock (Chapter 5A). The text says the put costs \((5{,}950 - S_T) \times \$100\) at settlement and the position breaks even at 5,899, the same number.
- The naked short put's Reg-T requirement is kept as the source gives it, "approximately 20% of strike value − OTM amount", with a pointer to Chapter 12G, whose source (dl:11.6) gives the fuller formula on the underlying's value plus the premium. See the questions below.
- Formulas added where the source gives none, derived from the legs by the slope rule of Chapter 12A: the short straddle and short strangle cards; the long call condor's maximum profit, loss and breakevens; the call ratio backspread's breakevens (1 by 2) and the put ratio backspread's maximum loss, lower breakeven and maximum profit; the broken-wing butterfly's maximum profit, loss above the wide wing and upper breakeven; the covered short straddle's and covered short strangle's maximum profit, maximum loss and breakeven; the bull call ladder's maximum profit and upper breakeven, and the bear put ladder's; the reverse jade lizard's breakeven; the call and put ratio spreads' maximum profit and breakevens.
- Greeks the source does not give are derived from the legs and labeled as such where they are less obvious: the call ratio backspread (delta near zero or slightly positive, gamma positive, theta negative, vega positive, since it holds more options than it sells) and the covered short straddle (delta about +1 per share, gamma very negative). A Black-Scholes check of a 6,000/6,100 backspread at 15% volatility and 30 days gives net gamma of about +0.0013 per point.
- The source's "Bull/Bear Call/Put Ladders" defines only the bull call ladder. The text defines it and its mirror, the bear put ladder, with formulas, and says in one sentence that reversing every leg gives the opposite profile. It does not name the reversed ladders separately. See the questions below.
- "Long Call Condor / Long Put Condor: Same payoff shape as iron condor": kept, with the parity identity that a long call condor bought for \(D\) is an iron condor sold for \(W - D\) (Chapter 5F). Because the payoff is the iron condor's, the text states that the CNDR evidence and the Edge Book's position apply (Chapters 12C and 10I), as the brief asks wherever condors appear.
- Added teaching with hypothetical numbers, each labeled: the naked short put's margin against its cash-secured version (5,900 SPX put: about $108,000 against $590,000, about 5 times the contracts on the same capital); the short straddle sold for Chapter 12C's 80-point long straddle price (a 10% fall costs 520 points); the short strangle example's loss at a 10% fall (440 points, about 7 times the credit, inside the source's "5-10×"); the call ratio backspread's trough (100 points, $10,000, at 6,100) and upper breakeven (6,200); a broken-wing butterfly with a hypothetical credit of 5; a covered short straddle on a $100 stock with a hypothetical $8 credit; a bull call ladder with a hypothetical 20-point debit.
- Old references translated: Section 3C (margin expansion) to Chapter 2E.
- One "Familiar ground" analogy: sizing a naked short option by its tail as a phase 1 trial sets a dose by its dose-limiting toxicity.
- Checked: "Every entry here has an unbounded or very large loss on at least one side" and the recap's "The advanced structures each leave one side open" were false for the long call and put condors, whose card gives a maximum loss of the debit \(D\) (the iron condor's payoff). They now read "Every entry here but the long condors has ..." and "Most advanced structures leave one side open".

## Questions for the author
- The naked put's Reg-T rule is "approximately 20% of strike value − OTM amount" here and "20% of underlying minus OTM amount + premium, same formula, floored at 10%" in dl:11.6. Should the atlas use one form? Cboe's rules may set a lower percentage for broad-based index options such as SPX than for equity options; if so, the 20% in both places overstates the SPX requirement. This needs checking against a current margin source.
- Ladders: should the reversed ladders (bear call ladder, bull put ladder, as some sources name them) get their own entries?
- "When used systematically with cash-secured collateral (XSP strangles), can be operationally viable": a strangle's short call cannot be secured by cash in the way a put can. Is the intended meaning a strangle held with enough cash to cover the put's assignment and a stress loss on the call?
- The short strangle's "probability of profit about 70%" (dl:11.3) does not follow from the example's own premiums, which imply about 8% volatility and give about 64%; at 15% to 18% volatility it would be about 33% to 39%. Which premiums or which probability should the example use? (Raised by the second check of the maths.)
