dl:11.2, the intermediate tier: credit spreads, neutral structures (with the CNDR evidence and the cndr-vs-spx-historical figure), calendar spreads, debit spreads and directional structures, all of it. The Edge Book's position on condors comes from spx:10.6 through Chapter 10I, as the brief asks, and is attributed to it in the text.

- "K1", "K2", "K3": the strikes are written with subscripts, \(K_1\), \(K_2\), \(K_3\), and renumbered from lowest to highest in every entry (see Changed).

## Changed
- Strikes are numbered from lowest to highest throughout, so that \(K_1 < K_2\) always. The source's bull put spread named the short (higher) strike K1 and the long (lower) strike K2, with "Breakeven: K1 − net credit". Here the short put is at \(K_2\) and the breakeven is \(K_2 - C\): the same strike and the same number.
- Fixed: the calendar call spread's gamma. The source gives "Γ mixed (short near gamma, long far gamma; net positive)". With both legs at the money, the near-dated short call has more gamma than the far-dated long call (at-the-money gamma grows as \(1/\sqrt{T}\), Chapter 5C), so the calendar is net short gamma at the strike. A Black-Scholes check at 15% implied volatility, 7 and 30 days, gives net gamma of −0.0017 per point at the strike, turning positive beyond about 3% either side. The card says "gamma − at the strike, turning + far from it", which keeps the source's "mixed".
- Fixed: in the calendar example, the long call "has ~21 days remaining" at the 7-day expiry. A 30-day option has 23 days left after 7. The text says 23; the rest of the example is unchanged. The facts checker reports 21.
- Fixed: the diagonal call spread example buys a 1-year LEAPS and says that "after 16 monthly rolls, premiums collected = $400 = full LEAP cost basis". A 1-year LEAPS lives about 12 months, so 16 monthly rolls do not fit; at $25 a month the rolls collect about $300 before it expires. The text keeps the 16 rolls and the $400 as the arithmetic of the source, says why they do not fit, and adds that the premiums need only cover the LEAPS's 100 points of time value (it is 300 points in the money at SPX 6,000) while SPX holds. A common-mistake callout repeats the point.
- Fixed: the bull put spread's regime fit says the defined risk lets you size "without the unbounded loss exposure of naked puts". A naked put's loss is bounded, at the strike less the premium (Chapter 5A), though large. The text says "the open-ended exposure of a naked put, whose loss runs all the way to the strike less the premium".
- Formulas added where the source gives none, all derived from the legs by the slope rule of Chapter 12A: breakevens for every credit and debit spread; the iron condor's loss with unequal wings (the wider wing sets it); the iron butterfly's maximum profit, maximum loss and breakevens (worked once with a hypothetical 35-point credit on 50-point wings); the long call butterfly's breakevens (5,960 and 6,040 in the example); the bear put spread, long strangle, inverse iron butterfly, inverse iron condor and collar formulas. The calendar and diagonal cards say why they have no closed-form breakeven, and bound the loss at about the net debit (true when the long call's strike is at or below the short call's).
- Added teaching: the breakeven win rate of the bull put spread example, 80%, from Chapter 1C's formula under the stated simplification that every trade is a full win or a full loss; the same arithmetic for a 75% win rate in "When it fails".
- "Long Put Butterfly: Same as call butterfly but using puts. Identical to call butterfly by put-call parity": kept, with the formulas.
- "2007-present" (the live CNDR history) is "live values since 2007", to avoid a time-anchored word.
- The long straddle example adds the breakeven move as a share of the index, about 1.3%.
- The figure: the text gives its readouts (3.61%, 11.00%, 14.16) and says they describe synthetic stand-ins. It sets them beside the Edge Book's figures for the real index (approximately 690% against approximately 5,500% from 1986 to 2024, about 5.5% and 11% a year), which Chapters 1D and 10A already quote from the Edge Book. The stand-in exaggerates the real multiple, 14.16 against about 7.1.
- The event markers are named as the figure labels them: Black Monday, the GFC (written "the global financial crisis"), Volmageddon and the COVID crash. No dates are added.
- One "Familiar ground" analogy: a condor's probability of profit as a surrogate endpoint, like a response rate that does not extend overall survival.

## Conflicts
- Iron condors. dl:11.2 teaches the iron condor as an intermediate structure for when expected realized volatility is below implied, while itself reporting that the CNDR record "strongly suggests the structure is unattractive for most retail implementations". The Edge Book (spx:10.6, Chapter 10I) rejects systematic condors for retail accounts and allows only occasional, discretionary condors at extreme implied volatility, sized small. The text states both after the figure, in the decision rule and in the recap, and links Chapter 10I. Chapters 1D, 2E, 5E and 10A describe the same disagreement and point here.

## Questions for the author
- The CNDR figure's stand-in series put CNDR at 3.61% a year and the total-return index at 14.16 times CNDR by 2024. The Edge Book's own numbers give about 5.5% and about 7.1 times. Should the figure's stand-in series be rescaled to match the published figures, or replaced by the real index history?
- The premiums in the examples are illustrative and differ across entries for the same strike (the 6,000 call is $35, $40, $50 or $25 in different examples). Is that intended, one self-contained example per entry?
- The iron butterfly entry has no worked example in the source; the text works its formulas once with a hypothetical credit. Should a full example be added?
- Collar: "The 'JPM Hedged Equity Fund' collar is this on SPX." The text keeps the name as given. Is the comparison meant loosely? If the text is to describe the fund, its actual option structure should be checked against a source, since it may differ from a plain collar.

## Figures
- cndr-vs-spx-historical: the text matches its controls (SPX total return on, SPX price return off, event markers on), its log scale, its indexing to 100 at the end of June 1986, its readouts (compound annual growth over 38.5 years and the total-return multiple of CNDR at the end of 2024) and its caption's statement that all three series are synthetic stand-ins. The prose computes the readouts from the figure's data (3.61%, 11.00%, 9.56% for price return, 14.16) and warns that they are not the real index's.
- Six figure ideas: credit spreads with the breakeven win rate against a lognormal chance of finishing above the short strike; the four neutral shapes on one chart; many monthly condors as an equity curve and a P&L histogram; a calendar's value and net gamma at the near expiry; a debit spread against the long call it trims; and buying the move with a straddle, a strangle and an inverse condor.
