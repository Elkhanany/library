- Fixed: "the predictive power of morning return on closing return generates Sharpe ratios in the 0.5-1.0 range", credited to the authors (dl:6D). The paper's predictor is the rest-of-day return (previous close to 30 minutes before the close), as the chapter's own summary of the paper says; "morning return" goes (the first half-hour predictor is Gao, Han, Li and Zhou 2018, which Chapter 2C now names; research: GaoHanLiZhou2018). Search summaries of the paper give annualized Sharpe ratios of about 0.87 to 1.73 for its timing strategy at the asset-class level, before costs, which does not match 0.5 to 1.0; neither range was checked against the paper's tables. The chapter attributes 0.5 to 1.0 to the Dealer Mechanics Lab, reports the higher figures as summaries of the paper's, and the practice callout says both (research: BaltussenDaLammersMartens2021; evidence.md item 31). - Fixed: "When dealers are aggregate net short gamma (typical historically)" (dl:6D). In the paper the S&P 500 dealer gamma measure (net gamma exposure from index option open interest, January 1996 to May 2020) assumes market makers are long calls and short puts; by it, dealer gamma was negative on about 2,930 of 6,088 days, slightly under half, and the momentum was present when it was negative and weak or absent when positive. The chapter keeps "typical historically" as the Lab's, attributed, and says the sign is the paper's assumption, with the count (research: BaltussenDaLammersMartens2021; evidence.md item 31).
- Fixed: the last 30 minutes as a mechanical hedging window ("moves into 4 pm ET frequently extend rather than reverse", dl:6D) is qualified in three paragraphs. The momentum is documented for index and other futures; among individual stocks the last 30 minutes show a reversal that Baltussen, Da and Soebhag (2024) find neither liquidity nor gamma hedging explains (they point to attention-driven retail buying and short sellers' end-of-day risk management). Leveraged-fund rebalancing overstates actual flow: Ivanov and Lenkey (2018) find investor flows into and out of US equity leveraged and inverse ETFs, 2006 to 2014, offset much of it, leaving its late-day effect economically insignificant. Barbon, Beckmeyer, Buraschi and Moerke (2022) find leveraged-fund rebalancing moved stocks' closes more than option hedging did, but less over time as liquidity providers anticipated it, with both effects reversing at the next open. "When it fails" and the recap say the pattern is for futures and that stocks reverse (research: BaltussenDaSoebhag2024, IvanovLenkey2018, BarbonBeckmeyerBuraschiMoerke2022; evidence.md item 32).

## Conflicts
- None between the two sources in this section.
- Within the dealer-lab curriculum, eq:hedgevar says hedging at intervals adds noise with zero mean, while dl:6F's table (Chapter 6E) has coarser hedging capture less of the variance on average. Chapter 6E reconciles the two: under a random walk the expected capture does not depend on the interval, and the dl:6F table assumes a mean-reverting intraday path. This chapter says the error averages zero and points to Chapter 6E.
- Chapter 2B (from dl:3B) also cites Baltussen and coauthors and adds the closing auction as part of the mechanism; the two chapters agree.
