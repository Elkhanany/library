1 · The cosmological principle as an assumption, and its evidence

Announce the destination. We write down the two symmetry assumptions that make cosmology possible, in the same language Chapter 3.7 used for the star — as conditions on Killing vectors — and then say exactly what observation supports them and where they are false.

1.1 · Two symmetries, and the difference from a star

Chapter 3.7 §1 refused to state spherical symmetry as a picture and stated it as a fact about Killing vectors: there exist three spacelike Killing fields whose commutators reproduce the algebra of rotations. That is isotropy about one point — the centre. Everything in Chapters 3.7 and 3.8 followed from it plus staticity.

Cosmology assumes two things, and the first is what makes it a different subject.

Homogeneity. There is a family of spacelike slices such that, for any two points $p$ and $q$ on one slice, some isometry of the geometry carries $p$ to $q$. Infinitesimally, that means three independent spacelike Killing fields at every point, spanning the directions along the slice. No place is special.

Isotropy about every point. At each point of a slice there are three further Killing fields vanishing at that point and rotating the directions around it, again with the algebra of rotations. No direction is special, and this holds wherever you stand, not merely at a centre.

Six Killing fields, then, where the Schwarzschild solution had four (three rotations and one time translation). Note what has been traded. The star's geometry has a timelike Killing field and isotropy at a single point; the universe's has isotropy at every point and — as §6 proves — no timelike Killing field at all. The two assumptions taken together are called the cosmological principle, and the whole of this chapter is the consequence of assuming it.

One clarification, because anyone who has done Part II will already be uneasy. Isotropy about every point does not follow from isotropy about one, and homogeneity does not follow from isotropy either; a cone is homogeneous along its axis without being isotropic, and a spherically symmetric star is isotropic about its centre and homogeneous nowhere. Isotropy about every point does imply homogeneity, and that is the version usually assumed, but stating the two separately keeps visible which one each later step uses.

1.2 · What is actually measured

⚑ The evidence, quoted, and its precision

The cosmological principle is an assumption supported by measurement, not a theorem. Three observations are quoted here and none is derived in this book.

The microwave background. The sky is filled with thermal radiation at $2.7255\ \mathrm{K}$. After one feature is removed, its temperature is the same in every direction to about one part in $10^{5}$. That removed feature is a dipole — one side of the sky hotter, the opposite side cooler, by about one part in $10^{3}$ — and it is not a failure of isotropy but a Doppler shift from our own motion at roughly $370\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ relative to the frame in which the radiation is isotropic, computed with Chapter 2.5 §7's formula. The residual $10^{-5}$ is the strongest quantitative statement of isotropy anybody has.

Galaxy surveys. Counting galaxies in boxes and comparing boxes, the fluctuation in the count falls as the boxes grow, and by about $100\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ across it is at the per-cent level. Below that scale the distribution is emphatically lumpy — filaments, clusters, voids.

What isotropy about us does not give. A universe isotropic about one point is Schwarzschild-like, not homogeneous. Getting from "isotropic about us" to "isotropic about everybody" needs the assumption that we occupy no special place, which is an assumption, and its ⚑ observational support is indirect: the microwave background as seen scattered off distant galaxy clusters looks isotropic from their position too.

Say plainly where the principle fails. It is false at the scale of your body, of the Earth, of the Solar System, and of the Galaxy, all of which are conspicuous departures from a uniform fluid. The object being described in this chapter is an average, taken over regions large enough that the lumps stop mattering, and the equations below govern that average and nothing smaller. Section 5.5 returns to this, because the commonest error in reading cosmology is applying a result derived for the average to something bound and small.

1.3 · The preferred slicing, and why it is not a betrayal of Part II

Homogeneity picks out a family of slices — those on which conditions are the same everywhere — and therefore a preferred notion of simultaneity and a preferred time coordinate. After six chapters insisting that no observer's slicing is privileged, that deserves an explicit answer.

The answer is that the privilege belongs to a solution, not to the theory. Nothing in the field equations distinguishes a slicing; what distinguishes one here is the matter, which happens to be distributed in a way that is uniform on one family of slices and not on others. A room full of air at rest picks out a frame too, and no one takes that as evidence against relativity. The invariant content of the statement is that the matter has a four-velocity field $u^{\mu}$, and "cosmic time" is proper time measured along it. Any observer may use any coordinates; the ones below are chosen because they make the symmetry visible, exactly as Chapter 3.7's were.

In plain terms 3.9.1

Every geometry in this part so far has been built around a lump: a star, a hole, a mass with empty space around it. The largest object there is has no outside, so the method must be turned inside out, and what replaces the lump is an assumption about how boring the universe is. Two statements do the work. There is no special place, meaning the geometry can be slid in any direction without changing what anyone would measure. And there is no special direction, meaning it can be turned about any axis with the same result. In the language of the last few chapters these are directions of dragging along which the distance rule is unchanged, six of them.

Two honesty notes belong with the assumption rather than after it. It is an assumption, supported by evidence and not proved: the oldest light in the sky has the same temperature in every direction to one part in a hundred thousand, and surveys stop looking clumpy once the boxes being compared are large enough. And it is plainly false on small scales, since you are not a smooth fluid and neither is the galaxy. What is being described is an average taken over regions vast enough that the lumps stop mattering, which is a decision about what question is being asked rather than a fact about the world.

2 · The FLRW metric

Announce the destination. We convert the two assumptions into a line element with one unknown function in it. The time part is fixed immediately; the spatial part requires showing that a homogeneous, isotropic three-geometry has constant curvature, and then solving one separable equation to find every such geometry. There are exactly three, up to scale.

2.1 · The time part, fixed by inspection

Use the matter's own four-velocity to build coordinates, as §1.3 described. Label each homogeneous slice by the proper time $t$ read on a clock carried by the matter, and give the matter fixed spatial coordinates $x^{i}$ — legitimate because the matter has no preferred direction to move in, so if it moved relative to these labels the motion would pick one out. Such coordinates are called comoving.

Two components are then fixed with no computation.

$g_{00}=1$. A comoving worldline has $\dd x^{i}=0$, and along it $\dd s^{2}=g_{00}(\dd x^{0})^{2}=g_{00}c^{2}\dd t^{2}$. But $t$ was defined as proper time along exactly that worldline, and Chapter 3.3 §3 defines proper time by $c^{2}\dd\tau^{2}=\dd s^{2}$. So $g_{00}=1$.

$g_{0i}=0$. The three quantities $g_{0i}$ form a spatial vector at each point, and isotropy says no spatial direction is distinguishable from any other. A non-zero vector distinguishes the direction it points in. So it vanishes.

Hence the line element is $\dd s^{2}=c^{2}\dd t^{2}+g_{ij}\dd x^{i}\dd x^{j}$, with the spatial part negative-definite and, by homogeneity, the same geometry on every slice up to an overall time-dependent factor. Write that factor out:

$$ \dd s^{2} \;=\; c^{2}\dd t^{2} \;-\; a^{2}(t)\;\dd\ell^{2}, \qquad \dd\ell^{2} \;=\; \tilde g_{ij}(x)\,\dd x^{i}\dd x^{j}, $$

with $\tilde g_{ij}$ a fixed positive-definite three-geometry carrying no time dependence and $a(t)\gt0$ the scale factor. That the time dependence can be pulled out as a single overall factor is homogeneity doing its work: if two slices differed by anything other than an overall scale, comparing them would define a position-dependent quantity, and there are no position-dependent quantities here.

2.2 · Which three-geometries qualify

Now the only real work in the section. We need every three-geometry that is homogeneous and isotropic. Do it in two steps, and note that the first step is an argument about a matrix, not about geometry.

Step 1 — isotropy forces the Ricci tensor to be a multiple of the metric. In three dimensions the Riemann tensor has $3^{2}(3^{2}-1)/12=6$ independent components by Chapter 3.4 §5.2's count, and the Ricci tensor $\tilde R_{ij}$, being symmetric, has exactly six as well: in three dimensions the Ricci tensor carries the whole of the curvature and discards nothing. So it is enough to constrain $\tilde R_{ij}$.

At a chosen point, $\tilde R_{ij}$ is a real symmetric $3\times3$ matrix, and by isotropy it must be unchanged by every rotation about that point. Chapter 0.5 §6's spectral theorem says a real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues. If two eigenvalues differed, the corresponding eigenspaces would be different, and rotating the eigenvectors of one into those of another would change the matrix — that is, the eigenvector directions would be distinguishable, which is exactly what isotropy forbids. So all three eigenvalues are equal and

$$ \tilde R_{ij} \;=\; 2K\,\tilde g_{ij}, $$

the factor $2$ being a convention chosen to make what follows tidy. Homogeneity then says $K$ is the same number at every point: it is built from the geometry, and no place is special.

Step 2 — solve . Isotropy about the origin lets us use exactly Chapter 3.7 §2's ansatz, minus the time part: spherical symmetry about a point plus the areal-radius convention — label spheres so that the sphere carrying label $r$ has area $4\pi r^{2}$ — gives

$$ \dd\ell^{2} \;=\; \ee^{2\beta(r)}\,\dd r^{2} \;+\; r^{2}\big(\dd\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\dd\varphi^{2}\big), $$

with a single unknown function $\beta(r)$. The same caution Chapter 3.7 §2 gave applies verbatim: $r$ is a label chosen for the area of a sphere, not the distance from the origin, and the two differ by exactly the factor $\ee^{\beta}$.

Grind box A computes the Ricci tensor of . Its $rr$ component is $2\beta'/r$, so reads

$$ \frac{2\beta'}{r} \;=\; 2K\,\ee^{2\beta}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \ee^{-2\beta}\,\dv{\beta}{r} \;=\; K\,r. $$

That is a separable first-order equation, Chapter 0.8 §2.1. Integrate both sides:

$$ -\tfrac12\,\ee^{-2\beta} \;=\; \tfrac12 K r^{2} + C \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ee^{-2\beta} \;=\; -Kr^{2} - 2C. $$

One constant, one physical condition, exactly as in Chapter 3.7. Here the condition is regularity at the origin: at $r=0$ the geometry must look like ordinary flat space to a local observer, since a deficit there would be a conical point and would make the origin a special place. That requires $\ee^{2\beta}\to1$ as $r\to0$, so $-2C=1$, and

$$ \boxed{\;\dd\ell^{2} \;=\; \frac{\dd r^{2}}{1-Kr^{2}} \;+\; r^{2}\,\dd\Omega^{2},\qquad \dd\Omega^{2}\equiv\dd\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\dd\varphi^{2}.\;} $$

Grind box A also checks the $\theta\theta$ component of and finds it satisfied by the same $\beta$, which is not automatic: two equations, one unknown function, and the system is consistent. That consistency is isotropy paying for itself.

Grind box A — the Ricci tensor of the isotropic three-geometry, and the consistency check

Metric $\tilde g_{ij}=\mathrm{diag}\big(\ee^{2\beta},\,r^{2},\,r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\big)$ in coordinates $(r,\theta,\varphi)$, all signs positive because this is the three-geometry itself and not its embedding in . From Chapter 3.3's formula, the non-zero connection coefficients are

$$ \tilde\Gamma^{r}{}_{rr}=\beta', \quad \tilde\Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}=-r\ee^{-2\beta}, \quad \tilde\Gamma^{r}{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-r\ee^{-2\beta}\sin^{2}\theta, $$ $$ \tilde\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}=\tilde\Gamma^{\varphi}{}_{r\varphi}=\frac1r, \quad \tilde\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta, \quad \tilde\Gamma^{\varphi}{}_{\theta\varphi}=\cot\theta. $$

Feeding these through Chapter 3.4's definition of the Riemann tensor and contracting gives a diagonal Ricci tensor with

$$ \tilde R_{rr} \;=\; \frac{2\beta'}{r}, \qquad \tilde R_{\theta\theta} \;=\; \ee^{-2\beta}\big(r\beta' + \ee^{2\beta} - 1\big), \qquad \tilde R_{\varphi\varphi} \;=\; \tilde R_{\theta\theta}\sin^{2}\theta. $$

The check. With $\ee^{2\beta}=(1-Kr^{2})^{-1}$ we have $\beta'=Kr/(1-Kr^{2})$, so $r\beta'=Kr^{2}/(1-Kr^{2})$ and $\ee^{2\beta}-1=Kr^{2}/(1-Kr^{2})$. Adding and multiplying by $\ee^{-2\beta}=1-Kr^{2}$,

$$ \tilde R_{\theta\theta} \;=\; \big(1-Kr^{2}\big)\cdot\frac{2Kr^{2}}{1-Kr^{2}} \;=\; 2Kr^{2} \;=\; 2K\,\tilde g_{\theta\theta}. \;\checkmark $$

(Verified symbolically for the general $\beta$, and the resulting metric checked to satisfy the full four-index condition $\tilde R_{ijkl}=K(\tilde g_{ik}\tilde g_{jl}-\tilde g_{il}\tilde g_{jk})$, with three-dimensional Ricci scalar $\tilde R=6K$.)

2.3 · The metric, and what the scale factor is not

Rescale the radial label to absorb the size of $K$. If $K\neq0$, put $\bar r=\sqrt{\abs K}\,r$ and move the factor $\abs{K}^{-1/2}$ into $a$; the geometry is unchanged and the constant is reduced to its sign. Dropping the bar and calling the sign $k$,

$$ \boxed{\;\dd s^{2} \;=\; c^{2}\dd t^{2} \;-\; a^{2}(t)\left[\frac{\dd r^{2}}{1-kr^{2}} \;+\; r^{2}\big(\dd\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\dd\varphi^{2}\big)\right], \qquad k\in\{-1,0,+1\}.\;} $$

This is the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric. One function, $a(t)$, and one discrete choice, $k$; everything else was forced. The curvature of a spatial slice at time $t$ is $K=k/a^{2}$, so a positively curved universe has three-dimensional Ricci scalar $6/a^{2}$ and grows flatter as it expands.

Three warnings about $a$, because all three are commonly got wrong.

It is not the radius of the universe. For $k=+1$ the slices are three-spheres and $a$ is their radius; for $k=0$ and $k=-1$ they are infinite in extent and there is no radius for $a$ to be.

Its absolute value carries no information when $k=0$. Multiplying $a$ by any constant while dividing every $r$ by the same constant leaves unchanged. Only the ratio $a(t_{1})/a(t_{2})$ is measurable — which is exactly the quantity §5 shows a redshift delivers. For $k=\pm1$ the rescaling freedom was already spent normalising $k$ to $\pm1$, so there $a$ does have an absolute meaning.

It is not a substance and it does not stretch anything. $a$ is a component of a metric. Section 5.3 makes this precise and §5.5 says what does not expand.

2.4 · Flat is not the same as infinite

Chapter 3.2 §1.1 said that whether the universe closes up on itself is "a question to be answered rather than assumed" and named this chapter as where it would be asked seriously. Chapter 3.4 §8's flagged converse and Chapter 3.5 §4.3 both pointed here too. Here is the answer, and it is a distinction rather than a number.

Everything derived above is local. Equation was obtained by solving a differential equation for the metric components, and a differential equation knows only about a neighbourhood. The sign $k$ is a statement about curvature, measurable in principle by summing the angles of a large enough triangle. It is not a statement about whether space is finite.

The counterexample is one Chapter 3.4 §8 pointed here for. Take flat $k=0$ space and identify $x\sim x+L$ in each of the three directions. Every local measurement is unchanged — the metric is still $\delta_{ij}$, the curvature is still zero, triangles still have angles summing to $\pi$ — and the resulting space is a three-torus, of finite volume $L^{3}$. Travel far enough in one direction and you return to where you started. Chapter 3.3's Problem 1 built the two-dimensional version of the same mismatch, a cone, and observed that flatness away from the apex did not make it a plane; the same mismatch between local and global appears here at cosmic scale, and it is the fifth time this book has met it.

So the measurement reported in §4 as $\Omega_{k}\approx0$ says the curvature of space is small. It says nothing whatever about the topology, which no local measurement can reach. Settling that would take a different kind of observation altogether: one would look for the pattern an identification forces, in which a single feature appears twice on the sky because two lines of sight wrap round and meet. A null result there bounds the size of any identification from below; it cannot exclude one, and it is not what a curvature measurement reports.

In plain terms 3.9.2

Assuming so little turns out to determine almost everything. Slice the universe into moments at which conditions are the same everywhere, let each slice be a geometry with no special place and no special direction, and ask which geometries qualify. Insisting that the curvature be the same at every point reduces the whole question to one first-order equation of the kind the toolkit taught anyone to integrate, plus a demand that nothing go wrong at the origin. Its solution comes in exactly three flavours, distinguished by the sign of one constant: the curvature is positive, zero, or negative, and no other possibility exists.

What is left over is a single function of one variable, a scale factor multiplying every spatial distance at once. It is worth being clear about what that function is not. It is not the radius of anything, except in the positively curved case where a radius happens to exist, and its absolute value means nothing, since doubling it while halving every coordinate describes the same geometry. Only ratios of it at two moments are measurable.

One further separation matters, and the curvature chapter promised that this one would turn it on the universe. The sign of the curvature is a local measurement; whether space closes up on itself is not. A geometry can be flat at every point and still finite, as a cylinder rolled from a flat page is.

3 · The Friedmann equations

Announce the destination, and the route, because the structure of this section matters as much as its results. We compute the Einstein tensor of , set it equal to the perfect fluid of Chapter 3.6 §1.3, and read off two equations. The $00$ component gives one directly. The $11$ component gives a second, but only after the first has been substituted into it — and that is not an inconvenience, it is the announcement that the two are not independent. We then show that differentiating the first and subtracting a multiple of the second leaves exactly the fluid's own equation of motion, which is Chapter 3.4 §7's contracted Bianchi identity wearing cosmological clothes. Finally we perturb the one static solution and watch it fall over.

3.1 · The $00$ component, and Friedmann I

Grind box B does the computation. Its result, in the primed notation of the conventions, is

$$ G_{00} \;=\; \frac{3\big(k+a'^{2}\big)}{a^{2}}, \qquad a'\equiv\dv{a}{x^{0}}\;=\;\frac{\dot a}{c}. $$
Grind box B — the connection, the Ricci tensor and the Einstein tensor for FLRW

Coordinates $(x^{0},r,\theta,\varphi)$ with $x^{0}=ct$, metric $g_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}\big(1,\,-a^{2}/(1-kr^{2}),\,-a^{2}r^{2},\,-a^{2}r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\big)$. Primes are $\dd/\dd x^{0}$.

Step 1 — the connection, from Chapter 3.3's formula. Thirteen non-zero components, counting each symmetric pair once:

$$ \Gamma^{0}{}_{rr}=\frac{aa'}{1-kr^{2}}, \qquad \Gamma^{0}{}_{\theta\theta}=r^{2}aa', \qquad \Gamma^{0}{}_{\varphi\varphi}=r^{2}aa'\sin^{2}\theta, $$ $$ \Gamma^{r}{}_{0r}=\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{0\theta}=\Gamma^{\varphi}{}_{0\varphi}=\frac{a'}{a}, \qquad \Gamma^{r}{}_{rr}=\frac{kr}{1-kr^{2}}, $$ $$ \Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}=-r\big(1-kr^{2}\big), \qquad \Gamma^{r}{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-r\big(1-kr^{2}\big)\sin^{2}\theta, $$ $$ \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^{\varphi}{}_{r\varphi}=\frac1r, \qquad \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta, \qquad \Gamma^{\varphi}{}_{\theta\varphi}=\cot\theta. $$

Two of these are worth pausing on. Every $\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}$ vanishes — §5.3 uses that, and it is the statement that comoving matter is in free fall. And $\Gamma^{0}{}_{ij}=-(a'/a)\,g_{ij}$ for every spatial pair, which is the single fact §5.1's redshift derivation needs; check it against the three entries above, remembering that $g_{ij}$ carries a minus sign.

Step 2 — the Ricci tensor, by contracting Chapter 3.4's Riemann tensor. It is diagonal, with

$$ R_{00}=-\frac{3a''}{a}, \qquad R_{rr}=\frac{aa''+2a'^{2}+2k}{1-kr^{2}}, $$ $$ R_{\theta\theta}=r^{2}\big(aa''+2a'^{2}+2k\big), \qquad R_{\varphi\varphi}=R_{\theta\theta}\sin^{2}\theta, $$

and the Ricci scalar, contracting once more with $g^{\mu\nu}$, is

$$ R \;=\; -\,\frac{6\big(aa''+a'^{2}+k\big)}{a^{2}}. $$

Step 3 — the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\half Rg_{\mu\nu}$. The $00$ entry is $-3a''/a-\half\cdot\big({-6(aa''+a'^{2}+k)/a^{2}}\big)$, and the $a''$ terms cancel:

$$ G_{00}=\frac{3\big(k+a'^{2}\big)}{a^{2}}, \qquad G_{rr}=-\,\frac{k+2aa''+a'^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}, $$

with $G_{\theta\theta}$ and $G_{\varphi\varphi}$ carrying the same bracket times $-r^{2}$ and $-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta$. That the three spatial components are the same equation is isotropy again: they could not have differed.

(Verified symbolically: the connection, the full Riemann tensor, the Ricci tensor and the Einstein tensor were computed from the metric with nothing assumed, returning exactly the components above.)

The source. Chapter 3.6 §1.3 built the perfect fluid $T^{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p/c^{2})u^{\mu}u^{\nu}-pg^{\mu\nu}$. Comoving matter has $u^{\mu}=(c,0,0,0)$, which satisfies the normalisation $g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=c^{2}$ because $g_{00}=1$, and lowering gives $u_{\mu}=(c,0,0,0)$ as well. So

$$ T_{00} \;=\; \Big(\rho+\frac{p}{c^{2}}\Big)c^{2} - p\cdot1 \;=\; \rho c^{2}, \qquad T_{rr} \;=\; 0 - p\,g_{rr} \;=\; \frac{p\,a^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}. $$

Now set $G_{\mu\nu}=\kappa T_{\mu\nu}$ with $\kappa=8\pi G/c^{4}$, from Chapter 3.6 §5. The $00$ component reads $3(k+a'^{2})/a^{2}=8\pi G\rho/c^{2}$. Multiply through by $c^{2}$ and use $a'=\dot a/c$:

$$ \boxed{\;\Big(\frac{\dot a}{a}\Big)^{2} \;=\; \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3} \;-\; \frac{kc^{2}}{a^{2}}.\;} $$

That is Friedmann's first equation, and it is one component of Einstein's equation with the symmetry already imposed. Define the Hubble parameter $H(t)\equiv\dot a/a$, the fractional rate of growth, whose reciprocal is a time and whose present value is written $H_{0}$. Read term by term: the square of the growth rate is set by the density, reduced by a curvature term that is negligible when $a$ is small and can matter when $a$ is large. Nothing in it is second order in time — this is a constraint on $\dot a$ given $\rho$, not an equation of motion, which is the shape Chapter 3.6 §7.2 predicted for the $00$ component of any solution.

3.2 · The $11$ component, and why it needs Friedmann I

Take the $rr$ component with the same substitutions. From grind box B and , cancelling the common factor $(1-kr^{2})^{-1}$ and its sign,

$$ k \;+\; 2aa'' \;+\; a'^{2} \;=\; -\,\frac{8\pi G}{c^{4}}\,p\,a^{2}. $$

This is not yet a statement about $\ddot a$ alone: it contains $a'^{2}$ and $k$ as well. Divide by $a^{2}$ and isolate them,

$$ \frac{2a''}{a} \;+\; \frac{a'^{2}+k}{a^{2}} \;=\; -\,\frac{8\pi G p}{c^{4}}, $$

and now notice that the second term on the left is precisely the combination evaluates: $(a'^{2}+k)/a^{2}=8\pi G\rho/3c^{2}$. Substituting Friedmann I into the $11$ equation and rearranging,

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{\ddot a}{a} \;=\; -\,\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^{2}}\right),\;} $$

having multiplied through by $c^{2}$ to convert primes to dots. This is the acceleration equation, and three things about it deserve to be said out loud.

The substitution was not optional. Equation on its own does not give ; it gives it only in combination with . That is the first evidence that these are not two independent statements about the universe, and §3.3 turns the evidence into a proof.

The bracket is Chapter 3.6's, exactly. That chapter's §5.5 derived the Newtonian limit of the field equations as $\nabla^{2}\Phi=4\pi G(\rho+3p/c^{2})$ and observed that pressure gravitates, three times over. Here is the same bracket, with no weak-field approximation anywhere, governing the acceleration of the whole universe. Everything that chapter said about the bracket applies verbatim: radiation, with $p=\rho c^{2}/3$, gives $2\rho$ and pulls twice as hard as dust of the same energy density; a fluid with $p\lt-\rho c^{2}/3$ gives a negative bracket and pushes.

With ordinary matter the universe cannot be static, and cannot always have been expanding. For any fluid with $\rho\gt0$ and $p\ge0$, gives $\ddot a\lt0$: the expansion always decelerates. So if $\dot a\gt0$ now, then $\dot a$ was larger in the past, so the graph of $a(t)$ lies below its own tangent at the present, so extrapolating that tangent backwards reaches $a=0$ later than the true curve does. The tangent hits zero after a time $a_{0}/\dot a_{0}=1/H_{0}$, so

$$ t_{0} \;\lt\; \frac{1}{H_{0}}, $$

with $a\to0$ at a finite time in the past. That single line, requiring nothing but the sign of the bracket, is the argument that a universe of ordinary matter has a beginning, and §4.4 puts a number on it.

3.3 · Two equations, not three — and it is the Bianchi identity again

There is a third equation available, and it has not been used: the fluid must obey its own equation of motion, $\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0$, which Chapter 3.6 §1.2 promoted to curved spacetime. Working it out for , the three spatial components vanish identically — there is nowhere for momentum to flow when nothing is special — and the time component gives

$$ \boxed{\;\dot\rho \;+\; 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{p}{c^{2}}\right) \;=\; 0.\;} $$

So there are three equations, , and , for two unknown functions $a(t)$ and $\rho(t)$ once an equation of state relates $p$ to $\rho$. One equation too many, and a system in that position has no business being consistent.

Here is why it is. Take in the form $\mathrm{I}\equiv a'^{2}-\tfrac{8\pi G}{3c^{2}}\rho a^{2}+k$, which vanishes, and in the form $\mathrm{II}\equiv a''+\tfrac{4\pi G}{3c^{2}}\big(\rho+3p/c^{2}\big)a$, which also vanishes. Differentiate the first with respect to $x^{0}$ and subtract $2a'$ times the second. The whole computation is four lines and every term cancels except one:

Line 1 — differentiate I. Using the product rule on both $\rho a^{2}$ terms,

$$ \dv{\mathrm{I}}{x^{0}} \;=\; 2a'a'' \;-\; \frac{8\pi G}{3c^{2}}\Big(\rho'a^{2} + 2\rho a a'\Big). $$

$$ \dv{\mathrm{I}}{x^{0}} - 2a'\,\mathrm{II} \;=\; -\frac{8\pi G}{3c^{2}}\left[\rho'a^{2} + 2\rho aa' + \Big(\rho+\frac{3p}{c^{2}}\Big)aa'\right]. $$

$$ \dv{\mathrm{I}}{x^{0}} - 2a'\,\mathrm{II} \;=\; -\,\frac{8\pi G a^{2}}{3c^{2}}\left[\rho' + 3\frac{a'}{a}\Big(\rho+\frac{p}{c^{2}}\Big)\right], $$

So any two of the three imply the third. 3.4 · The critical density, and a convenient bookkeeping

Equation has a distinguished value of the density: the one for which the curvature term vanishes. Setting $k=0$ and solving for $\rho$ at time $t$ defines the critical density

$$ \rho_{\text{c}}(t) \;\equiv\; \frac{3H^{2}(t)}{8\pi G}, $$

and dividing through by $H^{2}$ turns it into a statement about ratios. Writing $\Omega_{i}\equiv\rho_{i}/\rho_{\text{c}}$ for each contribution to the density, and $\Omega_{k}\equiv-kc^{2}/a^{2}H^{2}$ for the curvature term treated the same way,

$$ \Omega_{\text{r}} + \Omega_{\text{m}} + \Omega_{\Lambda} + \Omega_{k} \;=\; 1. $$

This is not a new equation; it is divided by its own left-hand side. Its usefulness is that the sign of $k$ is now readable off a measurement of densities: total density above critical means $\Omega_{k}\lt0$ means $k=+1$, and below critical means $k=-1$. The subscripts anticipate §4, where the three fluids are named.

3.5 · The Einstein static universe, and the pencil on its point

Chapter 3.6 §6.3 told the history: Einstein added the cosmological term in 1917 to permit a static universe, and that chapter's Problem 4(d) argued the balance is unstable by inspecting the signs, then said in writing that "Chapter 3.9 §3 shows the same thing by integrating the equations rather than arguing from them". Here is the integration.

Find the solution. Take dust of density $\rho_{\text{m}}$, with $p_{\text{m}}=0$, plus the cosmological term, which Chapter 3.6 §6.1 showed is a fluid with $\rho_{\Lambda}=\Lambda c^{2}/8\pi G$ and $p_{\Lambda}=-\rho_{\Lambda}c^{2}$. Static means $\dot a=0$ and $\ddot a=0$, and both Friedmann equations must hold. The acceleration equation requires the bracket to vanish:

$$ 0 \;=\; \big(\rho_{\text{m}}+\rho_{\Lambda}\big) + \frac{3}{c^{2}}\big(0 - \rho_{\Lambda}c^{2}\big) \;=\; \rho_{\text{m}} - 2\rho_{\Lambda} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \rho_{\text{m}} \;=\; 2\rho_{\Lambda}. $$

Then Friedmann I with $\dot a=0$ requires $0=\tfrac{8\pi G}{3}(\rho_{\text{m}}+\rho_{\Lambda})-kc^{2}/a^{2}=8\pi G\rho_{\Lambda}-kc^{2}/a^{2}$, which forces $k=+1$ — a closed universe, with no choice in the matter — and fixes the size:

$$ a_{\text{E}} \;=\; \frac{c}{\sqrt{8\pi G\rho_{\Lambda}}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}. $$

Now perturb it. Write $a(t)=a_{\text{E}}\big(1+\varepsilon(t)\big)$ with $\abs{\varepsilon}\ll1$. Two densities respond differently, and that difference is the whole argument. The dust dilutes with volume, $\rho_{\text{m}}=2\rho_{\Lambda}(a_{\text{E}}/a)^{3}=2\rho_{\Lambda} (1-3\varepsilon)$ to first order, using $(1+\varepsilon)^{-3}\approx1-3\varepsilon$. The cosmological term does not dilute at all, because Chapter 3.6 §6.1(iii) showed its density is built from constants. Substituting both into , whose left side is $\ddot\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)\approx\ddot\varepsilon$,

$$ \begin{aligned} \ddot\varepsilon \;&=\; -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\Big[2\rho_{\Lambda}\big(1-3\varepsilon\big) + \rho_{\Lambda} - 3\rho_{\Lambda}\Big]\\[3pt] \;&=\; -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\Big[-6\rho_{\Lambda}\varepsilon\Big] \;=\; 8\pi G\rho_{\Lambda}\,\varepsilon \;=\; \Lambda c^{2}\,\varepsilon, \end{aligned} $$

the zeroth-order terms cancelling by — which is the statement that the unperturbed solution is a solution. The result is $\ddot\varepsilon=+\Lambda c^{2}\varepsilon$, with a plus sign, whose solutions are

$$ \varepsilon(t) \;=\; A\,\ee^{+t/\tau} + B\,\ee^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau \;=\; \frac{1}{c\sqrt{\Lambda}}. $$

Any disturbance grows exponentially. Compare the sign with the one that would have given $\ddot\varepsilon=-\omega^{2}\varepsilon$ and oscillation: nothing here restores. Physically the runaway is easy to state — squeeze the universe slightly and the dust density rises while the cosmological term does not, so gravity wins and the squeeze accelerates; expand it slightly and the dust thins while the cosmological term holds steady, so the repulsion wins and the expansion runs away. That is Chapter 3.6's Problem 4(d) in words, and in symbols.

Put a number on it with the measured $\Lambda=1.1\times10^{-52}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}$ from Chapter 3.6 §6.3. Then $\sqrt{\Lambda}=1.05\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and

$$ \tau \;=\; \frac{1}{\big(2.998\times10^{8}\big)\big(1.05\times10^{-26}\big)} \;=\; 3.2\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{s} \;\approx\; 10\ \mathrm{Gyr}. $$

A disturbance of one part in $10^{9}$ therefore grows to order one in about $20\tau$, some $200\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ — long, and not remotely long enough, since the universe is a fifteenth of that age and its density fluctuations were never that small.

A check worth doing, because it tests the whole scheme

Friedmann I was not used in the perturbation, so it is available as a check. Expand $\mathrm{I}=0$ about the static solution with the same substitutions. The terms of order $\varepsilon^{0}$ cancel — that is . At second order it gives $\dot\varepsilon^{2}=\Lambda c^{2}\varepsilon^{2}$, which is exactly what the growing mode $\varepsilon\propto\ee^{t/\tau}$ of satisfies. In plain terms 3.9.3

Two symmetries and one fluid leave the field equations with almost nothing to say, and what they do say is two equations where there appear to be three. The timekeeping entry gives a first-order statement: the square of the fractional growth rate equals the density, up to constants, less a curvature term. That is not an accident, and you have met it before in other clothes. The identity that forced the shape of the field equations three chapters ago says the geometry side has no divergence, so the matter side cannot either, so the matter's own equation of motion is contained in the field equations rather than added to them. The pencil on its point arrives at the end. There is a solution in which the universe sits still, matter's pull exactly cancelled by the repulsive term, and it is why that term was invented; disturbing it by any amount produces growth doubling on a timescale of billions of years.

4 · The three fluids, and what each does to $a(t)$

Announce the destination. Everything a universe does is decided by one number per constituent: the ratio of its pressure to its energy density. We put that ratio into the fluid equation and get how each constituent dilutes; put the result into Friedmann I and get $a(t)$ for each acting alone; and then find that because the three dilute at different rates, the order in which they take charge is fixed no matter how much of each there is.

4.3 · Which fluid is in charge, and when the expansion accelerates

A real universe contains all three, and the total density is the sum. Because they dilute at different rates, the sum is dominated by different terms at different times, and the order is fixed:

$$ \rho(a) \;=\; \rho_{\text{r},0}\Big(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\Big)^{4} + \rho_{\text{m},0}\Big(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\Big)^{3} + \rho_{\Lambda}. $$

As $a\to0$ the steepest term wins, so radiation dominates the early universe whatever the proportions are. As $a\to\infty$ the shallowest wins, so the cosmological term dominates the late universe whatever the proportions are, provided it is not zero. Matter has its era in between. That running order is not an observation; it is arithmetic, and no measurement could have come out otherwise.

The curvature term is worth putting on the same footing. Written as a contribution to it behaves as $a^{-2}$ — between matter and $\Lambda$ — which is why §4.2's $k=0$ assumption is the right first case: whatever $k$ is, at small enough $a$ the curvature term is negligible beside radiation and matter.

When does the expansion accelerate? Read : $\ddot a$ has the sign of $-(\rho+3p/c^{2})$, and with $p=w\rho c^{2}$ that bracket is $\rho(1+3w)$. So a single fluid accelerates the expansion exactly when

$$ w \;\lt\; -\tfrac13, $$

which is precisely Chapter 3.6 §5.5's condition for a substance to repel rather than attract, arriving here with no weak-field approximation. That chapter's figure had a shaded region below $w=-1/3$ and said the observed universe sits at $w\approx-1$; here is what sitting there does. Note also what says about the other end of the table: radiation, with $w=+1/3$, has a bracket of $2\rho$ and decelerates the expansion twice as hard as dust of the same energy density. That is Chapter 3.6 §5.5's "a gas of light gravitates twice as strongly", which that section flagged as something this chapter would need. It is needed here.

4.5 · The whole history, on one plot

The three eras are hard to see on a linear plot, because the scale factor changes by a factor of $10^{9}$ over the interval where anything interesting happens. On logarithmic axes each single-fluid solution of is a straight line of slope $2/3(1+w)$, so the eras appear as straight segments and the transitions as bends. The figure integrates numerically with all four terms of present, and reports the local slope $\dd\ln a/\dd\ln t$ at a marker you can drag.

Familiar ground — reading a mechanism off a slope, and where it breaks

The trick the figure uses is one you use in a different subject. Plot the logarithm of a plasma concentration against time and a straight line means first-order elimination, with the slope giving the rate constant; a straight line on log–log axes instead would mean a power law, with the slope giving the exponent. The mathematics is identical here. Each single-fluid solution is a power $a\propto t^{n}$, so $\ln a=n\ln t+\text{const}$ is a straight line of slope $n$, and reading $n=0.500$ or $n=0.667$ off the plot identifies which fluid is in charge without solving anything. That is why the figure is drawn on these axes and not on linear ones.

Where the analogy breaks, and it breaks in a way worth knowing. In pharmacokinetics the rate constant is a property of one elimination pathway, and a two-compartment model shows two straight segments because two distinct processes act in sequence. Here nothing acts in sequence: all three fluids are present at every moment, and is a sum, not a switch. The slope is therefore never exactly $\tfrac12$ or $\tfrac23$ — it is a weighted compromise that approaches those values asymptotically, which is why the marker reads $0.653$ rather than $0.667$ at $a=10^{-2}$. A straight-looking segment on this plot is an approximation whose error you can compute, not a régime with a boundary.

In plain terms 3.9.4

Everything a universe does is decided by what is in it, and the deciding quantity is the ratio of pressure to energy density. Cold matter has none worth mentioning and thins out as its volume grows, which is the arithmetic of counting: the same particles, more room. A gas of light thins out one factor faster, and that extra factor is precisely the stretching of each wavelength, arriving here as an energy loss. The energy of empty space, by the argument of three chapters ago, cannot thin out at all, because it is built from constants.

Feed each into the growth equation and the answers are three lines of algebra apiece, because the problem has fallen apart into independent pieces again. Light alone gives growth like the square root of time, matter alone like the two-thirds power, and the vacuum term alone gives growth by a fixed factor per unit time, without end. Since the three dilute at different rates, whichever falls off fastest wins early and whichever falls off slowest wins late, so the history has a fixed running order regardless of the amounts: light, then matter, then the term that does not dilute.

The last of those deserves stating plainly. Growth by a fixed factor per unit time never slows, so a universe containing any positive amount of that term is eventually dominated by it, whatever else is in it.

5 · Redshift, distance, and what "expanding" does not mean

Announce the destination. We follow a photon along an actual null geodesic of and find how the frequency a comoving observer measures changes. Then we do it a second way, by counting wave crests, and the two agree. Then — and this occupies more space than the derivation, because it repairs more damage — we say what the result does not mean.

5.1 · The redshift, from the geodesic equation

A photon has a four-momentum $p^{\mu}=\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda$ with $\lambda$ an affine parameter, which Chapter 3.8 §1.1 explained is what stands in for proper time on a null curve. It obeys the geodesic equation of Chapter 3.3 §8,

$$ \dv{p^{0}}{\lambda} \;+\; \Gamma^{0}{}_{\mu\nu}\,p^{\mu}p^{\nu} \;=\; 0, $$

and we need only its $0$ component. From grind box B, $\Gamma^{0}{}_{00}=0$ and $\Gamma^{0}{}_{0i}=0$, so the sum runs over spatial indices alone, and there $\Gamma^{0}{}_{ij}=-(a'/a)g_{ij}$. Hence

$$ \Gamma^{0}{}_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu} \;=\; -\,\frac{a'}{a}\,g_{ij}\,p^{i}p^{j}. $$

Now use the one property of a photon that distinguishes it. The null condition $g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}=0$ reads $(p^{0})^{2}+g_{ij}p^{i}p^{j}=0$, since $g_{00}=1$, so the spatial sum is $-\,(p^{0})^{2}$. Substituting into and then into ,

$$ \dv{p^{0}}{\lambda} \;=\; -\,\frac{a'}{a}\,\big(p^{0}\big)^{2}. $$

Trade the affine parameter for the coordinate, which is legitimate because $p^{0}=\dd x^{0}/\dd\lambda$ and $p^{0}\neq0$ for a photon that is going anywhere: by the chain rule $\dd p^{0}/\dd\lambda=(\dd p^{0}/\dd x^{0})\,p^{0}$, and one factor of $p^{0}$ cancels from each side:

$$ \frac{1}{p^{0}}\dv{p^{0}}{x^{0}} \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{a}\dv{a}{x^{0}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dd\big(\ln p^{0}\big) \;=\; -\,\dd\big(\ln a\big) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p^{0} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{a}. $$

Now convert that into something measurable, using Chapter 3.8 §5.1's rule verbatim: the energy an observer measures is the photon's momentum contracted with that observer's four-velocity. A comoving observer has $u^{\mu}=(c,0,0,0)$, so

$$ h\nu \;=\; p_{\mu}u^{\mu} \;=\; g_{00}\,p^{0}u^{0} \;=\; c\,p^{0} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{a}. $$

Defining the redshift $z$ by $1+z\equiv\lambda_{\text{obs}}/\lambda_{\text{em}}$, and using $\lambda\nu=c$ for both,

$$ \boxed{\;1+z \;=\; \frac{\nu_{\text{em}}}{\nu_{\text{obs}}} \;=\; \frac{a(t_{\text{obs}})}{a(t_{\text{em}})} \;=\; \frac{a_{0}}{a_{\text{em}}}.\;} $$

A measured redshift is a direct reading of how much the universe has grown since the light left. Light reaching us at $z=1$ set out when everything was half its present separation; the microwave background, at $z\approx1100$ ⚑ — a measurement of when the plasma recombined, which needs atomic physics Part IV has not reached and is not derived here — set out when it was a thousandth.

The same result a second way, sharing no step. Take a radial ray, $\dd\theta=\dd\varphi=0$, and set $\dd s^{2}=0$ in :

$$ c\,\dd t \;=\; \frac{a(t)\,\dd r}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \int_{t_{\text{em}}}^{t_{0}}\frac{c\,\dd t}{a(t)} \;=\; \int_{0}^{r_{\text{em}}}\frac{\dd r}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}} \;\equiv\; \chi, $$

the right-hand side being a fixed number, since the source sits at fixed comoving $r$. Send the next wave crest a time $\delta t_{\text{em}}$ later; it arrives a time $\delta t_{0}$ later; and the right-hand side of is the same for both, because it does not contain $t$ at all. Subtracting the two integrals leaves only the end contributions,

$$ \frac{\delta t_{0}}{a_{0}} \;-\; \frac{\delta t_{\text{em}}}{a_{\text{em}}} \;=\; 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\nu_{\text{em}}}{\nu_{0}} \;=\; \frac{\delta t_{0}}{\delta t_{\text{em}}} \;=\; \frac{a_{0}}{a_{\text{em}}}, $$

which is again. The first route used the geodesic equation, the connection and a four-velocity; the second used only the line element and the observation that $\chi$ does not depend on when you leave. Two derivations, one answer.

5.2 · It is not a Doppler shift, and here is the evidence

Look at what is absent from . No velocity appears. No relative speed of emitter and observer appears. Nothing about the expansion history between the two events appears either: two universes with wildly different $a(t)$ in between, agreeing only on the ratio of the endpoints, produce identical redshifts. A Doppler shift is none of those things — Chapter 2.5 §7's formula is a function of one relative velocity and nothing else.

The confusion has a respectable origin, and it is worth deriving so that its limits are visible. For a nearby source, expand $a(t_{\text{em}})$ about the present: $a_{\text{em}}\approx a_{0}\big[1-H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{\text{em}})\big]$. For small light-travel times the distance is $d\approx c(t_{0}-t_{\text{em}})$, so from ,

$$ 1+z \;\approx\; \frac{1}{1-H_{0}d/c} \;\approx\; 1+\frac{H_{0}d}{c} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad cz \;\approx\; H_{0}\,d. $$

That is Hubble's law, and the left-hand side has the units of a velocity, which is why it is universally reported as one. But is the first term of a Taylor expansion of , and the resemblance to a Doppler formula is a fact about first-order expansions rather than about physics. At $z=7$ there is no velocity of $7c$; there is no velocity at all.

⚠ Three different reasons a spectral line can shift, and they are not the same effect

This book has now derived three, and keeping them apart is worth a paragraph each.

Doppler (Chapter 2.5 §7): emitter and receiver in relative motion in flat spacetime. The shift is a function of their relative velocity, computed in one frame, and the effect is symmetric in a way that requires the relativity of simultaneity to unpick.

Gravitational (Chapter 3.8 §5): emitter and receiver both static in a static geometry, at different values of $g_{00}$. The shift is $\sqrt{g_{00}(\text{em})/g_{00}(\text{rec})}$ and the derivation needs a timelike Killing vector, so that a conserved charge exists to compare against each observer's local clock.

Cosmological (here): emitter and receiver both comoving, both in free fall, with $g_{00}=1$ at both ends and no timelike Killing vector anywhere. The shift is the ratio of scale factors. Notice that the middle derivation is unavailable here for exactly the reason §6 is about — there is no conserved photon energy to compare against a local clock, so route (a) of Chapter 3.8 §5.1 has nothing to be constant. What replaced it above was the geodesic equation itself, which needs no symmetry.

5.3 · Galaxies are not moving through space

Two facts, both computed rather than asserted.

Comoving worldlines are geodesics. Grind box B recorded that every $\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}$ vanishes. The geodesic equation for a curve with $u^{\mu}=(c,0,0,0)$ is $\dd u^{i}/\dd\tau+\Gamma^{i}{}_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=c^{2}\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}=0$, satisfied identically. A galaxy placed at rest in comoving coordinates stays at those coordinates for ever, in free fall, with nothing acting on it. It is not being dragged, pushed or carried.

What grows is the conversion from coordinates to distance. Define the proper distance to a comoving source at one instant of cosmic time by integrating along the slice, which means setting $\dd t=0$ in :

$$ d(t) \;=\; a(t)\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\dd r'}{\sqrt{1-kr'^{2}}} \;=\; a(t)\,\chi, $$

with $\chi$ the fixed comoving distance of . Differentiating, since $\chi$ is a constant,

$$ \dot d \;=\; \dot a\,\chi \;=\; \frac{\dot a}{a}\,d \;=\; H\,d. $$

This is Hubble's law again, and now it is exact rather than a first-order expansion, because it is a statement about proper distance at fixed $t$ rather than about a redshift. And it has a consequence that stops people: for $d\gt c/H$, gives $\dot d\gt c$. With $H_{0}=67.4$, that threshold is

$$ \frac{c}{H_{0}} \;=\; \frac{2.998\times10^{8}}{2.184\times10^{-18}} \;=\; 1.37\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m} \;=\; 4.45\ \mathrm{Gpc}, $$

and galaxies are catalogued well beyond it.

Why that violates nothing. Chapter 2.3 established a speed limit, and Chapter 3.2 §5 established what kind of statement it is. A velocity is a vector in the tangent space at one event. Comparing two velocities means comparing two vectors, and two vectors at different points of a manifold live in different vector spaces with no canonical map between them — that was the single most important sentence of Chapter 3.2, and this is where it is spent. The quantity $\dot d$ in is not anybody's velocity: it is the rate of change of an integral taken along a curve through many tangent spaces. No local measurement anywhere finds anything moving past anything else faster than $c$, and that is the statement the speed limit makes.

5.4 · The balloon, and the exact point where it fails

The standard picture is dots on an inflating balloon. It gets one thing exactly right, and an analogy this book keeps has to be shown to be tight where it is tight.

What is right, and it is a theorem rather than a resemblance. Two dots on a sphere of radius $R(t)$ at fixed angular separation $\vartheta$ are a geodesic distance $s=R\vartheta$ apart, so $\dot s=\dot R\vartheta=(\dot R/R)s$. Every dot sees every other receding at a rate proportional to its distance, with the same constant of proportionality, and no dot is the centre. That is exactly, one dimension down, with $R$ playing the part of $a$. The surface has no edge and no special point, and both of those are properties the $k=+1$ case genuinely has.

Where it breaks, and the break is not a detail. The rubber sits in a room. That room — the three-dimensional space the balloon is embedded in — is what makes the phrase "expanding into" meaningful, and it is what makes the question "what is outside?" answerable. There is no room. Nothing in §2 referred to an embedding at any point: Chapter 3.2 §1 forbade any reference to an ambient space and built the manifold so that curvature could be measured from inside, and the whole of was obtained by solving equations for the metric components alone. A universe is not a surface in something. The moment you start asking what lies beyond the balloon you have imported an object the mathematics does not contain, and every subsequent question you ask will be unanswerable for that reason and no other.

Two smaller failures follow from the same source. The rubber is a material, with tension, which stretches things stuck to it; the scale factor is a component of a metric and stretches nothing, as §5.3 computed. And the balloon is a closed surface, so it can only ever depict the $k=+1$ case; for $k=0$ and $k=-1$ the slices are infinite and there is no balloon to inflate.

5.5 · What does not expand

The commonest error in reading this chapter is applying its results to something small. Everything above was derived from the assumption of §1, and that assumption is violated by many orders of magnitude inside anything bound. The mean density of the Galaxy is around $10^{5}$ times critical; of the Solar System, vastly more; of your body, about $10^{29}$ times.

What governs a bound system is not but the solution Chapter 3.7 derived, and Chapter 3.7 §4's Birkhoff theorem is what licenses saying so: the geometry outside a spherical mass is Schwarzschild whatever the rest of the universe is doing. Atoms, rulers, planetary orbits and galaxies do not grow. ⚑ The cosmological term does exert a small outward tendency on a bound orbit — that much is quoted, since it needs a solution this book has not built — and Chapter 3.6's Problem 4(b) computed where it starts to matter — around $100\ \mathrm{pc}$ for a solar mass, which is far outside any bound system. Below that scale the effect exists and is unmeasurable.

In plain terms 3.9.5

Light from far away is followed the way the previous chapters followed it, by writing down the path a flash actually takes and asking what a receiver measures along it. Done here the answer is startlingly clean. The measured frequency falls in exact proportion to the scale factor, so the stretch of a spectral line is the ratio of the scale factor now to its value when the light set out. Distant spectra arrive reddened, so that ratio exceeds one and the scale factor is growing. No velocity appears anywhere in the derivation, and none appears in the answer.

Galaxies at rest with respect to the smoothed-out matter are in free fall and keep the same coordinates forever; nothing pushes them apart and nothing is moving through anything. What grows is the conversion between coordinate differences and measured distances. Distant galaxies therefore have separations growing faster than light, which breaks no rule: the speed limit compares two things at the same place, and velocities at different places cannot be compared without carrying one to the other along a path.

The rubber balloon gets the important part right and one thing badly wrong. Dots on it do recede from one another with no dot at the centre, and the surface has no edge. But the rubber sits in a room, and the room is what makes expanding into anything mean anything. There is no room.

6 · Energy is not conserved, and why that is Noether rather than a paradox

This is the section three separate chapters wrote down in advance, and the whole of it is one theorem applied honestly. Announce the destination. Chapter 1.4 proved that a conserved quantity is the output of an argument whose input is a continuous symmetry. Chapter 3.5 §9 showed that in a curved spacetime the symmetry in question is a Killing vector, and that the two theorems are one theorem. We now prove that an expanding universe containing matter has no timelike Killing vector. The machine is therefore handed nothing and returns nothing, and the photon gas loses energy with nothing to catch it.

6.1 · What the theorem requires, restated

Chapter 1.4 §2 proved Noether's theorem and its §3 specialised it: if the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on time, a quantity — the energy — is constant along every solution. That chapter's §4 was devoted to what happens when a hypothesis fails, and its §4.3, headed "An expanding universe: no conserved total energy", applied the failure to this case in advance and said in writing: "Chapter 3.5 makes this precise: the symmetries of a spacetime are its Killing vector fields, a conserved energy requires a timelike one, and the expanding solutions of Chapter 3.9 do not have one."

Chapter 3.5 §9 supplied exactly that machinery. Its §9.1 proved in four lines that $Q=\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}$ is constant along every geodesic when $\xi$ is Killing, and its §9.2 then showed that running Chapter 1.4's theorem on the same system returns the same charge, with the change in the Lagrangian under the shift turning out to be the Lie derivative of the metric. The geometric condition and the mechanical one are the same condition. Chapter 3.7 §5 spent that result to get $E$ and $L$ for a planet; Chapter 3.8 §5.1 spent it to get the gravitational redshift. Both spent it on a geometry with a timelike Killing vector.

So the question is not philosophical. It is: does the FLRW metric have a timelike Killing vector?

6.2 · The obvious candidate, and then the proof that no candidate works

The obvious candidate fails immediately. Try $\xi^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)$, the vector pointing along cosmic time. Chapter 3.5 §8.1 gives the Lie derivative of the metric as $(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g)_{\mu\nu}=\xi^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}+g_{\lambda\nu}\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}+g_{\mu\lambda}\partial_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda}$, and with constant components the last two terms vanish, leaving

$$ \big(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g\big)_{ij} \;=\; \partial_{0}\,g_{ij} \;=\; \partial_{0}\big(-a^{2}\tilde g_{ij}\big) \;=\; -2aa'\,\tilde g_{ij}, $$

which vanishes if and only if $a'=0$. Time translation is a symmetry exactly when the universe is static, and not otherwise. That is Chapter 3.5 §8.1's practical test — a coordinate the metric does not mention gives a Killing vector — reading the other way: the metric mentions $t$, through $a(t)$, so it does not.

But that rules out one candidate, not all of them. A Killing vector need not have constant components, and a timelike one need not point along the cosmic-time coordinate. Here is the general argument, and it takes three steps.

Step 1 — a Killing flow cannot change any scalar built from the geometry. Chapter 3.5 §7.1 defined the flow of a vector field, and $\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g=0$ says the metric is unchanged when dragged along $\xi$'s flow. Now take any scalar $S$ computed from the metric by a fixed recipe applied at each point — the Ricci scalar $R$ of Chapter 3.4 §6, say, or the Kretschmann scalar of Chapter 3.8 §6.4. Drag the whole configuration along the flow by a parameter $s$. The recipe is the same recipe everywhere and it is applied to a metric that the dragging left alone, so it returns the value it returned before. The scalar's value at the dragged point therefore equals its value at the original point, for every $s$, and differentiating at $s=0$,

$$ \xi^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}S \;=\; 0 \qquad\text{for every Killing vector }\xi\text{ and every geometric scalar }S. $$

Step 2 — produce a scalar that changes with time. Take the trace of the field equation. In four dimensions $g^{\mu\nu}G_{\mu\nu}=R-\half R\cdot4=-R$, and Chapter 3.6's $T=g_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}=\rho c^{2}-3p$ gives the other side, so

$$ R \;=\; -\,\kappa T \;=\; -\,\frac{8\pi G}{c^{2}}\left(\rho - \frac{3p}{c^{2}}\right). $$

For dust this is $R=-8\pi G\rho/c^{2}$ with $\rho\propto a^{-3}$. For radiation the bracket vanishes identically, so $R=0$ and this particular scalar says nothing — but the Kretschmann scalar of Chapter 3.8 §6.4, computed from grind box B's connection in exactly the way that chapter computed it for Schwarzschild, is $K=\tfrac32\,(ct)^{-4}$ in the flat radiation era, which is not constant either. Take the dust case, which is the universe from $z\approx3400$ until recently. Then $R$ is a strictly monotonic function of $t$ alone whenever $\dot a\neq0$: it is large early and small late, and it takes a different value on every slice.

Step 3 — combine. Apply with $S=R$. Since $R$ depends on $x^{0}$ alone, $\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}R=\xi^{0}\,\dd R/\dd x^{0}$, and the derivative is non-zero, so

$$ \xi^{0} \;=\; 0 \qquad\text{everywhere.} $$

A vector with no time component has norm $g_{\mu\nu}\xi^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}=g_{ij}\xi^{i}\xi^{j}$, and the spatial block of is negative-definite, so that norm is negative unless $\xi$ vanishes. By Chapter 2.3 §4's classification, every Killing vector of an expanding dust universe is spacelike. There are six of them, as §1.1 assumed, and not one of them is timelike. $\blacksquare$

⚠ The exception, stated rather than buried

Step 2 is where the argument can fail, and it fails for exactly one fluid. If the only content is the cosmological term, then $p=-\rho c^{2}$ and the bracket in is $\rho+3\rho=4\rho_{\Lambda}$, a constant. Then $R=-4\Lambda$ everywhere, the scalar argument gives no information, and one should suspect the conclusion is false. It is false. Pure de Sitter space, $a\propto\ee^{H_{\Lambda}t}$ with $k=0$, does have a timelike Killing vector in the region $\bar r\lt c/H_{\Lambda}$, and Problem 3 exhibits it by an explicit change of coordinates. Minkowski spacetime and the Einstein static universe of §3.5 are the other exceptions, for the same reason.

That is worth more than the theorem it qualifies. It shows the argument of §6.2 is doing real work rather than restating a slogan: expanding is not by itself what kills the conserved energy. What kills it is expanding while containing something that dilutes, so that a geometric scalar takes different values at different times. The universe we live in contains such things, in quantity.

6.3 · Therefore there is no conserved energy, and this is not a defect

Put the pieces together in the order they were proved. A conserved energy is the output of Chapter 1.4's theorem. That theorem's input is a continuous symmetry of the action. Chapter 3.5 §9 identified the relevant symmetry as a timelike Killing vector. Section 6.2 proved there is none. So no conserved energy can be produced this way. And the theorem runs backwards as well, which is what turns that into a result rather than a shrug: Chapter 1.4 §7.2 proved the converse — a conserved $Q$ with no explicit time dependence satisfies $\{Q,H\}=0$ and therefore is a symmetry, viewed as a generator. A conserved energy would generate a time translation, that translation would be a timelike Killing vector, and §6.2 proved there is none. So there is no conserved total energy of an expanding universe, and it is the converse, not the failure of the forward theorem, that says so. Even that is a statement about the Hamiltonian systems Chapter 1.4 worked with; what rules out a global energy for a whole universe is §6.5's second and third tiers. It remains true that a theorem handed no hypothesis returns no conclusion.

Chapter 1.1 §2 saw this coming, three parts ago, before Noether's theorem had even been stated: "in an expanding universe the laws are time-dependent, and the energy of the cosmological photon gas is correspondingly not conserved… A quantity whose conservation you have been taught is absolute turns out to be contingent on a symmetry that the universe does not exactly possess." That sentence is now a calculation.

Three places you will instinctively look for it, and why none of them holds it.

Not the matter. Each non-interacting fluid obeys separately, and that equation has no source term coupling one to another. Transfer would appear as such a term; there is none.

Not the gravitational field. There is no local energy density for gravity to store it in, and the reason is the equivalence principle. Chapter 3.4 §5.3 constructed locally inertial coordinates in which the metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and its first derivatives vanish at a chosen event. Any candidate local energy density built from the metric and its first derivatives, and required to vanish where there is no gravitational field at all, is therefore zero at that event in those coordinates, and a scalar that vanishes in one chart at an event vanishes in every chart there. So no such scalar exists to be non-zero anywhere.

Not "the expansion". The expansion is not an object with an energy account. Section 5.3 showed that nothing is moving, nothing is being stretched, and $a$ is a metric component.

6.5 · What does survive, in three tiers

Chapter 1.4 §4.3 set out three claims in decreasing order of confidence and marked all three as quoted forward to here. Each can now be checked.

Tier one, exactly and always true: the local statement. $\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0$ holds, and in this spacetime it is not an extra assumption but a consequence of the field equations, by §3.3. More than that: you have already written it down. The $\nu=0$ component of $\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0$ for the FLRW metric is exactly the fluid equation , up to a factor of $c^{2}$, and the spatial components vanish identically. Nothing is created or destroyed at any point.

Tier two: why that cannot be added up. Chapter 3.5 §6.4 proved a divergence identity — $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}(\sqrt{-g}V^{\mu})/\sqrt{-g}$ — which turns the covariant divergence of a vector into an ordinary derivative, so that integrating it gives a boundary term and hence a conservation law. For a tensor with a free index the same manipulation leaves a remainder:

$$ \nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\partial_{\mu}\big(\sqrt{-g}\,T^{\mu\nu}\big) \;+\; \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}T^{\mu\lambda}, $$

the extra term coming from the second index, which the connection also has to correct. Evaluate that remainder here, for $\nu=0$. Using $\Gamma^{0}{}_{ij}=-(a'/a)g_{ij}$ and $T^{ij}=-p\,g^{ij}$, so that $g_{ij}T^{ij}=-3p$,

$$ \Gamma^{0}{}_{\mu\lambda}T^{\mu\lambda} \;=\; -\,\frac{a'}{a}\,g_{ij}T^{ij} \;=\; +\,3\,\frac{a'}{a}\,p. $$

The obstruction is the pressure. Set $p=0$ and it vanishes, the divergence becomes a total derivative, and dust's energy in a comoving box is conserved — which is the first row of the table above. With pressure it does not vanish, and there is no boundary term to collect. The second obstruction is Chapter 3.2's: $\nu$ is a free index, so the thing one would like to add up over a region is a vector at each point, and vectors at different points of a manifold cannot be added.

Tier three: when a total energy does exist. ⚑ In an asymptotically flat spacetime — an isolated star, or Chapter 3.8's black hole in an otherwise empty universe — the geometry approaches Minkowski far away, the time-translation symmetry is restored there, and a conserved total energy can be defined from the behaviour of the field at infinity. It is called the ADM mass. We quote its existence and do not construct it. The expanding universe is not asymptotically flat, in the strong sense that it is not asymptotically anything: is the same everywhere, and there is no far away.

So neither slogan is right. "Energy conservation fails in general relativity" is too strong, because Chapter 3.8's black hole has a perfectly good conserved energy. "Energy is always conserved, you have to look harder" is false, because §6.2 is a proof and not an admission of ignorance. The correct statement is the one Noether's theorem hands you directly: you get a conserved energy exactly when you have a time-translation symmetry, and not otherwise.

Familiar ground — this is the first law, and here is the thing it is missing

Multiply the fluid equation by $c^{2}a^{3}V_{\text{c}}$ and regroup. The left-hand term becomes $\dd\big(\rho c^{2}a^{3}V_{\text{c}}\big)/\dd t$, which is $\dd U/\dd t$ for the comoving box; the right-hand term becomes $-p\,\dd\big(a^{3}V_{\text{c}}\big)/\dd t$, which is $-p\,\dd V/\dd t$. So is

$$ \dd U \;=\; -\,p\,\dd V, $$

the first law of thermodynamics for a reversible change with no heat added. Every step of the familiar derivation applies: the mathematics is not analogous, it is the same equation, and the three rows of the table above are what it gives for $w=0$, $w=\tfrac13$ and $w=-1$.

Where it breaks, and the break is the whole section. In thermodynamics $-p\,\dd V$ is work done on something: a piston moves, a spring compresses, a reservoir warms, and the energy is traceable to a place. Here there is no piston, because every comoving box is expanding at the same rate at the same time, so no boundary has anything on the other side of it doing anything different. There is no surroundings, because a homogeneous universe has no outside. The equation holds exactly and the story it usually carries does not hold at all. And the sign runs both ways: for the cosmological term $p$ is negative, so $\dd U$ is positive and the energy in a comoving box grows without limit, which is the same absence of a conservation law wearing its other face.

In plain terms 3.9.6

This is the oldest promise in the book, made where a stretched photon had nowhere to put the energy it lost, and the answer is not a puzzle but a theorem returning nothing when handed nothing. Conservation of energy was never a law standing on its own. It was the output of an argument whose input is a symmetry: laws that do not change with time yield a quantity that does not change with time. In an expanding universe the geometry itself changes with time, so the input is absent. Nothing has broken. A machine has been offered no raw material and has produced no product.

The absence can be established rather than asserted. A direction of dragging that leaves the geometry alone must leave alone every number computed from the geometry, and one such number is fixed by the field equations to be proportional to the density of matter, which falls as the universe grows. No such direction can therefore point along time.

The photon gas makes it concrete. Take a region expanding along with the matter and count the energy of the light inside: it falls in proportion to the stretching, with no piston, no surroundings and no reservoir to receive it. What does survive is the local statement, exactly and always: nothing is created or destroyed at any point. Adding that up over a large region is the step that fails.

This is the last section of Part III, and it does two things that no other section of this book is in a position to do. Then it tells you, in advance rather than by way of apology afterwards, what the next three parts are going to do with everything Part III has built.

7.1 · One number, owed

⚑ The entropy of a black hole, quoted and not derived

The horizon Chapter 3.8 §7 constructed has an entropy, and it is

$$ S \;=\; \frac{k_{\text{B}}\,c^{3}}{4G\hbar}\;A \;=\; \frac{k_{\text{B}}}{4}\,\frac{A}{\ell_{\text{P}}^{2}}, \qquad \ell_{\text{P}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^{3}}}, $$

where $A=4\pi r_{s}^{2}$ is the area of the horizon and $\ell_{\text{P}}$ is the Planck length. In units where $\hbar=c=G=k_{\text{B}}=1$ this reads $S=A/4$, which is how it is usually written and why it is usually remembered. Nothing in this book derives it, and nothing in this book can.

Put a number on it before saying what is strange, because the number is part of what is strange. For a hole of one solar mass, Chapter 3.7 gave $r_{s}=2GM_{\odot}/c^{2}=2953\ \mathrm{m}$ using the measured $GM_{\odot}=1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{3}s^{-2}}$, so $A=4\pi r_{s}^{2}=1.096\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$. With $\ell_{\text{P}}^{2}=\hbar G/c^{3}=2.612\times10^{-70}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$,

$$ \frac{S}{k_{\text{B}}} \;=\; \frac{A}{4\ell_{\text{P}}^{2}} \;=\; \frac{1.096\times10^{8}}{4\times2.612\times10^{-70}} \;=\; 1.05\times10^{77}. $$

Set that beside the number of particles involved. Dividing the measured $GM_{\odot}$ by the measured $G=6.674\times10^{-11}$ — and it is $G$ that limits the precision, as Chapter 3.7 warned — gives $M_{\odot}=1.99\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, which is $1.19\times10^{57}$ protons' worth of mass. So the entropy is about $10^{20}$ times Boltzmann's constant per nucleon. Chapter 0.6's Problem 3, which derived $S=k_{\text{B}}\ln W$ from a maximisation, then says the hole has around $\ee^{10^{77}}$ distinguishable microscopic configurations.

Now what is strange, in three parts.

It is an area. Entropy counts configurations, configurations are built from degrees of freedom, and the degrees of freedom of everything else in this book scale with volume: double the linear size of a box of gas and you get eight times the entropy, not four. Here the answer scales with the boundary of the region rather than its interior. Whatever the microscopic description of a gravitating region is, it has as many degrees of freedom as a sheet, not as a solid.

It contains $\hbar$ and $G$ together. Set $\hbar\to0$ and $S$ diverges; set $G\to0$ and it diverges too. So it belongs to neither quantum mechanics nor general relativity separately, and it is one of very few numbers in physics of which that is true. This chapter's equations and Chapter 3.8's have no $\hbar$ in them anywhere.

And general relativity's own answer is zero. This is the sharpest way to say what is owed. Chapter 3.7 §4 proved Birkhoff's theorem: the geometry outside a spherical mass is determined completely by one number. Chapter 3.8 §6 found nothing else to measure. So classically a black hole of mass $M$ has exactly one configuration, $W=1$, and $S=k_{\text{B}}\ln1=0$. The debt is therefore not a missing constant but a missing list: something has $10^{77}$ states, and general relativity says it has one.

7.3 · What the next three parts do with gravity, said in advance

Here is the thing that ought to be said before it happens rather than excused afterwards. Parts IV, V and VI are done entirely on flat spacetime. Gravity does not appear again until Chapter 7.1. Twenty-five chapters, after nine spent establishing that spacetime is dynamical and that the metric is a field with an equation of motion of its own.

That is not the book quietly abandoning its own result. It is an accurate report of the state of physics, and it can be justified with a number.

Chapter 3.4 §4.5 computed the curvature at the Earth's surface as $GM_{\oplus}/c^{2}r^{3}=1.7\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}$ — a radius of curvature of $2\times10^{11}$ metres, which is why nobody noticed it for three centuries. A curvature is one over a length squared, so its effect on a system of size $\ell$ is of order (curvature)$\times\ell^{2}$. For a hydrogen atom, $\ell\approx5.3\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}$, and

$$ \big(1.7\times10^{-23}\big)\big(5.3\times10^{-11}\big)^{2} \;=\; 4.8\times10^{-44}. $$

Set that beside the precision of the best-tested prediction in Part V, the magnetic moment of the electron, which ⚑ agrees with experiment to about one part in $10^{13}$. The correction from spacetime curvature is thirty orders of magnitude below the error bars. The same comparison made with forces rather than lengths: the gravitational attraction between two protons, divided by their electrostatic repulsion, is

$$ \frac{G m_{p}^{2}}{e^{2}/4\pi\epsilon_{0}} \;=\; \frac{\big(6.674\times10^{-11}\big)\big(1.673\times10^{-27}\big)^{2}}{\big(1.602\times10^{-19}\big)^{2}\big(8.988\times10^{9}\big)} \;=\; \frac{1.87\times10^{-64}}{2.31\times10^{-28}} \;=\; 8.1\times10^{-37}. $$

Flat spacetime is therefore not an approximation of convenience. It is an approximation with a computed error, and the error is smaller than any measurement made on a particle has ever been able to resolve. Part II's Minkowski spacetime is the arena for Parts IV to VI because that is what the measurements say, not because the mathematics is easier — though it is.

And now say where it stops being legitimate, since a bound is worth nothing without the place it fails. Four places, three of which this part has already reached.

(i) The centre of a black hole. As $a\to0$ the density diverges and the curvature with it, and the same Planck scale is reached at a cosmic time of order $\sqrt{\hbar G/c^{5}}=5.4\times10^{-44}\ \mathrm{s}$. Everything this chapter derived assumed a classical metric, so everything this chapter derived stops applying there. 8 · Worked examples

Worked example 1 — how far you can see, and how far you can ever send a signal

(a) Define the distance light has had time to travel since $a=0$ and show that for $a\propto t^{n}$ it is finite exactly when $n\lt1$, with the value $ct_{0}/(1-n)$. Evaluate for the matter and radiation eras. (b) Explain why the answer exceeds $ct_{0}$ without anything having travelled faster than light. (c) Quote the concordance value and use it to state the puzzle that motivates inflation. (d) Show that a universe with a cosmological term also has a future horizon, and compute its size.

(a) The particle horizon. Light travels on $\dd s^{2}=0$, so by the comoving distance covered between $a=0$ and the present is $\chi_{\text{ph}}=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}c\,\dd t/a(t)$, and the proper distance now to the furthest object whose light has reached us is $d_{\text{ph}}=a_{0}\chi_{\text{ph}}$. Put $a=a_{0}(t/t_{0})^{n}$:

$$ d_{\text{ph}} \;=\; a_{0}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{c\,\dd t}{a_{0}\big(t/t_{0}\big)^{n}} \;=\; c\,t_{0}^{n}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}t^{-n}\,\dd t \;=\; c\,t_{0}^{n}\,\frac{t_{0}^{1-n}}{1-n} \;=\; \frac{c\,t_{0}}{1-n}. $$

The integral converges at the lower limit exactly when $n\lt1$ — Chapter 0.2 §5's classification of $\int_{0}t^{-n}\dd t$, again. So matter, $n=2/3$, gives $d_{\text{ph}}=3ct_{0}$, and radiation, $n=1/2$, gives $2ct_{0}$. For $n\ge1$, which includes the de Sitter case, the integral diverges: there is no particle horizon at all and every point has always been in causal contact with every other. That is the property inflation is invented to exploit.

(b) Why $3ct_{0}$ is not a contradiction. Nothing travelled $3ct_{0}$ in a time $t_{0}$. The light travelled through a sequence of local frames in each of which it moved at $c$; meanwhile the proper distance between its starting point and us grew, by , while it was in transit. The factor $1/(1-n)$ is precisely the accumulated growth. Section 5.3 said why comparing the two numbers is not a comparison of velocities.

(c) The concordance number, and the puzzle. With all four terms of present the integral is not elementary, and numerically

$$ \chi_{\text{ph}} \;=\; \frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\dd z}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\text{r}}(1+z)^{4}+\Omega_{\text{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}} \;=\; 3.18\,\frac{c}{H_{0}} \;=\; 14.1\ \mathrm{Gpc}, $$

which is $46\ \mathrm{Gly}$ — larger than the crude $3ct_{0}=41\ \mathrm{Gly}$, the difference being the recent $\Lambda$ era. The same integral with its lower limit moved to last scattering gives the comoving distance light could have covered before the microwave background was emitted: $0.28\ \mathrm{Gpc}$, against $13.9\ \mathrm{Gpc}$ for the comoving distance to the last-scattering surface itself. The ratio is the angle that horizon subtends on our sky:

$$ \theta \;=\; \frac{0.278}{13.87} \;=\; 0.020\ \mathrm{rad} \;=\; 1.15^{\circ}. $$

Two patches of the microwave sky more than about a degree apart were never in causal contact, and they have the same temperature to one part in $10^{5}$. That is the horizon problem, and §7.3 named inflation as the proposed answer: an early era with $n\ge1$, which by (a) removes the horizon entirely.

(d) The future horizon. A signal sent now covers comoving distance $\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}c\,\dd t/a$. With matter alone, $a\propto t^{2/3}$ and the integrand goes as $t^{-2/3}$, whose integral to infinity diverges: every comoving point is eventually reachable. With a cosmological term, $a\propto\ee^{H_{\Lambda}t}$ and the integral converges. In the pure de Sitter case,

$$ \chi_{\text{ev}} \;=\; \int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{c\,\dd t}{a_{\ast}\ee^{H_{\Lambda}t}} \;=\; \frac{c}{a_{\ast}H_{\Lambda}\ee^{H_{\Lambda}t_{0}}} \;=\; \frac{c}{a_{0}H_{\Lambda}}, \qquad d_{\text{ev}}=a_{0}\chi_{\text{ev}}=\frac{c}{H_{\Lambda}}. $$

With $H_{\Lambda}=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}$ that proper distance is $c/H_{0}\sqrt{0.685}=4.45/0.828=5.4\ \mathrm{Gpc}$, or $17.5\ \mathrm{Gly}$; the exact integral with matter included gives $16.7\ \mathrm{Gly}$. Anything further away than that can never receive a signal sent today, whatever either party does. It is a one-way surface produced by integrating a null condition, exactly as Chapter 3.8 §7.1's tortoise coordinate was — and Problem 3 identifies it as the horizon of the static patch that de Sitter space, alone among expanding universes, possesses.

Worked example 2 — one galaxy at $z=1$, taken apart completely

A galaxy is observed with every spectral line at twice its laboratory wavelength. Using the concordance parameters with $H_{0}=67.4$: (a) how big was the universe then; (b) how old was it; (c) how long has the light been travelling; (d) how far away is the galaxy now, and how fast is that distance growing; (e) at what redshift does the answer to (d) reach $c$, and why can we nonetheless see things beyond it?

(a) Doubled wavelength means $z=1$, so by $a/a_{0}=1/(1+z)=\half$. Every separation between comoving objects was half its present value; the matter density was $2^{3}=8$ times higher; the radiation density $2^{4}=16$ times higher.

(b) Invert the exact solution . Squaring it and using $\rho_{\text{m},0}/\rho_{\Lambda}=\Omega_{\text{m}}/\Omega_{\Lambda}$,

$$ \sinh^{2}\!\left(\frac{3H_{\Lambda}t}{2}\right) \;=\; \frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\Omega_{\text{m}}}\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{3} \;=\; 2.175\times\frac18 \;=\; 0.2719, $$

so $\sinh(3H_{\Lambda}t/2)=0.5214$ and $3H_{\Lambda}t/2=\mathrm{arsinh}(0.5214)=\ln\big(0.5214+\sqrt{1.2719}\big)=\ln(1.6492)=0.5004$. With $H_{\Lambda}=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}=1.808\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$,

$$ t \;=\; \frac{2\times0.5004}{3\times1.808\times10^{-18}} \;=\; 1.845\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{s} \;=\; 5.85\ \mathrm{Gyr}. $$

(c) The lookback time is $t_{0}-t=13.80-5.85=7.95\ \mathrm{Gyr}$. The light left when the universe was $42\%$ of its present age.

(d) The comoving distance follows from rewritten in terms of redshift, using $\dd z=-(1+z)H\,\dd t$ from :

$$ \chi \;=\; \int\frac{c\,\dd t}{a} \;=\; \frac{c}{a_{0}H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\dd z'}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\text{m}}(1+z')^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}}. $$

The integrand at $z'=0$ is $1$ and at $z'=1$ is $\big(0.315\times8+0.685\big)^{-1/2}=0.5586$; numerically the integral is $0.765$, so $d=a_{0}\chi=0.765\,c/H_{0}=0.765\times4.45=3.41\ \mathrm{Gpc}=11.1\ \mathrm{Gly}$. Note that this exceeds the $7.95\ \mathrm{Gly}$ the light actually travelled, for the reason Worked example 1(b) gave. Then by ,

$$ \dot d \;=\; H_{0}d \;=\; 0.765\,c. $$

(e) $\dot d=c$ requires $\chi=c/H_{0}$, that is the integral in (d) equal to $1$, which happens at $z\approx1.5$. Galaxies are routinely observed at $z=7$ and beyond, where the same integral gives $1.98$: their proper distance from us is growing at twice the speed of light, and their light arrives all the same. Nothing paradoxical has happened. What decides whether light arrives is not a speed but a comparison of two comoving distances: the source's, $1.98\,c/H_{0}$ at $z=7$, against the particle horizon's $3.18\,c/H_{0}$ from Worked example 1(c). The first is smaller than the second, so the light has had time. Section 5.3's warning is the whole of the explanation: $\dot d$ is not anybody's velocity, so it is not the quantity a causality question is asked about.

Problem 1 — the closed universe, and how long it lasts

Take dust only, $k=+1$, no cosmological term, and write $C\equiv\tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\text{m},0}a_{0}^{3}$, a constant by . (a) Show that Friedmann I becomes $\dot a^{2}=C/a-c^{2}$, and deduce that the expansion stops at $a_{\max}=C/c^{2}$. (b) Verify that the parametric curve $a=\tfrac{C}{2c^{2}}(1-\cos\eta)$, $t=\tfrac{C}{2c^{3}}(\eta-\sin\eta)$ solves it — a cycloid, the curve traced by a point on a rolling wheel. (c) Find the total lifetime from $a=0$ back to $a=0$. (d) Take $\Omega_{\text{m}}=2$, $\Omega_{\Lambda}=0$ and $H_{0}=67.4$. Compute $a_{\max}/a_{0}$, the total lifetime, and the present age. Compare the last with §4.4's globular clusters and say what the comparison rules out.

Solution

(a) With $\rho=\rho_{\text{m},0}(a_{0}/a)^{3}$ and $k=+1$, multiplied by $a^{2}$ gives $\dot a^{2}=\tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\text{m},0}a_{0}^{3}/a-c^{2}=C/a-c^{2}$. The left side is a square, so the motion is confined to $C/a\ge c^{2}$, that is $a\le C/c^{2}$, with equality where $\dot a=0$. This is Chapter 1.3's turning-point reasoning applied to one variable, and Chapter 3.7 §6 used it in the same way for an orbit.

(b) $\dd a/\dd\eta=\tfrac{C}{2c^{2}}\sin\eta$ and $\dd t/\dd\eta=\tfrac{C}{2c^{3}}(1-\cos\eta)$, so

$$ \dot a^{2} = \left(\frac{\dd a/\dd\eta}{\dd t/\dd\eta}\right)^{2} = c^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}\eta}{(1-\cos\eta)^{2}}, \qquad \frac{C}{a}-c^{2} = \frac{2c^{2}}{1-\cos\eta}-c^{2} = c^{2}\,\frac{1+\cos\eta}{1-\cos\eta}. $$

These agree, since $\sin^{2}\eta=1-\cos^{2}\eta=(1-\cos\eta)(1+\cos\eta)$. ✓ (Checked symbolically: the residual is exactly zero.)

(c) $a$ returns to zero at $\eta=2\pi$, so the lifetime is $t(2\pi)=\tfrac{C}{2c^{3}}\cdot2\pi=\pi C/c^{3}$, and the maximum is at $\eta=\pi$, halfway through.

(d) $\Omega_{\text{m}}=2$ with nothing else gives $\Omega_{k}=-1$, and by the definition below that means $c^{2}/a_{0}^{2}=H_{0}^{2}$, so $a_{0}=c/H_{0}$. Also $C=\tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\text{m},0}a_{0}^{3}=H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\text{m}} a_{0}^{3}=2H_{0}^{2}a_{0}^{3}$. Hence

$$ a_{\max}=\frac{C}{c^{2}}=\frac{2H_{0}^{2}a_{0}^{3}}{c^{2}}=2a_{0}\left(\frac{H_{0}a_{0}}{c}\right)^{2}=2a_{0}, $$

so the universe expands to twice its present size and turns round. The lifetime is $\pi C/c^{3}=2\pi/H_{0}=2\pi\times14.51=91\ \mathrm{Gyr}$. For the present age, $a=a_{0}$ means $a/a_{\max}=\half$, so $1-\cos\eta=1$ and $\eta=\pi/2$; then $t_{0}=\tfrac{C}{2c^{3}}(\pi/2-1)=(\pi/2-1)/H_{0}=0.571\times14.51=8.3\ \mathrm{Gyr}$.

That is the point of the problem. The oldest globular clusters are ⚑ dated near $13\ \mathrm{Gyr}$, so a universe with $\Omega_{\text{m}}=2$ and no cosmological term contains stars older than itself. Age arguments of exactly this shape were among the reasons a matter-dominated closed universe was in trouble long before the supernova measurements, and they are why §4.4's disagreement over $H_{0}$ is watched so closely: the age is the quantity a wrong $H_{0}$ damages first.

Problem 2 — a galaxy given a shove, and why it stops

Work with $k=0$ in comoving Cartesian coordinates, so that $\dd s^{2}=c^{2}\dd t^{2}-a^{2}(t)\big(\dd X^{2}+\dd Y^{2}+\dd Z^{2}\big)$. (a) Identify a Killing vector by inspection and write down the conserved charge of Chapter 3.5 §9 for a massive particle. (d) Our own peculiar velocity relative to the microwave background is $370\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ (§1.2). Solution

(a) The metric components do not mention $X$, so by Chapter 3.5 §8.1's practical test $\xi^{\mu}=(0,1,0,0)$ is a Killing vector, and by 's $g_{XX}=-a^{2}$ the charge is

$$ Q \;=\; \xi_{\mu}u^{\mu} \;=\; g_{XX}\,u^{X} \;=\; -a^{2}\,u^{X} \;=\; \text{constant}. $$

Three such charges, one per direction — the same three Killing vectors §1.1 called homogeneity, now doing work.

(b) The proper distance moved per unit proper time, as measured locally, is $a\,\dd X/\dd\tau=a\,u^{X}$, and the momentum per unit mass is that same quantity. From (a), $u^{X}=-Q/a^{2}$, so

$$ \abs{p} \;=\; m\,a\,\abs{u^{X}} \;=\; \frac{m\abs{Q}}{a} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{a}. $$

(c) For light, §5.1 found $p^{0}\propto1/a$ and hence $h\nu\propto1/a$; since a photon's energy is $c$ times its momentum, its momentum also falls as $1/a$. The two agree because the derivations differ only in which normalisation was imposed, and neither derivation used the normalisation at the step where the $1/a$ appeared. Physically: momentum decays as $1/a$ for everything, and only the relation between momentum and energy distinguishes the cases.

(d) $370/10=37\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Peculiar motion decays. A galaxy given any kick coasts asymptotically to comoving rest without anything acting on it — which is why §5.3's "at rest in comoving coordinates" is not a special initial condition but an attractor, and why the comoving frame is a physically distinguished one even though nothing in the field equations distinguishes a slicing. In reality gravitational infall towards nearby masses keeps regenerating peculiar velocities, and $370\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ is what that competition currently produces.

Problem 3 — the one expanding universe that does have a conserved energy

Solution

(a) Differentiate the definitions: $\dd\bar r=\ee^{Ht}\dd r+H\bar r\,\dd t$ and $\dd\bar t=\dd t+H\bar r\,\dd\bar r/(c^{2}f)$. Substitute into the static form:

$$ f c^{2}\dd\bar t^{2} = fc^{2}\dd t^{2} + 2H\bar r\,\dd t\,\dd\bar r + \frac{H^{2}\bar r^{2}}{c^{2}f}\dd\bar r^{2}, $$

so subtracting $f^{-1}\dd\bar r^{2}$ leaves $fc^{2}\dd t^{2}+2H\bar r\,\dd t\,\dd\bar r+\dd\bar r^{2}\big(H^{2}\bar r^{2}/c^{2}-1\big)/f$, and the last bracket over $f$ is exactly $-1$. Now put $\dd\bar r=\ee^{Ht}\dd r+H\bar r\,\dd t$ into what remains:

$$ 2H\bar r\,\dd t\,\dd\bar r - \dd\bar r^{2} = 2H\bar r\ee^{Ht}\dd t\,\dd r + 2H^{2}\bar r^{2}\dd t^{2} - \ee^{2Ht}\dd r^{2} - 2H\bar r\ee^{Ht}\dd r\,\dd t - H^{2}\bar r^{2}\dd t^{2}, $$

in which the cross terms cancel identically, leaving $H^{2}\bar r^{2}\dd t^{2}-\ee^{2Ht}\dd r^{2}$. Adding $fc^{2}\dd t^{2}=(c^{2}-H^{2}\bar r^{2})\dd t^{2}$ gives $c^{2}\dd t^{2}-\ee^{2Ht}\dd r^{2}$, and the angular part matches because $\bar r^{2}=\ee^{2Ht}r^{2}$. ✓ (Verified symbolically; the difference is exactly zero.)

(b) In the barred coordinates no metric component mentions $\bar t$, so $\xi=\partial_{\bar t}$ is a Killing vector by Chapter 3.5 §8.1, with norm $\xi\cdot\xi=g_{\bar t\bar t}=fc^{2}$. That is positive — timelike, by Chapter 2.3 §4 — precisely for $\bar r\lt c/H$, and it vanishes at $\bar r=c/H$. That radius is the proper distance Worked example 1(d) computed as the future horizon, and the two calculations are the same fact: the surface where the Killing vector goes null is where signals stop being exchangeable. Compare the structure with Chapter 3.8: $f$ plays the role of $A(r)$, and $\bar r=c/H$ the role of $r_{s}$, with the inequality reversed.

(c) For $w=-1$, $p=-\rho c^{2}$, so $\rho-3p/c^{2}=4\rho_{\Lambda}$ and gives $R=-32\pi G\rho_{\Lambda}/c^{2}=-4\Lambda$, a constant. Step 3 of §6.2 divided by $\dd R/\dd x^{0}$, which is zero here, so the argument yields nothing — correctly, since the conclusion is false.

(d) Two honest halves. Yes, locally and for test bodies: inside $\bar r\lt c/H$ the charge $\xi_{\mu}p^{\mu}$ is conserved along every geodesic by Chapter 3.5 §9, so a photon crossing the static patch has a well-defined conserved energy and Chapter 3.8 §5's machinery applies unchanged. No, as a statement about the universe: the patch is a region and not the spacetime, every comoving observer has a different one, and de Sitter space contains no matter to have an energy in the first place. Adding any dust at all destroys the Killing vector by §6.2, and the real universe is $31.5\%$ dust.

Problem 4 — Friedmann from Newton, and the three things it cannot see

A famous coincidence: Friedmann's first equation can be obtained without any general relativity at all. (a) Take a uniform ball of dust of density $\rho(t)$ and radius $R(t)$, and a test particle on its surface. Write down Newtonian energy conservation for that particle and rearrange it into the form of . What plays the part of $-kc^{2}$? (b) Two objections should occur to you: the ball has an edge, and the universe does not; and matter outside the ball should matter. Say which results from Chapters 3.7 and 0.7 answer each. (c) Now find the failures. Show that the same argument gives $\ddot R=-GM/R^{2}$ and hence an acceleration equation without the pressure term. For which of §4.1's three fluids does that give the wrong answer, and by how much? (d) Show that the Newtonian argument also forces $\rho\propto a^{-3}$ and therefore cannot describe radiation at all, and say in one sentence what the $a^{-4}$ requires that Newton does not have.

Solution

(a) Mass enclosed $M=\tfrac43\pi R^{3}\rho$, constant if the dust moves with the surface. Energy per unit mass: $\half\dot R^{2}-GM/R=\mathcal{E}$, a constant. Multiply by $2/R^{2}$:

$$ \left(\frac{\dot R}{R}\right)^{2} \;=\; \frac{2GM}{R^{3}} + \frac{2\mathcal{E}}{R^{2}} \;=\; \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3} + \frac{2\mathcal{E}}{R^{2}}, $$

which is with $R$ for $a$ and $2\mathcal{E}=-kc^{2}$. A bound ball ($\mathcal{E}\lt0$) corresponds to $k=+1$ and recollapses, an unbound one to $k=-1$, and the marginal case to $k=0$ — which is why the closed universe of Problem 1 is the one that turns round.

(b) The edge: Chapter 3.7 §4's Birkhoff theorem says the geometry outside any spherical distribution depends only on the mass inside, and ⚑ its interior counterpart, quoted rather than proved, says a spherical shell contributes nothing to the field inside it. So the ball may be taken as a piece cut from a homogeneous universe and the rest ignored. Chapter 0.7 §8 derived the same statement for the inverse-square law from the divergence theorem — only the enclosed source appears, and a hollow shell exerts no force inside itself, which it named as Newton's shell theorem. Both give the same licence, and Birkhoff is the reason the Newtonian licence survives into general relativity.

(c) Differentiating the energy equation, or directly from $\vv F=m\vv a$, $\ddot R=-GM/R^{2}=-\tfrac{4\pi G}{3}\rho R$. Compare : the bracket $\rho+3p/c^{2}$ has become $\rho$ alone. For dust that is exact and the Newtonian answer is right. For radiation, $p=\rho c^{2}/3$ makes the true bracket $2\rho$, so the Newtonian version underestimates the deceleration by a factor of two. For the cosmological term the true bracket is $-2\rho$ and the Newtonian one is $+\rho$: the sign is wrong, so a Newtonian argument predicts that vacuum energy attracts, and the entire late-time behaviour of the universe is invisible to it.

(d) The Newtonian argument holds $M=\tfrac43\pi R^{3}\rho$ fixed, which is $\rho\propto R^{-3}$ by construction, so it cannot produce any other dilution law. Getting $a^{-4}$ requires the energy of each quantum to fall as $1/a$, which is §5.1's null-geodesic result, and Newtonian gravity has no null geodesics and no reason for light to respond to gravity at all. So the coincidence in (a) is genuine but narrow: it works for pressureless matter, which is exactly the case in which relativity has nothing new to say.

The brick you just laid — and the wall Part III has finished

The metric was forced, not chosen. Two symmetry assumptions — no special place, no special direction, six Killing fields — fix $g_{00}=1$ and $g_{0i}=0$ by inspection, reduce the spatial geometry to one of constant curvature, and turn that condition into a single separable equation whose solution, made regular at the origin, is $\dd\ell^{2}=\dd r^{2}/(1-kr^{2})+r^{2}\dd\Omega^{2}$ with $k$ of one of three signs. One function $a(t)$ survives. And $k$ is a statement about curvature and not about whether space is finite, which is the fifth appearance in this book of a local fact failing to determine a global one.

Two equations, and the third for free. $G_{00}$ gives $(\dot a/a)^{2}=8\pi G\rho/3-kc^{2}/a^{2}$; the $11$ component gives $\ddot a/a=-\tfrac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho+3p/c^{2})$, but only once Friedmann I has been substituted into it; and $\dd\mathrm{I}/\dd x^{0}-2a'\mathrm{II}$ is exactly $-8\pi Ga^{2}/3c^{2}$ times the fluid equation. Three fluids, one running order. $\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$ from one separable equation; $a\propto t^{2/3(1+w)}$ from another, plus $\ee^{H_{\Lambda}t}$ for the case the formula misses. The three exponents put radiation first, matter second and the cosmological term last, whatever the amounts, and the expansion accelerates exactly when $w\lt-\tfrac13$ — Chapter 3.6 §5.5's condition, unchanged. With the ⚑ measured parameters the universe is $13.8\ \mathrm{Gyr}$ old, began accelerating at $z=0.63$, and its two best measurements of $H_{0}$ disagree by five times their uncertainty.

The redshift, and what it is not. Following $p^{0}$ along a null geodesic gives $p^{0}\propto1/a$ and hence $1+z=a_{0}/a_{\text{em}}$, with no velocity anywhere in the derivation or the answer; crest-counting gives the same thing by a route sharing no step. Comoving worldlines are geodesics because $\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}=0$, so nothing is moving through anything; proper distances grow as $\dot d=Hd$ and exceed $c$ beyond $4.45\ \mathrm{Gpc}$, which contradicts nothing because velocities at different points live in different tangent spaces. The balloon is right about having no centre and wrong about the room it sits in.

And the thesis. A conserved energy is the output of Noether's theorem; its input is a symmetry; in a curved spacetime that symmetry is a timelike Killing vector; and there is none, because a Killing flow preserves every geometric scalar while $R=-\tfrac{8\pi G}{c^{2}}(\rho-3p/c^{2})$ changes with time whenever anything dilutes. So the theorem returns nothing, and $99.91\%$ of the microwave background's energy has gone since last scattering with nothing holding it. What survives is $\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0$, which in this spacetime is the fluid equation; what obstructs adding it up is the term $3(a'/a)p$ and the fact that $\nu$ is a free index.
