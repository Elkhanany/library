Part III · General Relativity — Chapter 3.3

Metric and Connection

Two objects are missing from the arena: one that measures, and one that compares. This chapter builds both, and finds that the second of them is the gravitational field.

Where we are

Chapter 3.2 finished by listing, deliberately, what the new arena could not do. There was no way to measure a length, no way to say that two directions meet at a right angle, and — the one that stops everything — no way to compare a vector at one point with a vector at another. Since a rate of change is exactly such a comparison, the manifold of Chapter 3.2 does not support differentiating a vector field at all. Equation (3.2.18) there was not a hard limit; it was a limit with an undefined numerator.

Two separate objects repair the two separate gaps, and it is worth keeping them apart because they are logically independent. The metric supplies measurement: an inner product on each tangent space, varying smoothly from point to point. The connection supplies comparison: a rule for carrying a vector from one point to a neighbouring one. On a bare manifold you may have either without the other. What makes general relativity possible is that once you have the metric, and once you make two modest demands, the connection is forced — there is exactly one, and we derive it.

The route, announced in advance. Sections 1 to 3 build the metric and spend it: the line element, then a deliberately chosen warning example (flat space in polar coordinates, whose metric components vary wildly while nothing whatever is curved), then proper time and the action of a free particle. Section 4 exhibits the obstruction in full — the coordinate derivative of a vector field is not a tensor, and we display the exact term that spoils it. Section 5 defines the connection as whatever cancels that term, and derives the strange transformation law it must therefore obey. Section 6 uses it to transport a vector along a curve. Section 7 is the set piece: two physical demands, and a formula for the connection in terms of the metric, derived with every step shown. Section 8 derives the geodesic equation twice, by two routes that share no assumptions, and finds one equation — which is where Chapter 1.2's promise is collected. Section 9 works the sphere and the plane in polar coordinates in full.

Conventions, stated once and used without further comment. The signature is $(+,-,-,-)$, as in all of Part II, so that timelike intervals are positive. Greek indices run $0,1,2,3$ and Latin indices $1,2,3$. We write $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ for the general metric and reserve $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ for the flat one. The connection is $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$ and the covariant derivative $\nabla_{\mu}$. $G$ and $c$ are kept explicit throughout Part III; nothing is harder on a reader than a constant that has quietly been set to one.

Tools you'll need  — Chapter 3.2 in its entirety: the tangent space, the coordinate basis $\partial_{\mu}$, the transformation law (3.2.16) for vector components, and above all §5, which is the problem this chapter solves. Chapter 2.4 §4: the metric as the dictionary between vectors and covectors, raising and lowering, and §6, the theorem that a tensor equation true in one chart is true in all of them. 1 · The metric: an inner product at every point

Chapter 0.5 defined an inner product on a vector space by axioms rather than by a formula: a symmetric bilinear map taking two vectors to a number. Chapter 2.4 §4 put one on Minkowski spacetime, with indefinite signature, and showed that it does two jobs at once — it measures intervals, and it converts vectors into covectors. Both constructions apply to a single vector space. We now have one vector space per point, so we install one inner product per point.

Definition — the metric

A metric on a manifold $M$ assigns to each point $p$ a map

$$ g_{p}:\ T_{p}M\times T_{p}M \;\longrightarrow\; \R $$

which is bilinear (linear in each slot separately), symmetric ($g_{p}(X,Y)=g_{p}(Y,X)$), and non-degenerate (if $g_{p}(X,Y)=0$ for every $Y$, then $X=0$), and which varies smoothly with $p$. Its components in a chart are

$$ g_{\mu\nu}(x) \;\equiv\; g\big(\partial_{\mu},\,\partial_{\nu}\big), $$

an array of functions of position, symmetric in its two indices.

Three remarks on that definition, each of which will be used.

(i) It is a tensor field. The map eats two vectors and returns a number, linearly in each — which is Chapter 3.2 §7's definition of a type $(0,2)$ tensor, word for word. So its components obey the tensor law with two factors of the inverse Jacobian. We can also derive that law directly, and it is worth doing once because the derivation is three lines and fixes the index placement. Expand the same vectors in two charts and demand that $g(X,Y)$ be one number:

$$ g'_{\mu\nu}\,X'^{\mu}Y'^{\nu} \;=\; g_{\alpha\beta}\,X^{\alpha}Y^{\beta}. $$

Substitute the component law $X^{\alpha}=(\partial x^{\alpha}/\partial x'^{\mu})X'^{\mu}$ — which is (3.2.16) read backwards — on the right, and the same for $Y$:

$$ g'_{\mu\nu}\,X'^{\mu}Y'^{\nu} \;=\; g_{\alpha\beta}\,\pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\beta}}{x'^{\nu}}\;X'^{\mu}Y'^{\nu}. $$

Since $X'$ and $Y'$ are arbitrary, their coefficients must agree index by index, giving

$$ \boxed{\;g'_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\;\pdv{x^{\beta}}{x'^{\nu}}\;g_{\alpha\beta}.\;} $$

(ii) The signature is fixed. At each point $g_{\mu\nu}$ is a real symmetric matrix, so by the spectral theorem (Chapter 0.5 §6) it has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigenbasis, and non-degeneracy says none of the eigenvalues is zero. The number of positive and negative eigenvalues is the signature, and it cannot change from point to point on a connected manifold without an eigenvalue passing through zero on the way, which non-degeneracy forbids. ⚑ Making that continuity argument airtight is Sylvester's law of inertia plus a connectedness argument; we quote the conclusion and use it. For spacetime we impose signature $(+,-,-,-)$: three minus signs and one plus, everywhere, which is what makes each tangent space a copy of the Minkowski spacetime of Chapter 2.3 and is the precise statement that special relativity holds locally.

(iii) The line element. The traditional notation for a metric is

$$ \dd s^{2} \;=\; g_{\mu\nu}(x)\;\dd x^{\mu}\,\dd x^{\nu}, $$

and it deserves one sentence of honesty, because Chapter 3.2 §6 taught us what $\dd x^{\mu}$ really is. It is a basis covector, and the product of two covectors written side by side means their symmetric tensor product. So is shorthand for the tensor $g=g_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^{\mu}\otimes\dd x^{\nu}$, and feeding it the same tangent vector twice returns the squared length of that vector. The older reading — "the squared length of an infinitesimal displacement" — gives the right answers because it is that statement with the vector left implicit. We use the notation freely from here on, having said once what licenses it.

1.1 · Raising, lowering, and the gradient that finally exists

Non-degeneracy says the matrix $g_{\mu\nu}$ is invertible at each point. Write the inverse $g^{\mu\nu}$, defined by

$$ g^{\mu\lambda}\,g_{\lambda\nu} \;=\; \delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}. $$

Everything Chapter 2.4 §4 built now transfers unchanged, because it was pointwise algebra: $V_{\mu}\equiv g_{\mu\nu}V^{\nu}$ lowers an index, $\omega^{\mu}\equiv g^{\mu\nu}\omega_{\nu}$ raises one, and the two are inverse operations by . The one alteration is the one Chapter 3.2 §7 identified: the dictionary is now different at every point.

Familiar ground — the gradient, four chapters late

Chapter 0.6 §4 distinguished $\dd f$, which any smooth function produces for free, from $\nabla f$, which requires an inner product; Chapter 3.2 §6 pointed out that on a bare manifold the first exists and the second does not, and made that the reason the metric would have to be introduced as a separate object. The debt is now settled:

$$ \big(\nabla f\big)^{\mu} \;=\; g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\nu}f. $$

Chapter 3.2's Problem 4 showed two different metrics turning one function into two genuinely different gradient vectors, pointing in different directions. That was not a defect in the problem. It is the statement that the gradient is a joint property of the function and the geometry, and on a manifold the geometry is a physical field.

The length of a vector and the angle between two are read off in the usual way, with one warning inherited from Part II. In Lorentzian signature $g(X,X)$ may be positive, negative or zero, and Chapter 2.3's three-way classification applies at each point separately: $X$ is timelike if $g(X,X)>0$, spacelike if $g(X,X)<0$, and null if $g(X,X)=0$ with $X\neq0$. Since the signature is the same everywhere, so is the classification, and the light cone at each point is a genuine structure rather than an artefact of a chart.

In plain terms 3.3.1

The arena handed over at the end of the last chapter was deliberately unfurnished. Nothing in it could measure a distance, nothing could say whether two directions stood at right angles, and nothing could compare a direction here with one over there. The first two gaps close with one object installed at every point, taking two directions and returning a number. Out of that number come lengths, angles, and the sorting of directions into timelike, spacelike and lightlike that the previous part built its geometry on.

Two features of the installation matter. Because it happens point by point, the numbers describing it are functions of position rather than constants, and that dependence is where gravity will eventually live. Because the thing installed eats directions and returns numbers, it belongs to the species of the measuring machines of the tensor chapter, and responds to relabelling in the one controlled way that species does.

An old debt is settled almost in passing, and it is the charge the previous chapter warned would fall due. Ever since the multivariable chapter there has been a distinction between the object a function supplies for free, reporting how fast it changes in a direction, and the arrow of steepest ascent, undefinable until length exists. On the bare arena only the first existed. A length having been supplied, the second exists too, depending on the geometry as much as on the function.

2 · Flat space in polar coordinates — a warning delivered early

This section exists to destroy a belief before it forms. The belief is: if the metric components depend on position, the space is curved. It is false, it is the single most common misunderstanding at this stage, and it will wreck the next two chapters for anyone holding it. So we work the counterexample now, at length, before curvature has even been defined.

2.1 · The change of chart, one step at a time

Take the ordinary Euclidean plane, with Cartesian coordinates $(x,y)$ and the metric everyone agrees on,

$$ \dd s^{2} \;=\; \dd x^{2} \;+\; \dd y^{2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad g_{ij} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

Now change chart to polar coordinates by $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. This is a legitimate chart on the plane minus the origin: smooth, with a smooth inverse, exactly as Chapter 3.2 §2 requires. Differentiate each coordinate, using the product rule on $r\cos\theta$ and treating $r$ and $\theta$ as the two variables:

$$ \dd x \;=\; \cos\theta\;\dd r \;-\; r\sin\theta\;\dd\theta, \qquad \dd y \;=\; \sin\theta\;\dd r \;+\; r\cos\theta\;\dd\theta. $$

Square the first, one term at a time:

$$ \dd x^{2} \;=\; \cos^{2}\theta\;\dd r^{2} \;-\; 2r\sin\theta\cos\theta\;\dd r\,\dd\theta \;+\; r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\;\dd\theta^{2}. $$

And the second:

$$ \dd y^{2} \;=\; \sin^{2}\theta\;\dd r^{2} \;+\; 2r\sin\theta\cos\theta\;\dd r\,\dd\theta \;+\; r^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\;\dd\theta^{2}. $$

Add them. The cross terms are equal and opposite and cancel outright; the two $\dd r^{2}$ coefficients combine by $\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1$; the two $\dd\theta^{2}$ coefficients combine by the same identity after factoring out $r^{2}$. The result is

$$ \boxed{\;\dd s^{2} \;=\; \dd r^{2} \;+\; r^{2}\,\dd\theta^{2}, \qquad g_{ij} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&r^{2}\end{pmatrix}.\;} $$

2.2 · Nothing happened to the plane

Look at what changed and what did not. The component $g_{\theta\theta}=r^{2}$ is a function of position; it is $1$ at $r=1$, $100$ at $r=10$, and it grows without bound. By the eye test the metric now looks dramatically more complicated than .

And yet the plane is the plane. The change of chart was a smooth bijection; no point was added, removed, stretched or bent. Every geometric statement one could make survives verbatim, and three of them are worth checking explicitly because they are the ones people reach for when asked what "flat" means.

Circumference. Take the curve $r=R$ constant, $\theta$ running from $0$ to $2\pi$. Along it $\dd r=0$, so gives $\dd s = R\,\dd\theta$, and the length is

$$ L \;=\; \int_{0}^{2\pi} R\;\dd\theta \;=\; 2\pi R, $$

which is Euclid's answer exactly, with no correction of any order.

Straight lines. The curve $\theta=\theta_{0}$ constant has $\dd s=\dd r$, so its length between $r=a$ and $r=b$ is $b-a$. Radial rays are still straight lines and still have the length they always had.

Angles. The off-diagonal component vanishes, so the coordinate directions $\partial_{r}$ and $\partial_{\theta}$ are perpendicular at every point, as radial and circular directions ought to be.

⚠ The belief this section exists to prevent

Position-dependent metric components do not mean curvature. They mean the coordinate grid is not a Cartesian one — that its lines are not everywhere parallel, or that the coordinate directions do not everywhere have unit length, or both. In the basis vector $\partial_{\theta}$ has length $\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}=r$, so it is short near the origin and long far away, and the metric components are simply reporting that fact.

The sharpest form of the warning is a comparison. The unit sphere carries $\dd s^{2}=\dd\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\dd\phi^{2}$ and the flat plane carries $\dd s^{2}=\dd r^{2}+r^{2}\dd\theta^{2}$. Both have one constant component and one that varies with the other coordinate. One of the two spaces is curved and one is not. Inspection of the components cannot tell them apart, and therefore a genuine test for curvature has to be built. Building it is the whole of Chapter 3.4.

Two more things will be shown about before this chapter ends, and it is worth stating them now so that you know what is coming. In §7 we compute the connection coefficients of the flat plane in polar coordinates and find them non-zero: $\Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}=1/r$. In Chapter 3.4 we compute the curvature of the same metric and find it identically zero, component by component. Non-zero connection, zero curvature, in a space that is flat by construction: that pair of facts is the warning in its final form, and it also explains where Chapter 1.1's centrifugal and Coriolis terms came from.

In plain terms 3.3.2

Here is a habit of thought that has to be broken now rather than later. Write down the rule for measuring distances on a flat page using ordinary square coordinates and it looks utterly plain, with the same two numbers everywhere. Describe the very same page using distance-from-the-centre and angle-around-the-centre instead, and the rule acquires a factor that grows with distance and never stops growing. Nothing was done to the paper. Only the labelling changed.

The factor that appeared reports something real but modest, namely that a step of one unit in the angle coordinate carries you further when you are further out. Coordinate directions in such a description have lengths depending on where you stand, and the rule for measuring distances has to say so. That is bookkeeping about labels rather than a fact about the page.

The reason this matters so much is a coincidence of appearance. The rule for distances on a globe has exactly the same shape as the rule for the flat page in circular labels: one plain term and one term carrying a position-dependent factor. One of those surfaces is curved and one is not, and no amount of staring at the two rules will tell you which is which. A real test has to be built from scratch, and building it is the business of the chapter after this one.

3 · Proper time, and the action of a free particle

Chapter 2.3 §5 defined proper time along a worldline by $c^{2}\dd\tau^{2}=\dd s^{2}$, the interval being positive along timelike curves in our signature. That definition referred to $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ only through $\dd s^{2}$, so replacing the flat metric by the general one carries it across untouched:

$$ c^{2}\,\dd\tau^{2} \;=\; g_{\mu\nu}(x)\;\dd x^{\mu}\,\dd x^{\nu}. $$

Parametrise a worldline by anything at all, say $\lambda$, write $\dot x^{\mu}=\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda$, and divide by $\dd\lambda^{2}$ to get the elapsed proper time as an integral:

$$ \tau \;=\; \frac{1}{c}\int_{\lambda_{1}}^{\lambda_{2}} \sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}(x)\;\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}}\;\;\dd\lambda. $$

Two immediate readings of , both of which are collected debts.

A clock standing still. An observer who stays at fixed spatial coordinates has $\dd x^{i}=0$, so only the $00$ term survives. With $x^{0}=ct$ as always,

$$ c^{2}\dd\tau^{2} \;=\; g_{00}\,\big(\dd x^{0}\big)^{2} \;=\; g_{00}\,c^{2}\,\dd t^{2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \dd\tau \;=\; \sqrt{g_{00}}\;\dd t. $$

Chapter 3.1 §6.5 wrote exactly this line in advance and used it, together with the redshift derived from the accelerating cabin, to read off $g_{00}=1+2\Phi/c^{2}$ to first order. That argument used no general relativity, and it is now expressed in the notation it was waiting for. It is also the reason the metric cannot be $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ near a mass: two static clocks at different heights tick at different rates, and only a position-dependent $g_{00}$ can say so.

The action. Chapter 2.5 §5 obtained the action of a free relativistic particle by demanding a Lorentz-invariant number attached to each history and finding that only one was available: $S=-mc^{2}\!\int\dd\tau$. Nothing in that argument used flatness. So

$$ \boxed{\;S \;=\; -\,mc^{2}\!\int\dd\tau \;=\; -\,mc\int \sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}}\;\;\dd\lambda.\;} $$

Check the units before going on, since the constants are being kept explicit: $g_{\mu\nu}$ is dimensionless, $x^{\mu}$ is a length, so the square root has the dimensions of a length per unit $\lambda$, and $mc\times$ length is $\mathrm{kg\,m^{2}\,s^{-1}}$, which is an action. And setting $g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}$ returns Chapter 2.5's expression character for character.

What has just been claimed, and what has not

Equation is the entire dynamics of a particle in a gravitational field. There is no force term, no potential, and no coupling constant; the mass appears only as an overall factor and will cancel out of the equations of motion, which is Chapter 3.1's universality wearing new clothes. Everything a freely falling body does is contained in that integral, once $g_{\mu\nu}$ is known.

What has not been claimed is any way of finding $g_{\mu\nu}$. The metric is at this stage an arbitrary field, and nothing so far says which one nature uses. Determining it from the matter present is Chapter 3.6, and requires curvature, which requires this chapter.

In plain terms 3.3.3

The quantity a wristwatch reads was defined in the previous part using the fixed geometry of empty spacetime, and the definition survives the move to a variable geometry with no change of wording, because it only ever referred to the rule for measuring separations. Feed it the new rule and it computes elapsed time along any history whatever. For somebody sitting still, only one entry in the rule survives, and their watch differs from the coordinate clock by the square root of that single entry.

That last sentence is a promise being collected. The equivalence-principle chapter showed, using only an accelerating cabin and the ordinary Doppler effect, that two stationary clocks at different heights tick at different rates. It then wrote down what the timekeeping entry of the geometry would have to be, and left the notation for later. The notation has arrived, and the conclusion stands unchanged.

The number attached to each possible history is likewise inherited without alteration: elapsed watch time, multiplied by the mass and the square of the speed of light, with a minus sign. The previous part cornered it into that form by demanding that everyone compute the same value. What is startling is how little there is. No force appears and no potential energy appears, and the mass factors out and drops from the equations of motion, which is the universality of falling that started this part.

4 · The coordinate derivative is not a tensor

Here is the obstruction, in full. Chapter 3.2 §5.4 asserted it and deferred the computation to this section; Chapter 3.2 §7 listed it as the single entry of Chapter 2.4's table that goes wrong on a manifold. Everything from §5 onward exists to remove it, so it is shown completely rather than summarised.

Announce the destination. We differentiate the vector transformation law, find two terms where a tensor would have one, and identify the extra term exactly. It contains a second derivative of the coordinate change, and it vanishes if and only if that change is affine.

Line 1 — what we are differentiating. Chapter 3.2 (3.2.16) established

$$ V'^{\nu} \;=\; \pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;V^{\beta}. $$

Line 2 — the derivative operator in the new chart. By the chain rule (Chapter 0.6 §5), differentiating with respect to a primed coordinate means going through the unprimed ones:

$$ \pdv{}{x'^{\mu}} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\;\pdv{}{x^{\alpha}}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \partial'_{\mu} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\;\partial_{\alpha}. $$

Line 3 — apply it. Act with on . The object being differentiated is a product of two position-dependent things, the Jacobian factor and the components, so the product rule of Chapter 0.1 §4 applies and produces two terms:

$$ \partial'_{\mu}V'^{\nu} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\;\partial_{\alpha}\!\left(\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;V^{\beta}\right) \;=\; \underbrace{\pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;\partial_{\alpha}V^{\beta}}_{\text{the tensor part}} \;+\; \underbrace{\pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}\,\partial x^{\beta}}\;V^{\beta}}_{\text{the obstruction}}. $$

Read the two terms carefully, because the whole chapter turns on the difference between them.

The first term is exactly what Chapter 3.2 §7's definition demands of a type $(1,1)$ tensor: one factor of $\partial x'/\partial x$ for the upper index, one factor of $\partial x/\partial x'$ for the lower one, contracted against the components in the old chart. Had the calculation stopped there, $\partial_{\alpha}V^{\beta}$ would be a tensor and this chapter would not exist.

The second term is the obstruction, and note precisely what it is made of. It carries a second derivative of the new coordinates with respect to the old ones, and it is proportional to the components $V^{\beta}$ themselves rather than to their derivatives. Nothing about it can be absorbed into a redefinition of $\partial_{\alpha}V^{\beta}$, because it does not contain that object at all.

4.1 · When the obstruction vanishes, and why Part II never met it

The extra term dies exactly when $\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}/\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\beta}=0$ for all indices, which by integrating twice means

$$ x'^{\nu} \;=\; A^{\nu}{}_{\beta}\,x^{\beta} \;+\; b^{\nu} \qquad\text{with } A,b \text{ constant} $$

— an affine change of coordinates. Lorentz transformations and translations are precisely of this form, which is why Chapter 2.4 could list $\partial_{\nu}V^{\mu}$ in its table of tensors without qualification and never be caught out. The moment arbitrary smooth relabellings are admitted, as Chapter 3.2 §7 said they must be, the second derivative is generically non-zero and the entry is false.

4.2 · A concrete demonstration, in the plane

Abstract failures are easy to nod at and hard to believe. Here is the failure with numbers in it, in the flat plane of §2 — no curvature anywhere, only a change of chart.

Take the constant vector field $V=\partial_{x}$: the same arrow at every point of the plane, pointing along the $x$-axis, with Cartesian components $(V^{x},V^{y})=(1,0)$. Every derivative of every component vanishes in the Cartesian chart. Now find its components in the polar chart. By , with primed meaning polar,

$$ V^{r} \;=\; \pdv{r}{x}\,V^{x} \;=\; \cos\theta, \qquad V^{\theta} \;=\; \pdv{\theta}{x}\,V^{x} \;=\; -\,\frac{\sin\theta}{r}, $$

using $r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}$ so that $\partial r/\partial x = x/r = \cos\theta$, and $\theta=\arctan(y/x)$ so that $\partial\theta/\partial x = -y/r^{2} = -\sin\theta/r$. Now differentiate:

$$ \partial_{\theta}V^{r} \;=\; -\sin\theta \;\neq\; 0, \qquad \partial_{r}V^{\theta} \;=\; +\,\frac{\sin\theta}{r^{2}} \;\neq\; 0. $$

So the array $\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ is zero in one chart and non-zero in another, for a field that is manifestly the same field. No tensor behaves like that: the transformation law is linear and homogeneous in the components, so a tensor vanishing in one chart vanishes in all of them (Chapter 2.4 §6). The array $\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ fails that test, and the reason is visible in : the polar-to-Cartesian map is not affine.

Recap — what went in, what came out

In: the vector transformation law, the chain rule, and the product rule. Nothing else.

Out: $\partial'_{\mu}V'^{\nu}$ equals the tensor answer plus a term proportional to $V^{\beta}$ carrying the second derivative of the coordinate change. The coordinate derivative of a vector field is therefore not a tensor unless the charts are affinely related.

What it costs, and what it buys: the cost is that the most obvious way of differentiating a field is unusable in physics, since its answer depends on a choice nobody made. The purchase is that we now know the exact shape of the disease, which is what makes the cure of §5 constructible rather than guessed.

In plain terms 3.3.4

Differentiating a field means comparing its values at neighbouring points, and the previous chapter established that on a curved arena such a comparison is unavailable. One escape presents itself: work in a set of labels, treat the components as ordinary functions of those labels, and differentiate those. The result is a perfectly good array of numbers. The trouble is that the array describes the labelling as much as the field.

Following the calculation through shows precisely where the damage enters. Relating one labelling to another involves a matrix of rates of change, and taking a derivative makes that matrix get differentiated too, which produces a surplus term that a genuine measuring device would never carry. In the previous part the matrix was the same everywhere, so its derivative vanished and the problem never appeared; the moment arbitrary smooth relabellings are permitted, it does.

The failure is worth seeing with numbers rather than symbols, and the flat plane supplies them. Take a field of identical arrows all pointing the same way, which is as unchanging as a field can be. Describe it in circular labels and the components acquire a plain dependence on position, since the arrow points along the radius here and across it there. Differentiate those components and you get something non-zero, from a field that does not change. The array is reporting the turning of the labels, and nothing else.

5 · The connection, defined as the repair

We do not now guess an object and check that it works. We demand that the disease be cured, and read off what the cure must be. Announce the destination: write down the most general first-order correction to the coordinate derivative, require the corrected object to be a tensor, and derive the transformation law that the correction is thereby forced to obey. It does not be a tensor itself, which is the surprise, and is the reason it can cancel something that is not a tensor either.

5.1 · The definition

Definition — covariant derivative and connection

Introduce $n^{3}$ functions $\Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}(x)$ per chart, entirely unspecified for the moment, and define the covariant derivative of a vector field by

$$ \nabla_{\mu}V^{\nu} \;\equiv\; \partial_{\mu}V^{\nu} \;+\; \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}\,V^{\lambda}. $$

The functions $\Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}$ are the connection coefficients. The index convention used throughout this book is that the first lower index is the direction of differentiation. Books differ; check before copying a formula.

The correction has been taken linear in $V$ and free of derivatives of $V$, which is the least it can be if it is to cancel 's obstruction term — that term is itself linear in $V$ and free of derivatives of $V$. Nothing else about $\Gamma$ is assumed.

5.2 · What the connection must do, derived

Demand that $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ transform as a type $(1,1)$ tensor:

$$ \nabla'_{\mu}V'^{\nu} \;\overset{!}{=}\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\;\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;\nabla_{\alpha}V^{\beta}. $$

Write out the left side using the definition, and substitute for the first piece:

$$ \nabla'_{\mu}V'^{\nu} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\,\partial_{\alpha}V^{\beta} \;+\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\frac{\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\beta}}\,V^{\beta} \;+\; \Gamma'^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}\,V'^{\lambda}. $$

Now write the right side of out too:

$$ \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\,\nabla_{\alpha}V^{\beta} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\,\partial_{\alpha}V^{\beta} \;+\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\,\Gamma^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma}V^{\gamma}. $$

The first terms of and are identical and cancel between the two sides. What is left is a requirement on $\Gamma'$:

$$ \Gamma'^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}\,V'^{\lambda} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\,\Gamma^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma}\,V^{\gamma} \;-\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\frac{\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\beta}}\,V^{\beta}. $$

To strip off the vector we must get $V'^{\lambda}$ on the left into the same form as the $V$'s on the right. Use in reverse, $V^{\gamma}=(\partial x^{\gamma}/\partial x'^{\lambda})V'^{\lambda}$, on both right-hand terms; in the second term the summed index is called $\beta$, so relabel it $\gamma$ first so that the two terms carry the same letter. Then every term is proportional to $V'^{\lambda}$, which is arbitrary, so its coefficients must agree:

$$ \boxed{\;\Gamma'^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\gamma}}{x'^{\lambda}}\,\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;\Gamma^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma} \;-\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\gamma}}{x'^{\lambda}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\gamma}}.\;} $$

5.3 · Three consequences, and the surprising one first

(a) The connection is not a tensor. Compare with the tensor law of Chapter 3.2 §7. The first term is exactly right — one Jacobian per index, in the correct places, for a type $(1,2)$ tensor. The second term has no business being there. A tensor law is homogeneous in the components; this one is inhomogeneous, carrying an additive piece that does not involve $\Gamma$ at all.

The consequence is startling on first meeting and is worth stating baldly: the connection can be zero in one chart and non-zero in another. Set $\Gamma^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma}=0$ in and the first term dies, but the second does not, so $\Gamma'\neq0$ in general. That is not a defect. It is precisely the property required, because $\Gamma$'s job is to cancel a term that is itself not tensorial, and only a non-tensor can do that. Section 7 will exhibit the phenomenon concretely: the flat plane has $\Gamma=0$ in Cartesian coordinates and $\Gamma\neq0$ in polar coordinates, and is what relates the two. Chapter 3.1 §2.1 already met the same phenomenon in physical dress, when a uniform gravitational field was deleted by changing coordinates.

(b) The difference of two connections is a tensor. Suppose $\Gamma$ and $\tilde\Gamma$ both satisfy . Subtract the two statements. The inhomogeneous term is identical in both and cancels exactly, leaving

$$ \big(\Gamma'-\tilde\Gamma'\big)^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\gamma}}{x'^{\lambda}}\,\pdv{x'^{\nu}}{x^{\beta}}\;\big(\Gamma-\tilde\Gamma\big)^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma}, $$

which is the tensor law exactly. So the set of connections is not a vector space but an affine space: you cannot add two connections, but you can subtract them and get a genuine tensor. This is used twice more in the book, once in §7 and once in Part VI where the same structure reappears with an internal space in place of the tangent space.

(c) The obstruction is now exactly cancelled. By construction, not by luck. That was the demand from which was derived.

5.4 · Extending $\nabla$ to everything else

We have defined $\nabla$ on vector fields. Two further requirements fix it on everything else, and both are natural rather than arbitrary:

  • On a scalar field, $\nabla_{\mu}f\equiv\partial_{\mu}f$. This is forced, because $\partial_{\mu}f$ is already a tensor — it is the covector $\dd f$ of Chapter 3.2 §6, and nothing needs repairing.
  • $\nabla$ obeys the Leibniz rule on products of tensors, as any object calling itself a derivative must.

From those two, the rule for covectors is derived rather than posited. Take a covector $\omega_{\nu}$ and a vector $V^{\nu}$; their contraction $\omega_{\nu}V^{\nu}$ is a scalar, so

$$ \partial_{\mu}\big(\omega_{\nu}V^{\nu}\big) \;=\; \big(\nabla_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}\big)V^{\nu} \;+\; \omega_{\nu}\,\nabla_{\mu}V^{\nu}. $$

Expand both sides. On the left, the ordinary product rule gives $(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu})V^{\nu}+\omega_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$. On the right, substitute the definition of $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\nu}$:

$$ \big(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}\big)V^{\nu} + \omega_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu} \;=\; \big(\nabla_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}\big)V^{\nu} \;+\; \omega_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu} \;+\; \omega_{\nu}\,\Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}V^{\lambda}. $$

The terms $\omega_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ appear on both sides and cancel. In the surviving $\Gamma$ term the summed indices are $\nu$ and $\lambda$; relabel them, swapping the two letters, so that the free vector index reads $\nu$ like everywhere else — this changes nothing, since both are dummies. The term becomes $\omega_{\lambda}\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}V^{\nu}$, and now every term carries a factor $V^{\nu}$ with $V$ arbitrary. Matching coefficients:

$$ \boxed{\;\nabla_{\mu}\omega_{\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,\omega_{\lambda}.\;} $$

Note the minus sign, and note that it was not chosen — it was forced by the Leibniz rule and the requirement that the pairing be a scalar. Iterating the same argument on a general tensor gives the pattern: one $+\Gamma$ term per upper index and one $-\Gamma$ term per lower index. For the two cases used below,

$$ \nabla_{\rho}T_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\rho}T_{\mu\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu}\,T_{\lambda\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\nu}\,T_{\mu\lambda}, $$
$$ \nabla_{\mu}T^{\rho}{}_{\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}T^{\rho}{}_{\nu} \;+\; \Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\lambda}\,T^{\lambda}{}_{\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,T^{\rho}{}_{\lambda}. $$

Equation is the one the whole of Chapter 3.4 will lean on, so it is worth a second look now: a $(1,1)$ tensor gets three terms, a derivative and one connection term per index, with the sign set by whether the index is up or down.

In plain terms 3.3.5

Rather than inventing a repair and hoping it works, the move is to write down the most general thing that could repair the damage and let the requirement of good behaviour decide what it is. The damage was an unwanted extra term proportional to the field itself, so the repair is an added term proportional to the field itself, carrying unknown coefficients. Call them the comparison coefficients, since their whole business is comparison at a distance; insisting that the sum behave properly under relabelling determines exactly how they must respond to relabelling, and nothing is left to choose.

What emerges has a property that reliably surprises people. They do not respond to relabelling the way an honest measuring device does. They pick up an extra additive piece, so they can be zero in one description of a situation and non-zero in another. That is not a flaw but the entire point, since their assignment is to cancel a quantity that misbehaves in exactly that way, and only something equally badly behaved can do the cancelling.

Anyone who has followed this part should feel a jolt of recognition. A quantity that can be made to vanish everywhere by choosing your description well, but which no choice removes once you look at a large enough region, is precisely how the first chapter of this part described the gravitational field. The comparison coefficients are that field.

6 · Parallel transport

The covariant derivative measures how a field changes. Setting it to zero along a curve is therefore the statement that the field does not change along that curve, which is exactly the comparison Chapter 3.2 §5.3 could not supply.

Definition — parallel transport

Let $\gamma$ be a curve with tangent $u^{\mu}=\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda$. A vector field $V^{\mu}$ defined along $\gamma$ is parallel-transported along it if

$$ u^{\nu}\,\nabla_{\nu}V^{\mu} \;=\; 0 \qquad\text{at every point of } \gamma. $$

Unpack that into something computable. Expand the covariant derivative and then deal with the first term:

$$ u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}V^{\mu} \;=\; u^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}V^{\mu} \;+\; \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\,u^{\nu}V^{\rho}. $$

The first term is a directional derivative along the curve, and by the chain rule (Chapter 0.6 §5) that is an ordinary derivative with respect to the parameter:

$$ u^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}V^{\mu} \;=\; \dv{x^{\nu}}{\lambda}\,\pdv{V^{\mu}}{x^{\nu}} \;=\; \dv{V^{\mu}}{\lambda}. $$

So parallel transport is the system of ordinary differential equations

$$ \boxed{\;\dv{V^{\mu}}{\lambda} \;=\; -\,\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\big(x(\lambda)\big)\;\dv{x^{\nu}}{\lambda}\;V^{\rho}.\;} $$

Three observations, all of which matter later.

It always has a solution, and only one. Equation is a linear first-order system in the $n$ unknowns $V^{\mu}(\lambda)$, with coefficients that are smooth functions of $\lambda$ along the curve. ⚑ By the standard existence-and-uniqueness theorem for such systems, quoted here and used without proof, a value of $V$ at one point of the curve determines $V$ at every other point of it. So the comparison Chapter 3.2 lacked now exists: given a path, a vector at one end can be carried to the other.

The answer depends on the path. Equation is an equation along a curve, and different curves joining the same two points give different coefficient functions and hence, in general, different answers. Chapter 3.2 §5.3's figure showed exactly this happening on a sphere, before there was any machinery to describe it. Chapter 3.4 takes the path-dependence, makes it quantitative, and calls it curvature.

Nothing yet says lengths are preserved. With $\Gamma$ still arbitrary, a transported vector may grow or shrink. Section 7 imposes the condition that stops it, and the figure below measures the result.

In plain terms 3.3.6

Setting the repaired derivative to zero along a path says the field is not changing as you walk that path, and that single sentence supplies the missing comparison. Plant a direction at one end, insist that it stay as unchanged as the arena permits at each step, and you arrive at the other end with a definite answer. The rule is an ordinary equation of the kind the differential-equations chapter solved, so a starting value fixes the result all along the route.

Notice the three words that had to be included. The answer is well defined given a path. Two routes between the same pair of points involve different equations, since the comparison coefficients are evaluated along different tracks, and their answers have no reason to agree. On a globe they visibly do not, and the previous chapter showed as much before any machinery existed to describe it. The disagreement is not a defect and no better construction removes it.

That disagreement is the whole subject of the next chapter. Make the route a small closed loop, carry a direction round it, and compare what returns with what set out. The mismatch shrinks as the loop shrinks, and the rate at which it shrinks belongs to the point rather than to the loop. That quantity is curvature, and by the end of the next chapter it will be the same thing as the tide.

7 · The Christoffel formula

This is the chapter's set piece, so we go slowly and announce everything before doing it.

Where we are going, in three steps

So far $\Gamma$ is any array obeying , and there are infinitely many. Two demands cut the field down to exactly one, and the point of this section is to derive it.

Demand 1 — metric compatibility, $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$. Parallel transport should not change the length of a vector or the angle between two. We show below that this is exactly what the equation says.

Demand 2 — vanishing torsion, $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}$. Second covariant derivatives of a scalar should commute, as ordinary second partial derivatives do.

The derivation, in three steps. Step 1: write Demand 1 out in full, with the connection appearing twice. Step 2: write two more copies of the same equation with the three free indices cyclically permuted. Step 3: add two of the three and subtract the third; four of the six connection terms cancel in pairs against each other, leaving one, which is then freed by raising an index.

7.1 · Demand 1, and what it actually says

Take two vectors $V$ and $W$, both parallel-transported along a curve with tangent $u$, and ask how their inner product changes along the way. The inner product is a scalar, so its rate of change along the curve is the ordinary derivative, which by is $u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}$ acting on it:

$$ \dv{}{\lambda}\Big(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}W^{\nu}\Big) \;=\; u^{\rho}\,\nabla_{\rho}\Big(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}W^{\nu}\Big). $$

Apply the Leibniz rule to the three-fold product on the right:

$$ u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}\big(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}W^{\nu}\big) \;=\; \big(u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}\big)V^{\mu}W^{\nu} \;+\; g_{\mu\nu}\big(u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}V^{\mu}\big)W^{\nu} \;+\; g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}\big(u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}W^{\nu}\big). $$

The last two terms vanish, because $V$ and $W$ are parallel-transported and that is precisely the statement $u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}V^{\mu}=0$. So

$$ \dv{}{\lambda}\Big(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}W^{\nu}\Big) \;=\; \big(u^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}\big)\,V^{\mu}W^{\nu}. $$

Read both ways. If $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$ then every inner product is constant along every curve, so lengths and angles are preserved by transport. Conversely, if inner products are preserved for all choices of $V$, $W$ and all curves, then the coefficients of $V^{\mu}W^{\nu}$ must vanish for every $u$, giving $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$. The condition and the geometric statement are the same thing.

That is why the figure in §6 reports a length constant to fourteen digits. It is not an accident of the numerical scheme; it is Demand 1, which we are about to impose, showing itself.

7.2 · Demand 2, and why it is a real condition

Apply two covariant derivatives to a scalar $f$. The first gives the covector $\partial_{\nu}f$; the second differentiates a covector, so applies:

$$ \nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}f \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}f \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\lambda}f. $$

Now swap $\mu$ and $\nu$ and subtract. The ordinary second derivatives cancel by Clairaut's theorem (Chapter 0.6 §6.1), leaving

$$ \nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}f \;-\; \nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}f \;=\; -\,\Big(\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}\Big)\,\partial_{\lambda}f \;\equiv\; -\,T^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\lambda}f. $$

The array $T^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$ is called the torsion, and two facts about it are worth deriving rather than asserting.

Torsion is a tensor, even though $\Gamma$ is not. Look at the inhomogeneous term in : it carries $\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}/\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\gamma}$, which is symmetric in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ by Clairaut, and it is contracted with two Jacobians carrying the lower indices $\mu$ and $\lambda$. So the whole inhomogeneous term is symmetric under $\mu\leftrightarrow\lambda$ and cancels when the antisymmetric part is taken. What survives transforms homogeneously — the tensor law exactly. Setting a tensor to zero is a statement independent of chart, so Demand 2 is a legitimate condition rather than a coordinate convention.

Setting it to zero is a genuine choice, with a reason. A connection with torsion is mathematically consistent; nothing so far forbids it. We set it to zero because shows that torsion is precisely the failure of second derivatives of a scalar to commute, and Chapter 3.2 built the entire manifold on smooth functions whose mixed partials do commute. ⚑ That general relativity chooses the torsion-free connection is an assumption about nature, and theories with torsion have been constructed and tested; the observational situation is that no torsion has been detected. We flag the choice and proceed.

7.3 · Counting, before any algebra

It is worth knowing in advance whether a unique answer should be expected. In four dimensions:

  • Unknowns. $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$ has one free upper index (4 values) and a pair of lower indices which Demand 2 makes symmetric, so $4\times5/2=10$ independent pairs. Total $4\times10=40$ functions.
  • Equations. $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$ has one free index $\rho$ (4 values) and a symmetric pair $\mu\nu$ (10 values). Total $4\times10=40$ equations.

Forty linear equations for forty unknowns. A unique solution is exactly what one should expect, and the algebra below produces it. Had the counting come out unequal we would have known in advance that either no connection or infinitely many satisfy the demands.

7.4 · Step 1 — write the condition out

The metric is a type $(0,2)$ tensor, so gives its covariant derivative. Setting that to zero:

$$ \textbf{(A)}\qquad \partial_{\rho}\,g_{\mu\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu}\,g_{\lambda\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\nu}\,g_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; 0. $$

7.5 · Step 2 — two cyclic copies

Equation holds for every assignment of the three free indices, so we may write it again with the letters moved around. Cycle them once, $\rho\to\mu$, $\mu\to\nu$, $\nu\to\rho$, meaning: wherever (A) has $\rho$ write $\mu$, wherever it has $\mu$ write $\nu$, wherever it has $\nu$ write $\rho$. The summed index $\lambda$ is untouched, being private to each term:

$$ \textbf{(B)}\qquad \partial_{\mu}\,g_{\nu\rho} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,g_{\lambda\rho} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\rho}\,g_{\nu\lambda} \;=\; 0. $$

Cycle once more, by the same rule applied to (B), so that overall $\rho\to\nu$, $\mu\to\rho$, $\nu\to\mu$ relative to (A):

$$ \textbf{(C)}\qquad \partial_{\nu}\,g_{\rho\mu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\rho}\,g_{\lambda\mu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}\,g_{\rho\lambda} \;=\; 0. $$

A third cycle would return (A), which is the check that the permutation was done correctly.

7.6 · Step 3 — the combination (B) + (C) − (A)

Now form that combination. Take it slowly; the derivative terms first, then the six connection terms one pair at a time. The derivative terms collect:

$$ \partial_{\mu}g_{\nu\rho} \;+\; \partial_{\nu}g_{\rho\mu} \;-\; \partial_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}. $$

The six connection terms are, written out with the signs they carry after (A) has been subtracted:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{from (B):}\quad & -\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,g_{\lambda\rho} \;\;-\;\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\rho}\,g_{\nu\lambda}\\[3pt] \text{from (C):}\quad & -\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\rho}\,g_{\lambda\mu} \;\;-\;\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}\,g_{\rho\lambda}\\[3pt] \text{from }-\text{(A):}\quad & +\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu}\,g_{\lambda\nu} \;\;+\;\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\nu}\,g_{\mu\lambda}. \end{aligned} $$

First cancellation. Compare the second term of the (B) row with the first term of the $-$(A) row:

$$ -\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\rho}\,g_{\nu\lambda} \;+\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu}\,g_{\lambda\nu} \;=\; 0. $$

Two separate symmetries were used and both should be named. The connections match because $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\rho}=\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu}$, which is Demand 2. The metrics match because $g_{\nu\lambda}=g_{\lambda\nu}$, which is the symmetry of the metric from §1. With both factors equal and the signs opposite, the pair cancels exactly.

Second cancellation. Compare the first term of the (C) row with the second term of the $-$(A) row:

$$ -\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\rho}\,g_{\lambda\mu} \;+\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\nu}\,g_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; 0, $$

by exactly the same two symmetries, applied to the index pair $\nu\rho$ this time.

What survives. The two remaining terms are the first of the (B) row and the second of the (C) row:

$$ -\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,g_{\lambda\rho} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}\,g_{\rho\lambda} \;=\; -\,2\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,g_{\lambda\rho}, $$

where again both symmetries were used: Demand 2 turns $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}$ into $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$, and the symmetry of the metric turns $g_{\rho\lambda}$ into $g_{\lambda\rho}$, so the two terms are identical and add.

Putting and together, the combination (B) + (C) − (A) reads

$$ \partial_{\mu}g_{\nu\rho} \;+\; \partial_{\nu}g_{\rho\mu} \;-\; \partial_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu} \;-\; 2\,\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,g_{\lambda\rho} \;=\; 0. $$

7.7 · Step 4 — free the connection

Only one thing remains: the surviving $\Gamma$ carries a metric factor, and we want it bare. Multiply through by $\half g^{\rho\sigma}$ and sum over $\rho$. On the connection term, $g^{\rho\sigma}g_{\lambda\rho}=\delta^{\sigma}{}_{\lambda}$ by , and the Kronecker delta then does its only job, which is to rename $\lambda$ into $\sigma$ and collapse the sum. Rearranging so that the connection is alone on the left, and finally relabelling the free index $\sigma\to\lambda$ and the summed index $\rho\to\sigma$ so the result reads in the conventional letters:

$$ \boxed{\;\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \half\,g^{\lambda\sigma}\Big(\partial_{\mu}g_{\nu\sigma} \;+\; \partial_{\nu}g_{\sigma\mu} \;-\; \partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}\Big).\;} $$

These are the Christoffel symbols, and is the Levi-Civita connection of the metric $g$. Two consistency checks, both immediate:

It is symmetric in $\mu\nu$, as Demand 2 requires. Swap the two letters on the right: the first two terms exchange with each other, and the third is already symmetric because $g_{\mu\nu}=g_{\nu\mu}$. Nothing changes. Had the formula come out unsymmetric we would have derived a contradiction.

It vanishes when the components are constant. If $\partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}=0$ everywhere — Cartesian coordinates on the plane, or the Minkowski chart of Part II — every term on the right of is zero, so $\Gamma=0$ and $\nabla_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}$. All of Part II's calculus is recovered exactly, which it had better be.

Recap — what went in, what came out

In: the requirement that transport preserve lengths and angles, the requirement that second derivatives of a scalar commute, the symmetry of the metric, and one raising of an index.

Out: a unique connection, , built entirely out of the metric and its first derivatives. There is no freedom left. Once you have fixed how lengths are measured, you have fixed how vectors are compared, and you had no say in the matter.

What it cost: two assumptions, both flagged. Metric compatibility is what makes geometry geometric rather than merely differential. Vanishing torsion is a genuine physical choice, marked ⚑ in §7.2. Everything else was algebra.

7.8 · The flat plane in polar coordinates, computed

Section 2 promised this calculation. The metric is , so $g_{rr}=1$, $g_{\theta\theta}=r^{2}$, $g_{r\theta}=0$, and the inverse is $g^{rr}=1$, $g^{\theta\theta}=1/r^{2}$, $g^{r\theta}=0$. Exactly one derivative of the metric is non-zero:

$$ \partial_{r}g_{\theta\theta} \;=\; 2r, \qquad\text{and every other } \partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\nu}=0. $$

Feed that into , one component at a time. Since the metric is diagonal, the sum over $\sigma$ has a single surviving term in each case.

$$ \Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta} \;=\; \half\,g^{rr}\Big(\underbrace{\partial_{\theta}g_{\theta r}}_{=0} + \underbrace{\partial_{\theta}g_{r\theta}}_{=0} - \underbrace{\partial_{r}g_{\theta\theta}}_{=2r}\Big) \;=\; \half\cdot 1\cdot(-2r) \;=\; -\,r. $$
$$ \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta} \;=\; \half\,g^{\theta\theta}\Big(\underbrace{\partial_{r}g_{\theta\theta}}_{=2r} + \underbrace{\partial_{\theta}g_{\theta r}}_{=0} - \underbrace{\partial_{\theta}g_{r\theta}}_{=0}\Big) \;=\; \half\cdot\frac{1}{r^{2}}\cdot 2r \;=\; \frac{1}{r}. $$

By the symmetry just established, $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta r}=\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}=1/r$. The remaining four components all vanish: $\Gamma^{r}{}_{rr}$, $\Gamma^{r}{}_{r\theta}$, $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{rr}$ and $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta\theta}$ each involve only derivatives that are zero by , or in the last case $\partial_{\theta}g_{\theta\theta}=\partial_{\theta}(r^{2})=0$. So

$$ \Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta} \;=\; -\,r, \qquad \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta} \;=\; \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta r} \;=\; \frac{1}{r}, \qquad \text{all others zero.} $$
⚠ Read this against §2

The plane is flat. Its connection coefficients in Cartesian coordinates are zero and in polar coordinates are not. Nothing about the plane changed between those two sentences; only the chart did, and is the law that relates the two answers — the entire content of is the inhomogeneous term of that law, since the first term is zero.

So a non-zero connection is not evidence of curvature, any more than position-dependent metric components were. In Chapter 3.4 §8 we compute the curvature of exactly this metric and find every component identically zero, which is the third and final form of §2's warning.

Familiar ground — where the centrifugal term went

Chapter 1.1 §4 wrote Newton's second law in plane polar coordinates and found two unexplained terms, attributing them vaguely to "the basis directions turning as you move". Its problem set promised that the residue would be called $\Gamma^{i}{}_{jk}$ from this chapter onward, and Chapter 3.2's Worked example 2 sharpened the diagnosis without being able to complete it.

Equation is the residue, and §9's second worked example puts it into the equation of motion and recovers $\ddot r - r\dot\theta^{2}=0$ and $\ddot\theta + (2/r)\dot r\dot\theta=0$ exactly. The centrifugal term is $\Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}$ and the Coriolis term is $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}$. They were never forces; they were the connection of a flat space in a curvilinear chart.

Chapter 3.1 §2.1 explained in advance why gravity is nonetheless different from the centrifugal term, and the difference is now sayable in one line. Both are connection coefficients. The centrifugal one can be removed everywhere at once by returning to Cartesian coordinates; the gravitational one cannot be removed anywhere except at a point, and the obstruction to removing it is the subject of the next chapter.

In plain terms 3.3.7

Up to this point the comparison coefficients were almost entirely free. Any set of them behaving correctly under relabelling would do, and there were infinitely many. Two requirements cut that down to exactly one, and neither is a technicality. The first is that carrying a direction along a path must not change its length or its angle to a companion, since a rule that quietly stretched things would be measuring nothing. The second is that two derivatives of an ordinary quantity taken in either order must agree, a property the arena was built to have.

Counting first tells you what to expect. In four dimensions the unknown coefficients number forty and the first requirement supplies forty equations, so a single answer is what should come out. The derivation obliges, by a trick worth naming: write the requirement three times with its indices rotated, add two copies and subtract the third, and four of the six unwanted terms annihilate in pairs because the comparison coefficients are symmetric and so is the measuring device.

What survives is a formula giving the comparison coefficients entirely in terms of the rule for measuring lengths and how it varies from place to place. That is a strong statement about the world rather than about the algebra. Fix how distances are measured and you have, without any further choice, fixed how directions at neighbouring points are matched up.

8 · Geodesics, twice

A geodesic is the curved-space replacement for a straight line, and there are two entirely different ways to say what a straight line is. It is the curve that never turns, and it is the shortest route between two points. In Euclidean geometry these agree, and the agreement is so familiar it is invisible. Here they are two separate definitions with two separate derivations, and we do both and compare.

8.1 · First route — the curve that transports its own tangent

"Never turns" means: the direction you are travelling in, carried forward by the transport rule of §6, is still the direction you are travelling in. In symbols, the tangent is parallel-transported along its own curve:

$$ u^{\nu}\,\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu} \;=\; 0, \qquad u^{\mu} \;=\; \dv{x^{\mu}}{\lambda}. $$

Expand it exactly as in §6 — the first term becomes a derivative with respect to the parameter by , and the second is the connection term with $V$ replaced by $u$:

$$ \dv{u^{\mu}}{\lambda} \;+\; \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\,u^{\nu}u^{\rho} \;=\; 0. $$

Writing $u^{\mu}=\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda$ in both places gives the geodesic equation:

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{\dd^{2}x^{\mu}}{\dd\lambda^{2}} \;+\; \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\;\dv{x^{\nu}}{\lambda}\,\dv{x^{\rho}}{\lambda} \;=\; 0.\;} $$

The parameter is not free. Equation is not invariant under an arbitrary reparametrisation; it holds for affine parameters, those related to each other by $\lambda\to a\lambda+b$ with constants $a,b$. Proper time is one of them, and here is the derivation of that fact. Compute the rate of change of $g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}$ along the curve using with $V=W=u$; metric compatibility kills the first factor, and kills what is left:

$$ \dv{}{\lambda}\Big(g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\Big) \;=\; 0. $$

So the "speed" $g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}$ is constant along a geodesic. For a timelike geodesic we may therefore normalise it to $c^{2}$ once and for all, and by that choice is exactly $\lambda=\tau$.

8.2 · Second route — extremise the proper time

Now forget parallel transport entirely. Take the action , which came from Chapter 2.5 and knows nothing about connections, and extremise it by Euler–Lagrange. Chapter 1.2 Worked example 2 did precisely this for the plane and the sphere and promised that the general case would be this chapter's business.

Drop the constant $-mc$ and work with

$$ F\big(x,\dot x\big) \;=\; \sqrt{g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}}, \qquad \dot x^{\mu} \;=\; \dv{x^{\mu}}{\lambda}. $$

A remark on the parameter, before varying. $F$ is homogeneous of degree one in $\dot x$: scale $\dot x$ by $k$ and $F$ scales by $k$. So under a change of parameter $\lambda\to\tilde\lambda$ the integrand transforms as $F(x,\dd x/\dd\tilde\lambda)\,\dd\tilde\lambda = F(x,\dd x/\dd\lambda)\,(\dd\lambda/\dd\tilde\lambda)\,\dd\tilde\lambda = F(x,\dd x/\dd\lambda)\,\dd\lambda$, so the integral — and therefore its Euler–Lagrange equation — is the same whichever parameter is used. That is Chapter 1.2 §7's form-invariance in a particularly clean special case. We may therefore vary with an arbitrary $\lambda$ and choose the parameter afterwards, which is what we do. Choosing it beforehand would restrict the competing paths and is not allowed.

The two derivatives. Differentiate $F$ with respect to the velocity first. The argument of the square root is $g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}$; differentiating it with respect to $\dot x^{\mu}$ hits each of the two velocity factors in turn and, since $g_{\alpha\beta}$ is symmetric, the two contributions are equal, giving $2g_{\mu\beta}\dot x^{\beta}$. The chain rule on the square root supplies $1/(2F)$:

$$ \pdv{F}{\dot x^{\mu}} \;=\; \frac{g_{\mu\beta}\,\dot x^{\beta}}{F}. $$

Now with respect to position. Only $g_{\alpha\beta}(x)$ depends on $x$, so

$$ \pdv{F}{x^{\mu}} \;=\; \frac{\partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\;\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}}{2F}. $$

Assemble Euler–Lagrange — Chapter 1.2 (1.2.22), in the form $\dv{}{\lambda}\big(\partial F/\partial\dot x^{\mu}\big)-\partial F/\partial x^{\mu}=0$:

$$ \dv{}{\lambda}\left(\frac{g_{\mu\beta}\dot x^{\beta}}{F}\right) \;-\; \frac{\partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\,\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}}{2F} \;=\; 0. $$

Now choose the parameter. Take $\lambda=\tau$. Then $F=\sqrt{g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}}=c$ by — a constant, which therefore passes through the derivative. Multiply through by $c$:

$$ \dv{}{\tau}\Big(g_{\mu\beta}\,\dot x^{\beta}\Big) \;-\; \half\,\partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\;\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;=\; 0. $$

Expand the total derivative. The factor $g_{\mu\beta}$ depends on $\tau$ only through the position, so the chain rule gives $\dd g_{\mu\beta}/\dd\tau=\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta}\dot x^{\alpha}$:

$$ g_{\mu\beta}\,\ddot x^{\beta} \;+\; \partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta}\;\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;-\; \half\,\partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\;\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;=\; 0. $$

Symmetrise the middle term. This is the one move in the derivation that is easy to miss, so here it is in full. The middle term is contracted with $\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}$, which is symmetric under exchanging $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Therefore only the symmetric part of $\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta}$ contributes, and we may replace it by its own average with the $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ relabelled copy:

$$ \partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta}\;\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;=\; \half\Big(\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta} + \partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\alpha}\Big)\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}. $$

Substituting into collects the three metric-derivative terms under a single factor of $\half$:

$$ g_{\mu\beta}\,\ddot x^{\beta} \;+\; \half\Big(\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta} + \partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\alpha} - \partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\Big)\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;=\; 0. $$

Raise the index. Multiply through by $g^{\nu\mu}$ and sum over $\mu$. On the first term $g^{\nu\mu}g_{\mu\beta}=\delta^{\nu}{}_{\beta}$, which renames $\beta$ into $\nu$ and collapses the sum:

$$ \ddot x^{\nu} \;+\; \half\,g^{\nu\mu}\Big(\partial_{\alpha}g_{\mu\beta} + \partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\alpha} - \partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\Big)\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta} \;=\; 0. $$

Recognise the bracket. Compare the coefficient in with the Christoffel formula . Take and rename its indices $\lambda\to\nu$, $\mu\to\alpha$, $\nu\to\beta$, $\sigma\to\mu$; it becomes

$$ \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\alpha\beta} \;=\; \half\,g^{\nu\mu}\Big(\partial_{\alpha}g_{\beta\mu} + \partial_{\beta}g_{\mu\alpha} - \partial_{\mu}g_{\alpha\beta}\Big), $$

which is character for character the coefficient in , using $g_{\beta\mu}=g_{\mu\beta}$ in the first term. Hence

$$ \frac{\dd^{2}x^{\nu}}{\dd\tau^{2}} \;+\; \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\alpha\beta}\;\dv{x^{\alpha}}{\tau}\,\dv{x^{\beta}}{\tau} \;=\; 0, $$

which is exactly.

Why the agreement is worth something

Count the shared assumptions of the two derivations, because there are almost none.

The first route used parallel transport, metric compatibility and vanishing torsion, and never mentioned an action, a variational principle, or the length of anything.

The second route used the action $-mc^{2}\!\int\dd\tau$ inherited from Chapter 2.5, the Euler–Lagrange equation from Chapter 1.2, and the symmetry of $\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}$. It never mentioned transport, never imposed $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$, and never required the connection to be symmetric — the symmetric combination appeared, because only the symmetric part of anything survives contraction with $\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}$.

The same equation came out both times. Straightest and shortest are the same curve, and they are the same curve because of the theorem of §7 — the connection that preserves lengths is the connection that the length functional produces. That is the content of the name Levi-Civita, and it is the reason this book's two halves, geometry and action principles, meet here.

8.3 · The Newtonian limit, and gravity as geometry

The claim of Part III is that a falling body is following a geodesic. That claim earns nothing until it reproduces Newton, so here is the check, with each approximation named as it is made.

Assumption 1 — slow motion. The body moves slowly compared with light, so $\abs{\dd x^{i}/\dd\tau}\ll c\,\dd t/\dd\tau$. Then in the sum $\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\dot x^{\nu}\dot x^{\rho}$ the dominant term is by far the one with $\nu=\rho=0$, and we keep only it.

Assumption 2 — a static field. All time derivatives of the metric vanish, $\partial_{0}g_{\mu\nu}=0$.

Assumption 3 — a weak field. $g_{00}=1+2\Phi/c^{2}$ with $\abs{\Phi}/c^{2}\ll1$, which is Chapter 3.1 §6.5's derived result, obtained there from the accelerating cabin and the Doppler effect with no general relativity used. The spatial part of the metric is $g_{ij}=-\delta_{ij}+O(\Phi/c^{2})$; we shall not need to know the correction, and it is worth watching why.

Compute $\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}$ from . Two of the three terms carry $\partial_{0}$ and die by Assumption 2, leaving

$$ \Gamma^{i}{}_{00} \;=\; \half\,g^{i\sigma}\Big(\underbrace{\partial_{0}g_{0\sigma}}_{=0} + \underbrace{\partial_{0}g_{\sigma0}}_{=0} - \partial_{\sigma}g_{00}\Big) \;=\; -\,\half\,g^{ij}\,\partial_{j}g_{00}, $$

where the sum over $\sigma$ collapsed to spatial values because $\partial_{0}g_{00}=0$ removes the $\sigma=0$ term. Now insert $g^{ij}=-\delta^{ij}+O(\Phi/c^{2})$ and $\partial_{j}g_{00}=2\partial_{j}\Phi/c^{2}$. The correction to $g^{ij}$ multiplies a quantity that is already first order in $\Phi$, so it contributes at second order and may be dropped — this is why the spatial metric never had to be specified:

$$ \Gamma^{i}{}_{00} \;=\; -\,\half\big(-\delta^{ij}\big)\frac{2\,\partial_{j}\Phi}{c^{2}} \;+\; O\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{2}}{c^{4}}\right) \;=\; \frac{\partial_{i}\Phi}{c^{2}} \;+\; O\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{2}}{c^{4}}\right). $$

Put that into the geodesic equation for a spatial component, keeping only the $00$ term by Assumption 1:

$$ \frac{\dd^{2}x^{i}}{\dd\tau^{2}} \;=\; -\,\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}\left(\dv{x^{0}}{\tau}\right)^{2} \;=\; -\,\frac{\partial_{i}\Phi}{c^{2}}\left(c\,\dv{t}{\tau}\right)^{2}. $$

For slow motion $\dd t/\dd\tau=1+O(v^{2}/c^{2})$, so to leading order $\tau$ and $t$ may be interchanged and the two factors of $c$ cancel the $c^{2}$ in the denominator:

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{\dd^{2}x^{i}}{\dd t^{2}} \;=\; -\,\partial_{i}\Phi \;=\; g^{i}.\;} $$

Newton's law of motion under gravity, recovered with no force anywhere in the derivation. The right-hand side of came out of $\Gamma$, which came out of the metric, which is the geometry. Chapter 3.1 §2 argued that gravity was eligible to be geometry; is the first place where it actually is.

One thing is still missing, and it is worth naming so that no more is claimed than has been shown. Nothing here determines $\Phi$, or equivalently $g_{\mu\nu}$. We have shown how matter moves in a given geometry. What geometry a given lump of matter produces requires field equations, which require curvature, which is Chapter 3.4 and then Chapter 3.6.

In plain terms 3.3.8

Two different sentences describe a straight line, and on a flat page they agree so quietly that nobody notices there were two. One says a straight line never turns: keep going in the direction you are already going. The other says it is the shortest route between its ends. Each survives the move to a curved arena, each becomes an equation, and the two equations are derived here separately with almost no shared assumptions.

The first derivation says that the direction of travel, carried forward by the comparison coefficients, is still the direction of travel. The second forgets them entirely, takes the number attached to each history from the previous part, and asks which history makes it stationary, using machinery built for pendulums four parts ago. Both give the same equation with the same coefficients, which is no accident: the rule preserving lengths is the one the length functional generates.

Then the test that matters. Feed in a weak, unchanging field, allow only slow motion, and use the timekeeping entry the equivalence-principle chapter obtained from an accelerating cabin and nothing else. Out comes the law of falling bodies, with the familiar acceleration appearing not as a force but as a coefficient of the geometry. Gravity has stopped pushing and become a fact about the shape of the arena. Still entirely missing is any rule saying which shape a given lump of matter produces.

9 · Worked examples

Worked example 1 — the sphere, from metric to great circles

Take the sphere of radius $a$ with the metric it inherits from ordinary geometry. Compute every Christoffel symbol from , write down the geodesic equations, and show that the solutions are great circles.

The metric. Use the polar angle $\theta$ measured from the north pole and the azimuth $\phi$. Moving in $\theta$ at fixed $\phi$ traverses a great circle of radius $a$, so that displacement has length $a\,\dd\theta$; moving in $\phi$ at fixed $\theta$ traverses a circle of latitude whose radius is $a\sin\theta$, so that displacement has length $a\sin\theta\,\dd\phi$. The two directions are perpendicular. Hence

$$ \dd s^{2} \;=\; a^{2}\,\dd\theta^{2} \;+\; a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\;\dd\phi^{2}, \qquad g_{\theta\theta}=a^{2},\quad g_{\phi\phi}=a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta,\quad g_{\theta\phi}=0. $$

The inverse of a diagonal matrix is the diagonal matrix of reciprocals: $g^{\theta\theta}=1/a^{2}$ and $g^{\phi\phi}=1/(a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta)$. Exactly one derivative is non-zero:

$$ \partial_{\theta}\,g_{\phi\phi} \;=\; 2a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta, \qquad\text{all others } =0. $$

The Christoffel symbols. Because the metric is diagonal, the sum over $\sigma$ in keeps only $\sigma=\lambda$. Work through the six independent components.

$$ \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\phi\phi} \;=\; \half g^{\theta\theta}\Big(\partial_{\phi}g_{\phi\theta}+\partial_{\phi}g_{\theta\phi}-\partial_{\theta}g_{\phi\phi}\Big) \;=\; \half\cdot\frac{1}{a^{2}}\cdot\big(-2a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\big) \;=\; -\sin\theta\cos\theta. $$ $$ \Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\theta\phi} \;=\; \half g^{\phi\phi}\Big(\partial_{\theta}g_{\phi\phi}+\partial_{\phi}g_{\phi\theta}-\partial_{\phi}g_{\theta\phi}\Big) \;=\; \half\cdot\frac{1}{a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\cdot 2a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta \;=\; \cot\theta, $$

and $\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\phi\theta}=\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\theta\phi}=\cot\theta$ by symmetry. The other four vanish:

$$ \Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta\theta}=\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\theta\theta}=\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\phi\phi}=0, $$

each because every metric derivative appearing in it is zero — for instance $\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\phi\phi}=\half g^{\phi\phi}\partial_{\phi}g_{\phi\phi}$, and $g_{\phi\phi}=a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta$ has no $\phi$ in it. Note that $a$ has cancelled out of every symbol: the connection of a sphere does not know its radius. (Every symbol above was confirmed independently by computer algebra.)

The geodesic equations. Substitute into , remembering that the sum over $\nu\rho$ counts $\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\theta\phi}$ and $\Gamma^{\phi}{}_{\phi\theta}$ separately, which is where the factor of $2$ comes from:

$$ \ddot\theta \;-\; \sin\theta\cos\theta\;\dot\phi^{2} \;=\; 0, \qquad \ddot\phi \;+\; 2\cot\theta\;\dot\theta\,\dot\phi \;=\; 0. $$

The equator solves them. Put $\theta=\pi/2$ constant, so $\dot\theta=\ddot\theta=0$. The first equation needs $\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^{2}=0$, and $\cos(\pi/2)=0$ makes it so for any $\dot\phi$. The second needs $\ddot\phi+2\cot(\pi/2)\cdot 0\cdot\dot\phi=0$, and $\cot(\pi/2)=0$, so $\ddot\phi=0$, meaning $\phi$ advances at a constant rate. Both are satisfied. The equator, traversed at constant rate, is a geodesic.

Every great circle solves them. The metric is unchanged by any rotation of the sphere — rotations move points around but preserve all distances, so they carry solutions of the geodesic equation to solutions. Any great circle is the image of the equator under some rotation. Hence every great circle is a geodesic. This is Chapter 1.2's argument, unchanged; what has changed is that we now have the equation rather than only the variational problem. $\blacksquare$

Two checks. First, the second geodesic equation can be integrated once. Multiply it by $\sin^{2}\theta$ and notice that the result is a total derivative:

$$ \dv{}{\lambda}\Big(\sin^{2}\theta\;\dot\phi\Big) \;=\; \sin^{2}\theta\,\ddot\phi + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi \;=\; \sin^{2}\theta\Big(\ddot\phi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi\Big) \;=\; 0, $$

so $\sin^{2}\theta\,\dot\phi$ is constant along every geodesic. That is Clairaut's relation, which Chapter 1.2's grind box obtained from the absence of $\phi$ in the Lagrangian and identified as Noether's theorem in cartographic disguise. The two routes agree, as §8 said they must.

Second, numerical: integrating the two geodesic equations from a tilted initial direction with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme and fitting a plane to the resulting points gives a plane through the centre of the sphere to a relative precision of $1.8\times10^{-13}$, while $\sin^{2}\theta\,\dot\phi$ holds constant to $3.3\times10^{-13}$. A plane through the centre cuts the sphere in a great circle.

Worked example 2 — the free particle in polar coordinates, and a debt from Chapter 1.1

Use to write the geodesic equation of the flat plane in polar coordinates, and compare with the equations Chapter 1.1 §4 obtained by brute force. Then check the connection against the transformation law .

The equations. With $\Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta r}=1/r$, equation gives, for $\mu=r$ and then $\mu=\theta$,

$$ \ddot r \;-\; r\,\dot\theta^{2} \;=\; 0, \qquad \ddot\theta \;+\; \frac{2}{r}\,\dot r\,\dot\theta \;=\; 0. $$

The factor of $2$ in the second equation is again the two orderings $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{r\theta}\dot r\dot\theta$ and $\Gamma^{\theta}{}_{\theta r}\dot\theta\dot r$ being counted separately by the double sum.

These are exactly Chapter 1.1 §4's equations for a free particle, in which $-r\dot\theta^{2}$ was called the centrifugal term and $(2/r)\dot r\dot\theta$ the Coriolis term. Multiplying the second by $r^{2}$ gives $\dv{}{t}(r^{2}\dot\theta)=0$, conservation of angular momentum, by exactly the manipulation used on the sphere above.

What that settles. Chapter 1.1's problem set promised these terms would be called $\Gamma^{i}{}_{jk}$ from this chapter onward, and Chapter 3.2's Worked example 2 diagnosed them as the residue left over from comparing velocity vectors at neighbouring points without a rule for doing so. Both promises are now kept, and with a formula rather than a hand wave: the terms are connection coefficients of a flat space in a curvilinear chart, and computes them.

The independent check. Section 5.3 said the polar connection ought to be recoverable from the transformation law alone, since the Cartesian connection is zero. With $\Gamma=0$ in Cartesian coordinates, reduces to its inhomogeneous term:

$$ \Gamma'^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; -\,\pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\gamma}}{x'^{\lambda}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}x'^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial x^{\gamma}}, $$

where primed means polar. Take $\nu=r$, $\mu=\lambda=\theta$. The needed Jacobian entries are $\partial x/\partial\theta = -r\sin\theta$ and $\partial y/\partial\theta = r\cos\theta$, and the needed second derivatives of $r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}$ are, by two applications of the quotient rule,

$$ \frac{\partial^{2}r}{\partial x^{2}} = \frac{y^{2}}{r^{3}}, \qquad \frac{\partial^{2}r}{\partial x\,\partial y} = -\frac{xy}{r^{3}}, \qquad \frac{\partial^{2}r}{\partial y^{2}} = \frac{x^{2}}{r^{3}}. $$

Assemble the four terms of the double sum, writing $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$:

$$ \Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta} = -\Big[ r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\cdot\frac{y^{2}}{r^{3}} + 2r^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\cdot\frac{xy}{r^{3}}\cdot(-1)\cdot(-1) + r^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\cdot\frac{x^{2}}{r^{3}} \Big], $$

which on substituting $x,y$ becomes $-\big[r\sin^{4}\theta + 2r\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta + r\cos^{4}\theta\big] = -r\big(\sin^{2}\theta+\cos^{2}\theta\big)^{2} = -r$. That is , obtained by a completely different route: the first calculation used the metric and the Christoffel formula, this one used only the fact that $\Gamma=0$ in Cartesian coordinates and the way $\Gamma$ transforms. (Both routes were also confirmed symbolically; the other components agree in the same way.)

Problem 1 — every surface of revolution at once

A large family of two-dimensional metrics can be written $\dd s^{2}=\dd u^{2}+f(u)^{2}\,\dd v^{2}$ for some positive function $f$. (a) Compute all Christoffel symbols in terms of $f$ and $f'$. (b) Check your answer against §7.8 by taking $f(u)=u$, and against Worked example 1 by taking $f(u)=a\sin(u/a)$ with $u=a\theta$. (c) Take $f(u)=ku$ with $0<k<1$. Show that the geodesic equations are those of the plane with $\theta$ replaced by $kv$, so this surface is locally indistinguishable from the flat plane — and then say what is nevertheless different about it globally.

Solution

(a) $g_{uu}=1$, $g_{vv}=f^{2}$, $g^{uu}=1$, $g^{vv}=1/f^{2}$, and the only non-zero metric derivative is $\partial_{u}g_{vv}=2ff'$. From ,

$$ \Gamma^{u}{}_{vv} = \half g^{uu}\big(-\partial_{u}g_{vv}\big) = -f f', \qquad \Gamma^{v}{}_{uv} = \Gamma^{v}{}_{vu} = \half g^{vv}\,\partial_{u}g_{vv} = \frac{f'}{f}, $$

and all others vanish, every term in them being a derivative that is zero.

(b) With $f=u$: $\Gamma^{u}{}_{vv}=-u$ and $\Gamma^{v}{}_{uv}=1/u$, which is with $u=r$, $v=\theta$. With $u=a\theta$ and $f=a\sin\theta$: $f'=\dd f/\dd u=\cos\theta$, so $\Gamma^{u}{}_{vv}=-a\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^{v}{}_{uv}=\cos\theta/(a\sin\theta)$. Converting from $u$ back to $\theta$ divides the first by $a$ and multiplies the second by $a$, giving $-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\cot\theta$: Worked example 1 exactly.

(c) With $f=ku$, $\Gamma^{u}{}_{vv}=-k^{2}u$ and $\Gamma^{v}{}_{uv}=1/u$. The geodesic equations are $\ddot u - k^{2}u\dot v^{2}=0$ and $\ddot v + (2/u)\dot u\dot v=0$. Substituting $\vartheta=kv$ turns them into $\ddot u - u\dot\vartheta^{2}=0$ and $\ddot\vartheta+(2/u)\dot u\dot\vartheta=0$, which are the flat plane's equations. So locally this is a plane: the map $(u,v)\mapsto(u,kv)$ is an isometry onto a piece of the plane.

Globally it is not. If $v$ runs over $[0,2\pi)$ then $\vartheta=kv$ runs only over $[0,2\pi k)$, so the surface is a plane with a wedge of angle $2\pi(1-k)$ cut out and the edges glued — a cone. Walking a circle of radius $R$ around the apex covers a length $2\pi kR$, not $2\pi R$. The local geometry is flat everywhere away from the apex, and the defect is invisible to any purely local measurement. This is precisely the loophole that Chapter 3.4 §8 has to be careful about when it asks whether vanishing curvature implies flatness.

Problem 2 — the inverse metric is compatible too

(a) Starting from $\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0$ and the definition , prove that $\nabla_{\rho}g^{\mu\nu}=0$ as well. (b) Deduce that raising and lowering indices commutes with covariant differentiation, so that $\nabla_{\rho}\big(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\nu}\big)=g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\rho}V^{\nu}$ and it is unambiguous to write $\nabla_{\rho}V_{\mu}$. (c) Show that $\nabla_{\rho}\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=0$ directly from , and say why this had to be true.

Solution

(a) Apply $\nabla_{\rho}$ to and use the Leibniz rule:

$$ 0 \;=\; \nabla_{\rho}\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu} \;=\; \nabla_{\rho}\big(g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu}\big) \;=\; \big(\nabla_{\rho}g^{\mu\lambda}\big)g_{\lambda\nu} \;+\; g^{\mu\lambda}\underbrace{\nabla_{\rho}g_{\lambda\nu}}_{=\,0}, $$

using part (c) for the first equality. So $(\nabla_{\rho}g^{\mu\lambda})g_{\lambda\nu}=0$; contract with $g^{\nu\sigma}$, which turns $g_{\lambda\nu}g^{\nu\sigma}$ into $\delta_{\lambda}{}^{\sigma}$ and renames $\lambda$ to $\sigma$, giving $\nabla_{\rho}g^{\mu\sigma}=0$.

(b) By Leibniz, $\nabla_{\rho}(g_{\mu\nu}V^{\nu})=(\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu})V^{\nu}+g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\rho}V^{\nu}$, and the first term is zero. So it does not matter whether one lowers then differentiates or differentiates then lowers, and $\nabla_{\rho}V_{\mu}$ has one meaning. This is used constantly from Chapter 3.4 onward and is worth noticing as a convenience that had to be earned.

(c) From with $T^{\rho}{}_{\nu}=\delta^{\rho}{}_{\nu}$: the ordinary derivative of a constant array is zero, and the two connection terms are $+\Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\lambda}\delta^{\lambda}{}_{\nu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\delta^{\rho}{}_{\lambda} = \Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}=0$, each delta having renamed one index. It had to be true because $\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}$ is the identity map on each tangent space, and that map is the same object in every chart; a tensor whose components are the same numbers in every chart cannot have a non-zero derivative.

Problem 3 — what the geodesic equation cannot see

Let $\Gamma$ and $\tilde\Gamma$ be two connections with $\tilde\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}+S^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$. (a) Show $S$ is a tensor. (b) Show that the two connections have the same geodesics if and only if $S^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$. (c) Conclude that the geodesic equation alone cannot detect torsion, and say which of §7's two demands is therefore doing the work of singling out the Levi-Civita connection when we require the two derivations of §8 to agree.

Solution

(a) This is : both connections obey with the same inhomogeneous term, which cancels on subtraction.

(b) The geodesic equations differ by $S^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}\dot x^{\nu}\dot x^{\rho}$. The product $\dot x^{\nu}\dot x^{\rho}$ is symmetric in $\nu\rho$, so contracting it with $S$ picks out only the symmetric part of $S$, by exactly the argument of . The difference therefore vanishes for all curves precisely when $S^{\mu}{}_{(\nu\rho)}=0$, that is when $S$ is antisymmetric in its lower indices.

(c) The torsion is $T^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}$, which is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ by construction. By (b), adding any antisymmetric $S$ changes the torsion but not a single geodesic. So no observation of freely falling bodies can determine the torsion, and §8's agreement between the two routes is therefore secured by metric compatibility alone — the variational derivation produced a symmetric coefficient because only the symmetric part survived, not because torsion was ruled out. Demand 2 remains a separate assumption, which is why §7.2 flagged it.

Problem 4 — standing still is an accelerated motion

In a static metric, consider an observer who stays at fixed spatial coordinates. (a) Show that their four-velocity is $u^{\mu}=\big(c/\sqrt{g_{00}},\,0,\,0,\,0\big)$. (b) Show that their four-acceleration $a^{\mu}\equiv u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu}$ has spatial components $a^{i}=c^{2}\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}/g_{00}$. (c) Evaluate it in the weak field of §8.3 and interpret the answer.

Solution

(a) Fixed spatial coordinates means $u^{i}=0$. Normalisation $g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=c^{2}$ then reads $g_{00}(u^{0})^{2}=c^{2}$, so $u^{0}=c/\sqrt{g_{00}}$, taking the root that points to the future.

(b) Expand $a^{\mu}=\dd u^{\mu}/\dd\tau+\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}$. The metric is static and the observer does not move, so $u^{\mu}$ is the same at every point of the worldline and the first term vanishes. Only $\nu=\rho=0$ contributes to the second, giving $a^{\mu}=\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{00}(u^{0})^{2}=c^{2}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{00}/g_{00}$.

(c) With , $\Gamma^{i}{}_{00}=\partial_{i}\Phi/c^{2}$, and $g_{00}\approx1$, so $a^{i}=\partial_{i}\Phi$. For $\Phi=-GM/r$ this is $+GMx^{i}/r^{3}$, pointing away from the mass with magnitude $GM/r^{2}$.

The interpretation is the one Chapter 3.1 kept insisting on. Free fall is unaccelerated; it is standing on the floor that requires a four-acceleration, supplied by the floor pushing up, of exactly the magnitude Newton would have called the gravitational field. The accelerometer in your pocket agrees: it reads $9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ on the table and zero in free fall. Nothing in this problem used curvature, only the connection.

The arena now measures and compares. The metric $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ is an inner product on each tangent space, with signature $(+,-,-,-)$ everywhere, and it supplies lengths, angles, the classification of vectors into timelike, spacelike and null, the raising and lowering of indices, and — settling a debt from Chapter 0.6 — the gradient. Proper time and the free particle's action $S=-mc^{2}\!\int\dd\tau$ transfer from Part II with $\eta$ replaced by $g$ and no other change.

The connection was not guessed. Section 4 showed in full that $\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ fails to be a tensor and exhibited the offending second-derivative term; §5 defined $\Gamma$ as whatever cancels it and derived the inhomogeneous law it must therefore obey, from which it follows that $\Gamma$ is not a tensor and can be zero in one chart and non-zero in another. Section 7 imposed metric compatibility and vanishing torsion, counted forty equations against forty unknowns, and derived the unique answer by the cyclic-permutation trick. Section 8 derived the geodesic equation twice, once as a curve that transports its own tangent and once by extremising proper time, and the two agreed — which collects Chapter 1.2's promise in full — and then recovered Newton's law of falling bodies in the weak, slow, static limit using nothing beyond Chapter 3.1's independently derived $g_{00}$.

The warning you should be carrying. Position-dependent metric components do not mean curvature: the flat plane in polar coordinates has them. Non-zero connection coefficients do not mean curvature either: the same plane has $\Gamma^{r}{}_{\theta\theta}=-r$. Both facts are about the chart. A genuine test has not yet been built.

Where this gets spent. Chapter 3.4 builds the test, by carrying a vector around a closed loop and asking whether it comes back unchanged; the answer is the Riemann tensor, and the difference between two geodesics governed by it is Chapter 3.1's tidal equation. Chapter 3.5 generalises §6's transport into the Lie derivative and finds the conserved quantities that make Chapter 3.7 solvable. Chapter 3.6 writes field equations for $g_{\mu\nu}$, which is the one thing this chapter conspicuously did not supply. And in Part VI the whole construction is run again with an internal space in place of the tangent space, at which point becomes the transformation law of a gauge field.
