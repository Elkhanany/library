Forms, Lie Derivatives, Killing Vectors

A chapter that mostly collects. Three promises made earlier in the book fall due at once, and the tool that makes the next chapter but one solvable is built at the end.

1 · What integration wants, and why it wants antisymmetry

Announce the destination. We want objects that can be integrated over a $p$-dimensional surface sitting inside a manifold, with the answer depending on the surface and not on how the surface was labelled. We will find that this single demand forces the integrand to be totally antisymmetric, and that nothing else is forced.

1.1 · The case that already works: a line integral

Take a one-form $\omega=\omega_{\mu}\,\dd x^{\mu}$ — Chapter 3.2 §6 built these — and a curve $C$ given in some chart by $x^{\mu}(\lambda)$ for $\lambda\in[a,b]$. Define

$$ \int_{C}\omega \;\equiv\; \int_{a}^{b}\omega_{\mu}\big(x(\lambda)\big)\,\dv{x^{\mu}}{\lambda}\;\dd\lambda. $$

Two things about deserve to be said out loud, because the whole chapter is built on them.

No metric was used. There is no length, no angle and no volume anywhere in . A one-form eats a vector and returns a number, and the tangent $\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda$ is a vector; the pairing is the duality of Chapter 3.2 §6, which predates any metric.

The answer does not depend on $\lambda$. Reparametrise, $\lambda=\lambda(\sigma)$ with $\dd\lambda/\dd\sigma>0$. By the chain rule $\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\sigma = (\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\lambda)(\dd\lambda/\dd\sigma)$, and the ordinary change-of-variable rule for a single integral supplies $\dd\lambda = (\dd\lambda/\dd\sigma)^{-1}\dots$ — more carefully, $\dd\lambda=(\dd\lambda/\dd\sigma)\,\dd\sigma$, so the two factors are

$$ \omega_{\mu}\dv{x^{\mu}}{\sigma}\,\dd\sigma \;=\; \omega_{\mu}\dv{x^{\mu}}{\lambda}\,\dv{\lambda}{\sigma}\,\dd\sigma \;=\; \omega_{\mu}\dv{x^{\mu}}{\lambda}\,\dd\lambda. $$

The Jacobian of the reparametrisation appears once from the tangent vector and once, inverted, from the measure, and they cancel. That cancellation is the entire mechanism, and everything in §1.2 is the same cancellation in more dimensions.

1.2 · Two dimensions, where something has to give

Now try a surface. Parametrise a two-dimensional patch by $(u^{1},u^{2})\mapsto x^{\mu}(u)$, and suppose the integrand is some rank-2 tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$. The only expression available with the right index structure is

$$ \int T_{\mu\nu}\,\pdv{x^{\mu}}{u^{1}}\,\pdv{x^{\nu}}{u^{2}}\;\dd u^{1}\dd u^{2}. $$

Ask what happens under a change of parameters $u\mapsto u'$. Write the Jacobian matrix $J^{a}{}_{b}=\partial u^{a}/\partial u'^{b}$, with $a,b\in\{1,2\}$. Two things change, and we take them one at a time.

Change one — the measure. By Chapter 0.6 §8, $\dd u^{1}\dd u^{2}=\abs{\det J}\,\dd u'^{1}\dd u'^{2}$. For an oriented integral we drop the absolute value and keep $\det J$, agreeing to count a patch negatively if the new parameters reverse its handedness. That is a definition, and it is the same choice made in Chapter 0.7 when Stokes' theorem acquired a right-hand rule.

Change two — the two tangent vectors. By the chain rule, $\partial x^{\mu}/\partial u'^{b}=(\partial x^{\mu}/\partial u^{a})J^{a}{}_{b}$. So the pair of tangents feeding $T$ becomes

$$ T_{\mu\nu}\,\pdv{x^{\mu}}{u'^{1}}\,\pdv{x^{\nu}}{u'^{2}} \;=\; J^{a}{}_{1}\,J^{b}{}_{2}\;T_{\mu\nu}\,\pdv{x^{\mu}}{u^{a}}\,\pdv{x^{\nu}}{u^{b}}. $$

Abbreviate $T_{ab}\equiv T_{\mu\nu}(\partial x^{\mu}/\partial u^{a})(\partial x^{\nu}/\partial u^{b})$ — this is just $T$ evaluated on the two coordinate tangents, a $2\times2$ array of numbers. The demand is that be unchanged, which by change one means we need

$$ J^{a}{}_{1}J^{b}{}_{2}\,T_{ab} \;=\; \big(\det J\big)\,T_{12} \qquad\text{for every invertible }J. $$

Expand both sides. The left has four terms; the right, using $\det J=J^{1}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}-J^{2}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}$, has two:

$$ \begin{aligned} J^{1}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}T_{11} + J^{1}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}T_{12} + J^{2}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}T_{21} + J^{2}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}T_{22}\\ =\; J^{1}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}T_{12} \;-\; J^{2}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}T_{12}. \end{aligned} $$

The four products $J^{1}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}$, $J^{1}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}$, $J^{2}{}_{1}J^{1}{}_{2}$ and $J^{2}{}_{1}J^{2}{}_{2}$ can be varied independently, so their coefficients must match one at a time. Reading them off in order:

$$ T_{11}=0, \qquad T_{12}=T_{12}, \qquad T_{21}=-\,T_{12}, \qquad T_{22}=0. $$

The first and last say the diagonal vanishes; the third says the off-diagonal entries are opposite. Both are contained in the single statement $T_{ab}=-T_{ba}$, since setting $a=b$ in it gives $T_{aa}=-T_{aa}$ and hence $T_{aa}=0$. And since $\partial x^{\mu}/\partial u^{a}$ can be made to point in any two independent directions by choosing the patch, antisymmetry of $T_{ab}$ for all patches forces antisymmetry of $T_{\mu\nu}$ itself.

What was actually proved, and where you have met it before

The requirement was not imposed for elegance. We asked that a surface integral depend on the surface and not on the labelling, discovered that this forces the integrand to change by $\det J$, and then noticed that the only multilinear functions of two vectors that reproduce $\det J$ are the antisymmetric ones.

That last sentence is Chapter 0.4 §5, where the determinant was characterised as the unique alternating multilinear function of the columns normalised to $1$ on the identity. Here that characterisation is not a fact about matrices; it is the reason integrands in geometry are antisymmetric. Chapter 0.4 built the tool eleven chapters before there was anything to use it on.

1.3 · Differential forms, and the wedge

Define. A $p$-form is a totally antisymmetric tensor of type $(0,p)$: an object $\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}$ that changes sign whenever any two of its indices are exchanged. A $0$-form is a function; a one-form is a covector field; §1.2 showed that a $2$-form is what a surface integral wants.

Two pieces of notation, both used constantly from here on.

Antisymmetrisation brackets. For any array,

$$ T_{[\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]} \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\text{permutations }\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\;T_{\mu_{\pi(1)}\cdots\mu_{\pi(p)}}, $$

with $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=\pm1$ the sign of the permutation (Chapter 0.4 §5). The $1/p!$ makes the operation idempotent: antisymmetrising something already antisymmetric returns it unchanged, since all $p!$ terms are then equal to the original. For two indices, $T_{[\mu\nu]}=\half(T_{\mu\nu}-T_{\nu\mu})$, which is Chapter 2.4 §7.1's antisymmetric part.

The wedge product. Given a $p$-form $\alpha$ and a $q$-form $\beta$, their tensor product $\alpha_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\beta_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{q}}$ is not antisymmetric across the two groups. Antisymmetrise it and normalise:

$$ \big(\alpha\wedge\beta\big)_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p+q}} \;\equiv\; \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!}\;\alpha_{[\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\,\beta_{\mu_{p+1}\cdots\mu_{p+q}]}. $$

The combinatorial factor is chosen so that basis elements multiply without stray numbers; the smallest case shows what it does. For two one-forms, $(p,q)=(1,1)$ and the factor is $2!/1!1!=2$, so

$$ \big(\alpha\wedge\beta\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; 2\,\alpha_{[\mu}\beta_{\nu]} \;=\; \alpha_{\mu}\beta_{\nu} - \alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\mu}. $$

The sign rule, derived. Swap the roles of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in . To turn $\beta_{[\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{q}}\alpha_{\mu_{q+1}\cdots\mu_{p+q}]}$ back into the original ordering, every one of the $p$ indices of $\alpha$ must be moved past every one of the $q$ indices of $\beta$. That is $pq$ transpositions, each costing a minus sign inside a totally antisymmetric bracket. Hence

$$ \alpha\wedge\beta \;=\; (-1)^{pq}\,\beta\wedge\alpha. $$

Two one-forms anticommute, $\alpha\wedge\beta=-\beta\wedge\alpha$, and in particular $\alpha\wedge\alpha=0$ for any one-form. A one-form and a $2$-form commute. Nothing here is a convention: was counted.

The basis. Every $p$-form can be written in the basis built by wedging coordinate differentials,

$$ \omega \;=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\;\dd x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^{\mu_{p}}, $$

the $1/p!$ compensating for the fact that the sum runs over all orderings of each index set and each ordering contributes the same thing. Equivalently, sum over strictly increasing index sets with no factorial. For example a $2$-form in four dimensions is

$$ \omega \;=\; \sum_{\mu<\nu}\omega_{\mu\nu}\,\dd x^{\mu}\wedge\dd x^{\nu} \;=\; \omega_{01}\,\dd x^{0}\wedge\dd x^{1} + \omega_{02}\,\dd x^{0}\wedge\dd x^{2} + \cdots, $$

six terms in all.

The count. A $p$-form in $n$ dimensions has one independent component for each way of choosing $p$ distinct indices from $n$ — distinct, because a repeated index makes the component vanish by antisymmetry, and only the choice matters, because reordering changes only the sign. So the number of components is $\binom{n}{p}$, which is Chapter 2.4 §7.2's count. In four dimensions:

components $\binom{4}{p}$$1$$4$$6$$4$$1$$0$

Two entries in that row are worth pausing on. The $6$ is the number of independent components of the electromagnetic field tensor (Chapter 2.6 §2.3), which is therefore a $2$-form and not merely an antisymmetric array — §10 collects this. And the $0$ is not an accident of arithmetic: a $5$-form in four dimensions must have a repeated index by the pigeonhole principle, so every component vanishes. In $n$ dimensions there are no forms of degree above $n$, which is why the tower above stops.

In plain terms 3.5.1

An integral over a patch of surface ought to depend on the patch and on nothing else, and in particular not on the grid of labels somebody painted on it. Relabelling stretches the cells of the grid by a factor the earlier parts identified as a determinant, and it stretches the two edge directions feeding the integrand as well; for the two effects to cancel, the integrand must respond to its slots exactly as a determinant responds to two columns, changing sign whenever they are swapped.

So antisymmetry is not a stylistic preference or a tidy formalism. It is what survives the demand that an answer be about a region rather than about a description of one, which is the demand governing this book since its first chapter on coordinates. The objects obeying it are called forms, they carry one slot per dimension of the thing they are integrated over, and the product building larger ones out of smaller inherits its sign rule from the same counting.

One arithmetic fact will be wanted shortly. The number of independent entries such an object can have in four dimensions runs one, four, six, four, one, and then stops dead. The six is the count of electric and magnetic components together, which is no coincidence, and the stopping is why there is a largest possible integral rather than an endless tower.

2 · The exterior derivative, which needs no connection

Announce the destination. We want a derivative that turns a $p$-form into a $(p+1)$-form and is a tensor without any connection being chosen. We will find that antisymmetrising the ordinary partial derivative does exactly this, and that the reason it works is the same symmetry of the connection that Chapter 3.3 §7.2 called the torsion-free condition.

2.1 · The definition, and the three familiar cases

Define the exterior derivative of a $p$-form $\omega$ by

$$ \big(\dd\omega\big)_{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}} \;\equiv\; (p+1)\;\partial_{[\mu_{0}}\,\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}, $$

or equivalently, in the basis notation of ,

$$ \dd\omega \;=\; \frac{1}{p!}\,\big(\partial_{\nu}\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\big)\;\dd x^{\nu}\wedge\dd x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^{\mu_{p}}. $$

The factor $(p+1)$ in cancels the $1/(p+1)!$ hidden in the bracket, leaving a plain alternating sum. Take the three smallest cases one at a time.

$p=0$. A $0$-form is a function $f$, the bracket does nothing, and

$$ \big(\dd f\big)_{\mu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}f. $$

That is the gradient one-form of Chapter 0.6 §4 — the object that chapter insisted was a row and not a column, and which needed a metric to be turned into the arrow called $\nabla f$. Here it appears with no metric at all, which is the first sign that $\dd$ is cheaper than $\nabla$.

$p=1$. With two indices the bracket is $\half(\;\cdot\;-\;\cdot\;)$ and the prefactor is $2$:

$$ \big(\dd\omega\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; 2\,\partial_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu} \;-\; \partial_{\nu}\omega_{\mu}. $$

In flat three-dimensional space with Cartesian coordinates, identify a vector field $\vv F$ with the one-form $\omega_{i}=F^{i}$. Then $(\dd\omega)_{12}=\partial_{x}F^{y}-\partial_{y}F^{x}$, which is $(\nabla\times\vv F)_{z}$, and the other two independent components are the other two components of the curl. The exterior derivative of a one-form is the curl.

$p=2$ in three dimensions. Identify a vector field with a $2$-form by $\omega_{jk}=\epsilon^{ijk}F^{i}$ — the same identification Chapter 0.7 §3.1 made implicitly when it wrote a flux as $\vv F\cdot\dd\vv A$, since a flux integrand eats two edge vectors. Then

$$ \big(\dd\omega\big)_{123} \;=\; \partial_{1}\omega_{23} + \partial_{2}\omega_{31} + \partial_{3}\omega_{12} \;=\; \partial_{x}F^{x}+\partial_{y}F^{y}+\partial_{z}F^{z} \;=\; \nabla\cdot\vv F, $$

where the three-term alternating sum has collapsed to three terms rather than six because the bracket's other three terms are equal to these by antisymmetry of $\omega$. The exterior derivative of a $2$-form is the divergence.

Chapter 0.7 §7.4 drew the chain

$$ \text{functions} \;\xrightarrow{\ \nabla\ }\; \text{vector fields} \;\xrightarrow{\ \nabla\times\ }\; \text{vector fields} \;\xrightarrow{\ \nabla\cdot\ }\; \text{functions} $$

and said that in this chapter's language the three objects would be forms of degree $0,1,2$ and all three arrows would be one operator. That is now shown rather than promised: , and are the three arrows, and each is at a different value of $p$. The chain has a fourth arrow that Chapter 0.7 could not draw, taking $3$-forms to $4$-forms, which vanishes in three dimensions and does not in four.

2.2 · Why $\dd\omega$ is a tensor, with no connection anywhere

Chapter 3.3 §4 proved that $\partial_{\mu}V^{\nu}$ is not a tensor, and the whole of that chapter's §5 was the repair. Antisymmetrising escapes the problem entirely, and the reason is one line.

Write the covariant derivative of a one-form, from Chapter 3.3 (3.3.30):

$$ \nabla_{\mu}\omega_{\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu} \;-\; \Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}\,\omega_{\lambda}. $$

Antisymmetrise both sides in $\mu\nu$. On the left the result is $\nabla_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]}$; on the right, the first term gives $\partial_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]}$ and the second gives $-\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{[\mu\nu]}\omega_{\lambda}$. But $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric in its two lower indices — that is the torsion-free condition, imposed in Chapter 3.3 §7.2 and derived there to be equivalent to the vanishing of $\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{[\mu\nu]}$. So the second term is antisymmetrising something symmetric, which gives zero, and

$$ \big(\dd\omega\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; 2\,\partial_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]} \;=\; 2\,\nabla_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]}. $$

The same argument runs at every $p$: each of the $p$ correction terms in the covariant derivative of a $p$-form carries a $\Gamma$ with the differentiating index and one form index on its two lower slots, and antisymmetrising over those two kills it.

is worth stating twice, in both directions. Read left to right, it says that the plain partial derivative, antisymmetrised, is secretly covariant and is therefore a tensor. Read right to left, it says that $\dd$ can be computed with partial derivatives and no Christoffel symbols ever, even on a curved manifold, and that the answer does not depend on which connection you would have used. The exterior derivative exists before the metric does. Chapter 3.6 will lean on this without saying so; Chapter 6.3, where a connection on an internal space is built from scratch, leans on it harder.

⚠ The belief this section exists to prevent

That $\dd$ needs no connection does not mean it needs no geometry, and the two claims get run together constantly. What §2.2 proved is narrow and should be held at exactly its width: the $\Gamma$ terms cancel by antisymmetry, so $\dd$ is defined on a bare manifold, before a metric is chosen and independently of which connection anyone would have chosen. Nothing more.

Everything else in this chapter is paid for. Section 6 builds the object that lets an integral know how much region it is covering, and it is $\sqrt{\abs{g}}$ — pure metric. Section 8 asks which flows leave distances alone, a question that cannot even be posed without one. And §10 writes electromagnetism in three symbols, of which exactly one requires a geometry: the homogeneous half of Maxwell's equations is $\dd F=0$ and holds on any manifold whatsoever, while the sourced half needs the star, and the star is built from $g_{\mu\nu}$.

The slogan to carry is not "forms need no geometry". It is that differentiating is free and measuring is not, which is the same division Chapter 3.2 §6 drew between the object a function hands you for nothing and the arrow of steepest ascent it cannot.

In plain terms 3.5.2

Two chapters ago, differentiating a field turned out to be impossible without extra equipment, because the values being compared live at different points and nothing brings one to the other for free. Building that equipment took a whole chapter. Here a cheaper route opens, for the special class of objects the previous section forced on us, and the reason it opens is worth keeping.

Take the naive derivative, which fails because it drags along a term built from the comparison coefficients, and antisymmetrise the result over the differentiating slot and one other. The offending term carries those two slots in a pair of positions where the comparison coefficients are known to be symmetric, and antisymmetrising anything symmetric annihilates it. The failure cancels rather than being repaired, so this derivative can be computed with no comparison rule at all, and it returns the same answer whichever rule you would have chosen.

The payoff is that the three operations the toolkit taught separately, the one measuring steepness, the one measuring swirl and the one measuring outflow, stop being three operations. They are one operation applied to objects carrying one, two and three slots, and the reason they looked different is that vectors in three dimensions can stand in for all three types. That coincidence is over, and what replaces it survives in any number of dimensions and on any curved space.

3 · $\dd^{2}=0$, and three debts collected at once

This is the section the chapter exists for. Announce the destination and the collection together. We will prove one line — that applying the exterior derivative twice always gives zero — and then show that this single line is Chapter 0.7's identity that the curl of a gradient vanishes, Chapter 0.7's identity that the divergence of a curl vanishes, and Chapter 2.6's discovery that two of Maxwell's four equations carry no physical content. Those are not three analogous facts. They are one fact seen three times.

3.1 · The proof

Take any $p$-form $\omega$ and apply twice. The result has $p+2$ indices, and every term in it carries two partial derivatives:

$$ \big(\dd\dd\omega\big)_{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p+1}} \;=\; (p+2)(p+1)\;\partial_{[\mu_{0}}\partial_{\mu_{1}}\omega_{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p+1}]}. $$

Look only at the two derivative indices. They sit inside a total antisymmetrisation, so the expression changes sign when they are exchanged. But $\partial_{\mu_{0}}\partial_{\mu_{1}}=\partial_{\mu_{1}}\partial_{\mu_{0}}$ by Clairaut's theorem (Chapter 0.6 §6.1), so the expression is unchanged when they are exchanged. A quantity equal to minus itself is zero. Hence

$$ \boxed{\;\dd\circ\dd \;=\; 0\;}\qquad\text{on every form, of every degree, on every manifold.} $$

That is the whole proof, and its brevity is the point rather than a shortcut. Nothing was assumed except that $\omega$ is twice continuously differentiable, which is Clairaut's hypothesis and was stated out loud in Chapter 0.6 §6.1.

3.2 · Debt one — the two vector identities of Chapter 0.7

Set $p=0$. Then $\omega=f$ is a function, $\dd f$ is the gradient one-form , and reads, using ,

$$ \big(\dd\dd f\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}f \;-\; \partial_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}f \;=\; 0. $$

In flat three-dimensional space the three independent components of a $2$-form are the three components of a vector, and §2.1 identified $\dd$ on a one-form with the curl. So is $\nabla\times\nabla f=\vv 0$, which is (0.7.54) — proved there in exactly this way, by writing out one component and cancelling two terms.

Set $p=1$. Then $\dd\omega$ is the curl, $\dd\dd\omega$ is the divergence of it, and is $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vv A)=0$, which is (0.7.56) — proved there by writing out six terms and cancelling them in pairs. Here the six terms are the six permutations inside the bracket of , and they cancel for the same reason.

So the two identities of Chapter 0.7 §7 are at $p=0$ and $p=1$. Chapter 0.7 §7.4 asserted this and flagged it as a promise. It is now a theorem.

3.3 · Debt two — half of Maxwell's equations

Now four dimensions. Assemble the electromagnetic potential of Chapter 2.6 §2.2 into a one-form,

$$ A \;=\; A_{\mu}\,\dd x^{\mu}, $$

and take its exterior derivative. By ,

$$ \big(\dd A\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}A_{\nu} - \partial_{\nu}A_{\mu} \;=\; F_{\mu\nu}, $$

which is Chapter 2.6's definition (2.6.9) of the field tensor, character for character. So $F=\dd A$: the electromagnetic field is the exterior derivative of the potential, and its six components are the six independent components a $2$-form in four dimensions is allowed by §1.3's table.

Apply :

$$ \dd F \;=\; \dd\dd A \;=\; 0. $$

Write out what $\dd F=0$ says in components. $F$ is a $2$-form, so $\dd F$ is a $3$-form with $\binom{4}{3}=4$ independent components, and by the same collapse as in each is a three-term cyclic sum:

$$ \big(\dd F\big)_{\lambda\mu\nu} \;=\; \partial_{\lambda}F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_{\mu}F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_{\nu}F_{\lambda\mu} \;=\; 0. $$

That is (2.6.25) exactly. Chapter 2.6 §3.2 expanded its four components and found $\nabla\cdot\vv B=0$ and the three components of Faraday's law; Chapter 2.6 §3.4 then substituted $F=\dd A$, watched six terms cancel in pairs by Clairaut, and concluded that given potentials, those two Maxwell equations cannot fail and carry no information about how electromagnetism works.

Here is the same conclusion in one symbol. The six terms Chapter 2.6 cancelled are the six permutations in at $p=1$, and the cancellation is . Chapter 2.6's warning box promised precisely this and called it a promise rather than a derivation; the promise is now kept.

3.4 · What the fact actually is

Three results have just been shown to be one result. It is worth saying what the one result is, because it is not a fact about derivatives at all.

Chapter 0.7 §5.4 made the observation, filed it, and moved on: a boundary has no boundary of its own. The boundary of a disc is a circle, and a circle has no endpoints; the boundary of a ball is a sphere, and a sphere has no edge. In symbols, $\partial(\partial M)=\emptyset$.

Section 5 below proves the general Stokes theorem, $\int_{M}\dd\omega=\oint_{\partial M}\omega$, which pairs the operator $\dd$ with the operation of taking a boundary. Grant it for a moment and run the pairing twice. For any $(p-2)$-form $\omega$ and any $p$-dimensional region $M$,

$$ \int_{M}\dd\dd\omega \;=\; \oint_{\partial M}\dd\omega \;=\; \oint_{\partial(\partial M)}\omega \;=\; 0, $$

the last step because there is nothing to integrate over. Since this holds for every region $M$, however small, the integrand must vanish: $\dd\dd\omega=0$.

Read that chain in reverse and it says the same thing. The statement that applying the derivative twice gives nothing is the algebraic shadow of the statement that a boundary has no boundary. A fact about regions is showing up as a fact about derivatives. That is why it feels like a coincidence that the curl of a gradient and the divergence of a curl both vanish, and why it feels like a second coincidence that two of Maxwell's equations are empty: the coincidence is that a single geometric triviality about shapes has three separate algebraic disguises, and each disguise was met before the shape was.

Recap — what went in, what came out

In: the definition of $\dd$ as an antisymmetrised partial derivative, and Clairaut's theorem.

Out: $\dd\dd=0$, in three lines. Specialising to three dimensions gives both of Chapter 0.7 §7's identities; specialising to the electromagnetic potential in four dimensions gives both of Maxwell's homogeneous equations and, with them, Chapter 2.6's verdict that half of Maxwell is bookkeeping.

What it cost: nothing beyond the machinery of §1 and §2 — and note in particular that no metric, no connection and no curvature entered at any point. The result holds on any manifold whatever, flat or curved, before any geometry is put on it.

In plain terms 3.5.3

Apply the new derivative twice and the answer is always nothing. The proof takes three lines: the two derivative slots sit inside a construction that changes sign when any two slots are swapped, while the derivatives themselves do not care about their order, so the expression equals minus itself and therefore equals zero.

What that line collects is the reason the section exists. The toolkit proved, by grinding through components, that a field of steepest ascent has no swirl and that a field of swirl has no outflow, and remarked that the two proofs looked alike. The chapter on electromagnetism found that two of the four famous equations follow from the existence of potentials and say nothing about how electricity behaves. All three are one statement, and the six terms cancelling in each case are literally the same six terms.

And the statement is not really about differentiation. The edge of a filled disc is a closed loop, and a closed loop has no ends; the skin of a ball is a sphere, and a sphere has no rim. Taking a boundary twice leaves nothing. Because a later theorem pairs the derivative with the boundary, that triviality about shapes must show up as a triviality about derivatives, and it does. What looked like a run of coincidences is one plain fact met three times in the wrong order.

4 · Closed, exact, and the shape of the region

Section 3 answered one direction of a question. Here is the other, and it is the direction where the topology of the region stops being a technicality.

Two definitions. A form $\omega$ is closed if $\dd\omega=0$. It is exact if $\omega=\dd\alpha$ for some form $\alpha$ of one degree lower. Equation says exact implies closed, always, with no hypotheses. The question is the converse.

You have met this question three times without its name. Chapter 0.7 §2.3 asked when a curl-free field is a gradient — that is, when a closed one-form is exact. Chapter 0.7 §7.3 asked when a divergence-free field is a curl — a closed $2$-form being exact — and quoted the answer as the Poincaré lemma, saying this chapter would prove it. Chapter 3.4 §8 met the same distinction as the gap between vanishing curvature and being a piece of flat space. All three are the same question, and in each the obstruction is the shape of the region.

4.1 · The counterexample, so that the theorem has something to exclude

Chapter 0.7 §2.4 built the counterexample in full and it is worth having in the new notation. On the plane with the origin removed, define

$$ \omega \;=\; \frac{x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}. $$

It is closed. Compute $(\dd\omega)_{xy}=\partial_{x}\omega_{y}-\partial_{y}\omega_{x}$ with $\omega_{x}=-y/(x^{2}+y^{2})$ and $\omega_{y}=x/(x^{2}+y^{2})$. Differentiating the first by the quotient rule,

$$ \partial_{x}\omega_{y} \;=\; \frac{(x^{2}+y^{2}) - x(2x)}{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}} \;=\; \frac{y^{2}-x^{2}}{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}}, $$

and the identical computation on $\omega_{x}$ gives the same thing, so the difference is zero everywhere the form is defined.

It is not exact. If $\omega$ were $\dd f$ then $\oint\omega$ around any closed loop would be $f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})=0$. But on the unit circle, parametrised by $(x,y)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, the definition gives $\omega_{\mu}\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\theta = (\cos\theta)(\cos\theta)-(\sin\theta)(-\sin\theta)=1$, so $\oint\omega=2\pi\neq0$.

Nothing is wrong with $\omega$; something is wrong with the region. The punctured plane has a loop that cannot be shrunk to a point without crossing the missing origin, and that loop is exactly what $\oint\omega=2\pi$ detects. Closed but not exact is a measurement of the hole.

4.2 · The Poincaré lemma, for one-forms, proved

Call a region star-shaped about a point if the straight segment from that point to any point of the region stays inside. A ball is star-shaped; the punctured plane is not.

Claim. On a star-shaped region about the origin, every closed one-form is exact.

The construction. Given a closed $\omega$, define a function by integrating $\omega$ out along the straight ray from the origin to $x$:

$$ f(x) \;\equiv\; \int_{0}^{1}\omega_{\mu}(t x)\;x^{\mu}\;\dd t. $$

This is legitimate precisely because the region is star-shaped: the ray $tx$ for $t\in[0,1]$ stays inside, so $\omega$ is defined all along it.

Line 1 — differentiate under the integral sign. Chapter 0.2 §4.4 licensed this move for continuous integrands. Differentiating with respect to $x^{\nu}$ hits the argument $tx$ of $\omega$ by the chain rule, and separately hits the explicit factor $x^{\mu}$ by the product rule:

$$ \partial_{\nu}f(x) \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\Big[\;t\,\big(\partial_{\nu}\omega_{\mu}\big)(tx)\;x^{\mu} \;+\; \omega_{\nu}(tx)\;\Big]\dd t. $$

The factor $t$ in the first term is the derivative of the argument $tx^{\nu}$ with respect to $x^{\nu}$; the second term came from $\partial_{\nu}x^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}$.

Line 2 — use closedness, and only here. Closed means $\partial_{\nu}\omega_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}$. Swap them inside the first term:

$$ \partial_{\nu}f(x) \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\Big[\;t\,\big(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}\big)(tx)\;x^{\mu} \;+\; \omega_{\nu}(tx)\;\Big]\dd t. $$

Line 3 — recognise a product rule in $t$. Consider the function $t\mapsto t\,\omega_{\nu}(tx)$ and differentiate it with respect to $t$. The product rule gives one term from the explicit $t$ and one from the argument, and the chain rule supplies $x^{\mu}$ in the second:

$$ \dv{}{t}\Big[\,t\,\omega_{\nu}(tx)\,\Big] \;=\; \omega_{\nu}(tx) \;+\; t\,\big(\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}\big)(tx)\,x^{\mu}. $$

The right-hand side of is exactly the bracket in .

Line 4 — integrate. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Chapter 0.2 §2),

$$ \partial_{\nu}f(x) \;=\; \Big[\,t\,\omega_{\nu}(tx)\,\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1} \;=\; \omega_{\nu}(x). $$

So $\omega=\dd f$. $\blacksquare$

Read line by line, the proof is: integrate the form radially, differentiate, use closedness once to swap two derivative labels, notice that what remains is a total derivative in the radial parameter, and apply the Fundamental Theorem. The star-shaped hypothesis is spent in exactly one place — writing down at all.

Grind box — the same proof for a $p$-form, and hence the vector potential

The construction generalises with one extra factor of $t$ per index. For a $p$-form $\omega$ on a region star-shaped about the origin, define the $(p-1)$-form

$$ \big(K\omega\big)_{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p}}(x) \;\equiv\; \int_{0}^{1}t^{\,p-1}\;x^{\mu_{1}}\,\omega_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p}}(tx)\;\dd t. $$

The claim is the homotopy formula

$$ \dd\big(K\omega\big) \;+\; K\big(\dd\omega\big) \;=\; \omega, $$

from which the lemma is immediate: if $\dd\omega=0$ the second term dies and $\omega=\dd(K\omega)$ is exact.

The verification is the four lines of the main text with more indices, and rather than sketch it we do the case the promise was about — $p=2$ in three dimensions, where the claim is the existence of the vector potential — completely and explicitly.

Setting up. Under §2.1's identification a $2$-form corresponds to a vector field $\vv B$, and $K$ applied to it reads, in components,

$$ A_{k}(\vv x) \;=\; \int_{0}^{1} t\;\epsilon_{kij}\,B_{i}(t\vv x)\,x_{j}\;\dd t, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \vv A(\vv x) \;=\; \int_{0}^{1} t\;\vv B(t\vv x)\times\vv x\;\dd t. $$

Take the curl, differentiating under the integral sign. The derivative hits the argument $t\vv x$ by the chain rule, producing a factor $t$, and separately hits the explicit $x_{j}$:

$$ \big(\nabla\times\vv A\big)_{m} \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\epsilon_{mnk}\epsilon_{kij}\Big[\,t^{2}\,\big(\partial_{n}B_{i}\big)(t\vv x)\,x_{j} \;+\; t\,B_{i}(t\vv x)\,\delta_{jn}\Big]\dd t. $$

Use the epsilon identity $\epsilon_{mnk}\epsilon_{kij}=\delta_{mi}\delta_{nj}-\delta_{mj}\delta_{ni}$, which Chapter 0.7 §4.4's grind box established. The first bracket becomes $t^{2}\big[(\partial_{j}B_{m})x_{j}-(\partial_{i}B_{i})x_{m}\big]$, and here is the one place the hypothesis is spent: $\partial_{i}B_{i}=\nabla\cdot\vv B=0$, so the second piece dies and the first is $t^{2}(\vv x\cdot\nabla)B_{m}$. The second bracket becomes $t\big[3B_{m}-B_{m}\big]=2tB_{m}$, the $3$ coming from $\delta_{nn}=3$.

Recognise a product rule in $t$. Differentiating $t^{2}B_{m}(t\vv x)$ with respect to $t$ gives $2tB_{m}(t\vv x)+t^{2}x_{j}(\partial_{j}B_{m})(t\vv x)$, which is exactly the integrand. So by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

$$ \big(\nabla\times\vv A\big)_{m} \;=\; \Big[t^{2}B_{m}(t\vv x)\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1} \;=\; B_{m}(\vv x). \qquad\blacksquare $$

What this settles. A divergence-free field on a star-shaped region is the curl of something, and the something is written down above. That is the existence of the vector potential, quoted at Chapter 0.7 §7.3 with the note that Chapter 3.5 would prove it. ⚑ The general $p$ runs the same way — the epsilon identity is replaced by the expansion of $\dd\omega$, the hypothesis $\dd\omega=0$ is spent at the same single step, and what survives is again a total derivative in $t$ whose endpoints give $\omega$ — but that is a sketch of a proof and not a proof. What is derived here is the case $p=2$ in three dimensions; the general statement is quoted, and this is the one place in this chapter where that happens.

4.3 · What is left over is topology

Put the two halves together. Exact always implies closed, by . Closed implies exact on a star-shaped region, by §4.2. On a region that is not star-shaped the implication can fail, by §4.1. So the collection of closed forms that are not exact, counted degree by degree, is a measurement of how far the region is from being simple — and it is a measurement made with calculus that returns an answer about shape.

That measurement has a name, de Rham cohomology, promised by that name at Chapter 0.7 §7.3. We will not develop it. What matters here is that the same distinction has now appeared four times in this book — the curl-free field with a non-zero circulation, the divergence-free field with no potential, Chapter 3.4 §8's cone that is flat everywhere and yet not a plane, and — and that in every case the local information is impeccable and the global conclusion fails. Chapter 3.9 meets it once more, on a universe that can be flat everywhere and still closed, and Part VI meets it as the reason certain field configurations cannot be smoothly undone.

In plain terms 3.5.4

Having shown that anything which is a derivative gives nothing when differentiated again, the natural next question is the converse: if differentiating something gives nothing, did it come from a derivative. The answer is yes on any region that can be shrunk to a point without leaving it, and the proof is constructive rather than abstract. Integrate the object outward along straight rays from the centre, and the resulting function has exactly the original as its derivative, with the hypothesis spent at precisely one step.

On a region with a hole the answer is no, and the failure is measurable. The toolkit's old example, the field circulating around a missing origin, differentiates to nothing everywhere it is defined and yet accumulates a full turn around any loop encircling the gap. Nothing is wrong with it. What the loop integral measures is the hole, using nothing but calculus, which is a strange and useful thing for calculus to be able to do.

Two debts are settled here. The toolkit quoted the theorem guaranteeing that a field with no outflow is the swirl of something else, promised a proof in this chapter, and now has one, complete with a formula for the potential. And the same local-versus-global gap that left a rolled paper cone flat everywhere and still not a plane turns out to be this gap rather than a relative of it.

5 · One theorem instead of four

Chapter 0.7 §5.4 lined up the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the gradient theorem, Stokes' theorem and the divergence theorem in a table, observed that every row says the same thing, wrote down $\int_{M}\dd\omega=\oint_{\partial M}\omega$ as (0.7.46), and said plainly: we are quoting the general form, not deriving it; the machinery is Chapter 3.5's job. The machinery now exists. This section pays.

Announce the destination. We define what it means to integrate a $p$-form over a $p$-dimensional oriented region, check that the definition does not depend on the labelling, prove the theorem on a cube by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applied one face-pair at a time, and extend it to general regions by the chopping-and-cancelling argument of Chapter 0.7 — which this time cancels exactly rather than approximately, because the antisymmetry of §1 does the work that hand-waving did before.

5.1 · The integral of a $p$-form

Let $M$ be a $p$-dimensional region inside the manifold, parametrised by $(u^{1},\ldots,u^{p})\mapsto x^{\mu}(u)$ over some region $U$ of parameter space. Define

$$ \int_{M}\omega \;\equiv\; \int_{U}\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\big(x(u)\big)\;\pdv{x^{\mu_{1}}}{u^{1}}\cdots\pdv{x^{\mu_{p}}}{u^{p}}\;\dd u^{1}\cdots\dd u^{p}. $$

Section 1.2 did the two-dimensional case of the check that this is well defined, and the general case is the same three sentences. Changing parameters multiplies the product of tangent vectors by $p$ copies of the Jacobian matrix, contracted into a totally antisymmetric object; by Chapter 0.4 §5 that is precisely $\det J$ times the original. The measure supplies $\det J$ the other way. They cancel. The integral belongs to the region.

Two remarks that will be needed. First, no metric appears in , so this notion of integration exists on a bare manifold — §6 is where a metric is finally wanted, and it is wanted for a different job. Second, reversing the orientation of the region reverses the sign of the integral, since it swaps two of the $u$'s and the integrand is antisymmetric.

5.2 · The theorem on a square, in full

Take $M$ to be the unit square $0\le u^{1},u^{2}\le1$ and $\omega$ a one-form on it, written $\omega=P\,\dd u^{1}+Q\,\dd u^{2}$. Then by the only independent component of $\dd\omega$ is $\partial_{1}Q-\partial_{2}P$, so

$$ \int_{M}\dd\omega \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\pdv{Q}{u^{1}} - \pdv{P}{u^{2}}\Big)\,\dd u^{1}\,\dd u^{2}. $$

The $Q$ term. Do the $u^{1}$ integral first and apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in that variable alone, holding $u^{2}$ fixed:

$$ \int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\pdv{Q}{u^{1}}\,\dd u^{1}\dd u^{2} \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\Big[\,Q(1,u^{2}) - Q(0,u^{2})\,\Big]\dd u^{2}. $$

The $P$ term. Do the $u^{2}$ integral first, same move, and keep the minus sign:

$$ -\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\pdv{P}{u^{2}}\,\dd u^{2}\dd u^{1} \;=\; -\int_{0}^{1}\Big[\,P(u^{1},1) - P(u^{1},0)\,\Big]\dd u^{1}. $$

The boundary. The square's boundary is four edges. Traverse them counterclockwise, which is the orientation induced by the square's own, and evaluate on each. On the bottom edge $u^{2}=0$ with $u^{1}$ running from $0$ to $1$, the tangent has only a $u^{1}$ component, so only $P$ contributes and the edge gives $+\int_{0}^{1}P(u^{1},0)\,\dd u^{1}$. On the right edge only $Q$ contributes, upward, giving $+\int_{0}^{1}Q(1,u^{2})\,\dd u^{2}$. The top edge runs backwards and gives $-\int_{0}^{1}P(u^{1},1)\,\dd u^{1}$; the left edge runs downward and gives $-\int_{0}^{1}Q(0,u^{2})\,\dd u^{2}$. Adding the four:

$$ \oint_{\partial M}\omega \;=\; \int_{0}^{1}\!\Big[P(u^{1},0)-P(u^{1},1)\Big]\dd u^{1} \;+\; \int_{0}^{1}\!\Big[Q(1,u^{2})-Q(0,u^{2})\Big]\dd u^{2}, $$

which is plus term for term. Hence $\int_{M}\dd\omega=\oint_{\partial M}\omega$ on the square. $\blacksquare$

Notice what proved it: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, used twice, in one variable at a time. Everything else was bookkeeping about which edge runs which way, and that bookkeeping is what the antisymmetry of §1 automates.

Grind box — the same argument on a $p$-dimensional cube, with the signs counted

Let $M$ be the unit cube $0\le u^{a}\le1$ in $p$ dimensions and let $\omega$ be a $(p-1)$-form. By linearity it is enough to treat one basis term at a time, so take

$$ \omega \;=\; f\;\dd u^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\dd u^{k}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd u^{p}, $$

the hat meaning that the $k$-th differential is omitted.

The left side. Applying , every term of $\dd\omega$ except the one carrying $\partial_{k}f$ contains a repeated differential and vanishes. Moving $\dd u^{k}$ from the front into its home slot costs $k-1$ transpositions, so

$$ \dd\omega \;=\; \pdv{f}{u^{k}}\;\dd u^{k}\wedge\dd u^{1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\dd u^{k}}\cdots \;=\; (-1)^{k-1}\,\pdv{f}{u^{k}}\;\dd u^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd u^{p}. $$

Integrating over the cube and doing the $u^{k}$ integral by the Fundamental Theorem,

$$ \int_{M}\dd\omega \;=\; (-1)^{k-1}\int\Big[f\big|_{u^{k}=1} - f\big|_{u^{k}=0}\Big]\;\dd u^{1}\cdots\widehat{\dd u^{k}}\cdots\dd u^{p}. $$

The right side. The cube has $2p$ faces. On a face $u^{j}=\text{const}$ with $j\neq k$, the parametrisation of the face has $\dd u^{j}=0$, and $\omega$ contains $\dd u^{j}$, so $\omega$ restricted to that face is zero and it contributes nothing. Only the two faces $u^{k}=0$ and $u^{k}=1$ survive, and on them $\omega$ restricts to $f$ times the volume element of the face. The orientation induced on the face $u^{k}=1$ by the cube is the one for which the outward direction comes first, which is $(-1)^{k-1}$ times the natural ordering $u^{1},\ldots,\widehat{u^{k}},\ldots,u^{p}$; the face $u^{k}=0$ has outward direction reversed and so carries the opposite sign. Adding the two gives exactly the previous display. $\blacksquare$

At $p=2$, $k=1$ gives the $Q$ term of the main text and $k=2$ gives the $P$ term, minus sign and all.

5.3 · General regions, and the cancellation made exact

Assume $M$ admits such a decomposition — that it can be chopped into finitely many cells, each smoothly mapped to a cube, with piecewise-smooth boundary. That is a real hypothesis, and the callout below says what it costs. Then: chop $M$ into small cells, each of which can be mapped to a cube. Write the theorem for each cell and add. On the right, every interior face is shared by exactly two neighbouring cells — and the orientation each cell induces on that shared face is the outward one for that cell, so the two neighbours induce opposite orientations. By the second remark of §5.1 the two contributions are exact negatives, and they cancel. What survives is the sum over faces lying on the outer boundary. Hence

$$ \boxed{\;\int_{M}\dd\omega \;=\; \oint_{\partial M}\omega.\;} $$
Familiar ground — and the gap Chapter 0.7 admitted, now closed

The chopping argument is Chapter 0.7 §5.2's, word for word: add up local changes, watch the interior cancel in pairs, keep what is left on the boundary. Chapter 0.7's grind box then admitted that the argument as given there was the right picture and not a proof, because the local statement in each cell was the definition of divergence read backwards and carried an error term whose sum over many cells was not controlled.

That gap is now closed. Here the statement in each cell is not an approximation with a remainder but an exact identity, proved in §5.2 by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus; the sum of exact identities is an exact identity. And Chapter 0.7 predicted the mechanism precisely — "the cancellation is enforced algebraically by the antisymmetry of forms instead" — which is the second remark of §5.1 being used above.

What remains genuinely technical is the same in both chapters: the cells must fit the region, the region's boundary must be piecewise smooth, and the forms must be continuously differentiable. That is not a small print we are hiding, it is the honest hypothesis, and it covers everything in this book.

5.4 · The four rows, recovered

Read at each degree and each dimension.

$p=1$, $\omega$ a function. The region is a curve, its boundary is two points, and the right-hand side is the difference of $f$ between them. That is the gradient theorem of Chapter 0.7 §2.2 — and on a straight interval in one dimension it is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus itself, rows one and two of Chapter 0.7's table.

$p=2$, $\omega$ a one-form, in three dimensions. By §2.1, $\dd\omega$ is the curl. The region is a surface, its boundary a closed curve, and reads $\int_{S}(\nabla\times\vv F)\cdot\dd\vv A=\oint_{\partial S}\vv F\cdot\dd\vv r$. That is (0.7.41), Stokes' theorem — row three. Restricting to a flat region in the plane gives Green's theorem (0.7.42), which Chapter 0.7 had already shown was not independent.

$p=3$, $\omega$ a $2$-form, in three dimensions. By , $\dd\omega$ is the divergence, and with the identification $\omega_{jk}=\epsilon^{ijk}F^{i}$ turns $\oint_{\partial V}\omega$ into the flux $\oint\vv F\cdot\dd\vv A$. So is $\int_{V}(\nabla\cdot\vv F)\dd V=\oint_{\partial V}\vv F\cdot\dd\vv A$, the divergence theorem (0.7.44) — row four.

Four theorems, four names, four sets of hypotheses, four right-hand rules; one line, proved once, in any number of dimensions, on any manifold, with no metric anywhere. That was Chapter 0.7's promise, and this is it.

In plain terms 3.5.5

Very early in the book, three results with three names and three right-hand rules were lined up in a table beside the fundamental theorem itself and shown to have identical shape: whatever a derivative accumulates throughout a region is bookkept entirely on that region's skin, whether the region is an interval with two ends, a curve, a patch of surface with a rim, or a solid with a hull. The table came with a promise that the four rows would become one line once a language existed for curved spaces of any dimension. That language now exists.

The proof is smaller than its reputation. On a single cube, integrating one variable at a time lets the ordinary Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the first result of the toolkit, convert each integral into a difference of endpoint values; keeping track of which face runs which way is the remaining work, and the sign conventions built earlier do it automatically. Then chop any region into cubes and add. Every internal wall is counted twice with opposite orientation and cancels exactly.

One honest improvement over the earlier treatment deserves noting. There the cancellation argument came with an admission that it was a picture rather than a proof, because the statement inside each cell was approximate. Here it is exact, so adding many introduces no error, and the earlier chapter's own prediction of the repair is what happens.

6 · The volume element, and a divergence theorem that works on a manifold

Announce the destination. Chapter 3.6 has to integrate a scalar over all of spacetime and then discard a total derivative. Neither operation is available yet: "all of spacetime" needs a chart-independent notion of volume, and "discard a total derivative" needs a divergence theorem. This section builds both, and the tool it uses is Chapter 0.6 §8's Jacobian determinant, which will then be used a second time in Chapter 3.6 for a different purpose.

6.1 · There is essentially one top-degree form

In $n$ dimensions an $n$-form has $\binom{n}{n}=1$ independent component, by §1.3's count. So any two top-degree forms are proportional, and the whole question is what the single component does under a change of chart.

Take $\Omega=\Omega_{01\cdots(n-1)}\,\dd x^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^{n-1}$. Under a chart change $x\to x'$, the components of a $(0,n)$ tensor pick up $n$ Jacobian factors:

$$ \Omega'_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{n}} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\nu_{1}}}{x'^{\mu_{1}}}\cdots\pdv{x^{\nu_{n}}}{x'^{\mu_{n}}}\;\Omega_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{n}}. $$

Take the one independent component, $\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{n}=01\cdots(n-1)$. The right-hand side is then a totally antisymmetric contraction of $n$ copies of the Jacobian matrix, which by Chapter 0.4 §5's characterisation of the determinant is $\det(\partial x/\partial x')$ times $\Omega_{01\cdots(n-1)}$. So the single component of a top-degree form transforms by one factor of the Jacobian determinant, and by nothing else.

6.2 · The metric determinant supplies exactly that factor

Write $g\equiv\det g_{\mu\nu}$, a number at each point, negative in Lorentzian signature because the metric has one positive and three negative eigenvalues. Chapter 3.3 §1 derived the transformation law of the metric components, (3.3.3):

$$ g'_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \pdv{x^{\alpha}}{x'^{\mu}}\,\pdv{x^{\beta}}{x'^{\nu}}\;g_{\alpha\beta}. $$

Read as a matrix equation: $g'=J^{\!\top}gJ$ with $J^{\alpha}{}_{\mu}=\partial x^{\alpha}/\partial x'^{\mu}$. Take determinants, using $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$ and $\det J^{\!\top}=\det J$ from Chapter 0.4 §5:

$$ g' \;=\; \big(\det J\big)^{2}\,g \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sqrt{-g'} \;=\; \abs{\det J}\;\sqrt{-g}. $$

The square is why the sign of $\det J$ drops out, and the modulus is why the square root is harmless. Meanwhile Chapter 0.6 §8 established $\dd^{n}x=\abs{\det J}^{-1}\dd^{n}x'$ — the coordinate volume shrinks by exactly the factor the metric determinant grows by. Multiplying,

$$ \boxed{\;\sqrt{-g}\;\dd^{n}x \;=\; \sqrt{-g'}\;\dd^{n}x' .\;} $$

This is the invariant volume element. For any scalar $f$, the quantity $\int f\sqrt{-g}\,\dd^{n}x$ is a number belonging to the region, not to the chart. Chapter 0.6 §8.3 told you this was coming and even checked the answer in two flat cases: plane polar coordinates give $\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}=\sqrt{1\cdot r^{2}}=r$, which is (0.6.52), and spherical coordinates give $r^{2}\sin\theta$, which is (0.6.53). Both were obtained there by cutting up regions by hand; is the reason. The sphere of radius $a$ gives $\sqrt{a^{2}\cdot a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}=a^{2}\sin\theta$, which is the area element Chapter 3.4 §1 used to compute the region enclosed by its loop.

It is convenient to have the corresponding tensor. Define

$$ \varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} \;\equiv\; \sqrt{-g}\;[\mu\nu\rho\sigma], $$

where $[\mu\nu\rho\sigma]$ is the permutation symbol, $+1$ for an even permutation of $0123$, $-1$ for an odd one, $0$ if any index repeats. The symbol on its own is not a tensor — it has the same entries in every chart, which no tensor with four indices does — but multiplying by $\sqrt{-g}$ supplies exactly the one Jacobian factor §6.1 showed a top-degree form needs, so $\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is one. The volume element is the $4$-form $\varepsilon=\sqrt{-g}\;\dd x^{0}\wedge\dd x^{1}\wedge\dd x^{2}\wedge\dd x^{3}$.

6.3 · A formula for the derivative of a determinant

One more tool, needed here and again in Chapter 3.6 §4. Announce it: we want the change in $\det M$ produced by a small change in the entries of $M$.

Line 1 — the cofactor expansion. Expanding a determinant along its $a$-th row gives $\det M=\sum_{b}M_{ab}C_{ab}$, where the cofactor $C_{ab}$ contains no entry from row $a$. So differentiating with respect to a single entry is immediate: $\partial(\det M)/\partial M_{ab}=C_{ab}$.

Line 2 — the cofactors are the inverse. The adjugate formula of Chapter 0.4 says $(M^{-1})_{ba}=C_{ab}/\det M$, so $C_{ab}=\det M\,(M^{-1})_{ba}$.

Line 3 — chain rule. For any variation $\delta M_{ab}$ of the entries,

$$ \delta\big(\det M\big) \;=\; \sum_{a,b}C_{ab}\,\delta M_{ab} \;=\; \det M\;\sum_{a,b}\big(M^{-1}\big)_{ba}\,\delta M_{ab} \;=\; \det M\;\operatorname{tr}\!\big(M^{-1}\delta M\big). $$

This is Jacobi's formula. Applied to the metric, with $\delta$ standing for any derivation whatever,

$$ \delta g \;=\; g\;g^{\mu\nu}\,\delta g_{\mu\nu}. $$

6.4 · The divergence, and the theorem Chapter 3.6 needs

The contracted connection. Take the Christoffel formula (3.3.50) and contract the upper index with the first lower one:

$$ \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; \half\,g^{\mu\sigma}\Big(\partial_{\mu}g_{\sigma\lambda} + \partial_{\lambda}g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_{\sigma}g_{\mu\lambda}\Big). $$

The first and third terms cancel. To see it, relabel the two summed indices in the third term, swapping the names $\mu$ and $\sigma$, which is legitimate because both are dummies: it becomes $-g^{\sigma\mu}\partial_{\mu}g_{\sigma\lambda}$, and $g^{\sigma\mu}=g^{\mu\sigma}$ by symmetry of the metric, so it is minus the first term exactly. What is left is

$$ \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\mu\lambda} \;=\; \half\,g^{\mu\sigma}\,\partial_{\lambda}g_{\sigma\mu} \;=\; \frac{1}{2g}\,\partial_{\lambda}g \;=\; \partial_{\lambda}\ln\sqrt{-g}, $$

the middle step being with $\delta=\partial_{\lambda}$, and the last being $\partial_{\lambda}\ln\sqrt{-g}=\half\partial_{\lambda}\ln(-g)=\partial_{\lambda}g/(2g)$, where the minus signs inside the logarithm cancel between numerator and denominator.

The divergence. Now write out $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}$ from Chapter 3.3 §5 and substitute:

$$ \nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}V^{\mu} + \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\mu\lambda}V^{\lambda} \;=\; \partial_{\mu}V^{\mu} + V^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}\ln\sqrt{-g} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\partial_{\mu}\!\Big(\sqrt{-g}\,V^{\mu}\Big), $$

the last step being the product rule read backwards. The covariant divergence of a vector field is an ordinary divergence in disguise, with the volume factor tucked inside. No Christoffel symbol survives, which is why this identity is worth having.

The theorem. Multiply by $\sqrt{-g}$ and integrate. The result is an ordinary partial derivative under an ordinary integral sign, and §5 converts it into a boundary term. Concretely, build the $3$-form $\Theta_{\nu\rho\sigma}\equiv\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}V^{\mu}$ and take its exterior derivative; using and the permutation-symbol identity $\tfrac{1}{3!}[\mu\nu\rho\sigma][\lambda\nu\rho\sigma]=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\lambda}$, every index sum collapses and

$$ \dd\Theta \;=\; \partial_{\mu}\!\Big(\sqrt{-g}\,V^{\mu}\Big)\;\dd x^{0}\wedge\dd x^{1}\wedge\dd x^{2}\wedge\dd x^{3} \;=\; \big(\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}\big)\,\varepsilon. $$

Feeding that into gives the divergence theorem for a curved manifold:

$$ \boxed{\;\int_{\Omega}\big(\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}\big)\,\sqrt{-g}\;\dd^{4}x \;=\; \oint_{\partial\Omega}\Theta.\;} $$

The only use Chapter 3.6 makes of is this: if a variation of the metric is required to vanish near the boundary, a term of the form $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}$ in the integrand contributes nothing at all. That single sentence is what turns a four-line variation into a field equation, and §4 of the next chapter also asks what it costs when the variation does not vanish there.

In plain terms 3.5.6

An integral needs to know how much region it is adding over, and on a curved space with arbitrary labels the naive answer is wrong by a factor that changes from place to place. The correction is a single number: the square root of the determinant of the rule for measuring distances. Relabelling squeezes the boxes of the coordinate grid by a determinant and stretches the distance rule by the square of the same determinant, so the square root of the second exactly undoes the first.

Two old results are collected in passing. The extra radial weight in polar coordinates, and the more elaborate weight in spherical coordinates, were obtained much earlier by dissecting regions by hand. Both are this one determinant, and so is the area element used to measure the patch enclosed by the circuit in the previous chapter.

The section ends by building the equipment the next chapter cannot do without. When the repaired derivative is applied to a field and the result summed over its slot, all the comparison coefficients cancel and what remains is an ordinary derivative times the volume factor. Combined with the previous section's theorem, any term of that shape inside an integral is bookkept on the boundary, and vanishes whenever the thing being varied is held fixed there. The next chapter's central computation lives or dies on that sentence.

7 · Dragging a tensor along a flow

The rest of the chapter changes subject. Announce the destination. Chapter 3.3 §4 showed that the ordinary derivative of a tensor is not a tensor and needed a whole chapter of repair. Section 2 found one escape from that, available only to antisymmetric objects. Here is a second escape, available to everything, at the price of choosing a vector field first. It costs no connection, and in §8 it becomes the definition of a symmetry.

7.1 · The flow of a vector field

A vector field $X$ assigns a direction to every point, so it defines a system of ordinary differential equations,

$$ \dv{x^{\mu}}{\varepsilon} \;=\; X^{\mu}\big(x(\varepsilon)\big), $$

whose solutions are the integral curves of $X$ — the paths that follow the arrows. Chapter 0.8 §1 guarantees a unique solution through each point for smooth $X$. Following every point along its own curve for a parameter distance $\varepsilon$ defines a map of the manifold to itself, $\phi_{\varepsilon}$, called the flow. Solving to first order by Taylor's theorem (Chapter 0.3 §1),

$$ \phi_{\varepsilon}(x)^{\mu} \;=\; x^{\mu} \;+\; \varepsilon\,X^{\mu}(x) \;+\; O(\varepsilon^{2}). $$

The idea. A tensor at the point $\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)$ cannot be compared with a tensor at $x$ — that was Chapter 3.2 §5's central prohibition and nothing has repealed it. But the flow supplies a map between the two points, and any smooth map between points carries tensors along with it, by contracting each index with the map's Jacobian. So: carry the tensor at $\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)$ back to $x$ along the flow, subtract the tensor already at $x$, divide by $\varepsilon$, and let $\varepsilon\to0$. That is the Lie derivative $\mathcal{L}_{X}$.

The price is visible in the recipe. The answer depends on the whole field $X$ near $x$, not just on $X$ at $x$, because a nearby direction is needed to define the map. So $\mathcal{L}_{X}T$ is not a derivative "in the direction $X$" in the way $\nabla_{X}T$ is. It is a comparison of $T$ with the version of itself that the flow of $X$ would produce.

7.2 · Scalars, then vectors, with every index step shown

A scalar. Carrying a number requires no Jacobian, so the recipe reads

$$ \mathcal{L}_{X}f \;=\; \lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac{f\big(x+\varepsilon X\big)-f(x)}{\varepsilon} \;=\; X^{\mu}\,\partial_{\mu}f \;=\; X(f), $$

which is the directional derivative, and also the way Chapter 3.2 §4 defined a tangent vector in the first place.

A vector. Now the Jacobian is needed. From , writing $x'=\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)$,

$$ \Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\nu} \;\equiv\; \pdv{x'^{\mu}}{x^{\nu}} \;=\; \delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu} \;+\; \varepsilon\,\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu} \;+\; O(\varepsilon^{2}). $$

Carrying a vector forward along the flow multiplies its components by . We want to carry backward, so we need the inverse matrix, which to first order is obtained by flipping the sign of the small term:

$$ \big(\Lambda^{-1}\big)^{\mu}{}_{\nu} \;=\; \delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu} \;-\; \varepsilon\,\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu} \;+\; O(\varepsilon^{2}), $$

as one checks by multiplying the two and seeing the $\varepsilon$ terms cancel.

Now assemble. The vector to be carried back is $Y$ evaluated at the shifted point, and Taylor's theorem gives $Y^{\nu}(x+\varepsilon X)=Y^{\nu}(x)+\varepsilon X^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}Y^{\nu}+O(\varepsilon^{2})$. Contract with , keeping terms to first order only:

$$ \begin{aligned} \big(\text{dragged back }Y\big)^{\mu} \;&=\; \Big(\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}-\varepsilon\,\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu}\Big)\Big(Y^{\nu}+\varepsilon\,X^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}Y^{\nu}\Big)\\[3pt] &=\; Y^{\mu} \;+\; \varepsilon\,X^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}Y^{\mu} \;-\; \varepsilon\,Y^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu} \;+\; O(\varepsilon^{2}). \end{aligned} $$

Two things happened in the second line and both are worth naming. The $\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}$ hit both factors, producing the first and second terms; and the $-\varepsilon\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu}$ hit only $Y^{\nu}$, because its other product is second order. Subtract $Y^{\mu}$, divide by $\varepsilon$ and take the limit:

$$ \boxed{\;\big(\mathcal{L}_{X}Y\big)^{\mu} \;=\; X^{\lambda}\,\partial_{\lambda}Y^{\mu} \;-\; Y^{\lambda}\,\partial_{\lambda}X^{\mu} \;=\; [X,Y]^{\mu}.\;} $$

The last equality is (3.2.32), the components of the commutator of two vector fields, which Chapter 3.2 §8 built by an entirely different route — demanding that the composition of two derivations be a derivation. Two independent constructions have produced the same object, and it is worth knowing that the bracket has this second meaning: $[X,Y]$ measures the failure of $Y$ to be carried into itself by the flow of $X$.

7.3 · Covectors, and then everything

A covector is carried the other way, so its components are contracted with rather than its inverse. Repeating the computation with the sign of the Jacobian term flipped and the index in the other position,

$$ \big(\mathcal{L}_{X}\omega\big)_{\mu} \;=\; X^{\lambda}\,\partial_{\lambda}\omega_{\mu} \;+\; \omega_{\lambda}\,\partial_{\mu}X^{\lambda}. $$

The pattern is now visible and it is the same one Chapter 3.3 §5.4 found for the covariant derivative: one transport term, plus one correction per index, with the sign set by whether the index is up or down. For the case this chapter needs, a $(0,2)$ tensor,

$$ \big(\mathcal{L}_{X}T\big)_{\mu\nu} \;=\; X^{\lambda}\,\partial_{\lambda}T_{\mu\nu} \;+\; T_{\lambda\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}X^{\lambda} \;+\; T_{\mu\lambda}\,\partial_{\nu}X^{\lambda}. $$

7.4 · The partial derivatives may be replaced by covariant ones

Equations to contain plain partial derivatives, and by Chapter 3.3 §4 those are not tensors individually. The combinations are, because the construction that produced them was manifestly tensorial. There is a cleaner way to see it, and §8 needs the result.

Claim. In and , every $\partial$ may be replaced by $\nabla$ without changing the answer, for any torsion-free connection.

Proof for the vector case. Write the two covariant derivatives out using Chapter 3.3 §5:

$$ X^{\lambda}\nabla_{\lambda}Y^{\mu} - Y^{\lambda}\nabla_{\lambda}X^{\mu} \;=\; X^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}Y^{\mu} + X^{\lambda}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\lambda\nu}Y^{\nu} \;-\; Y^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}X^{\mu} - Y^{\lambda}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\lambda\nu}X^{\nu}. $$

Look only at the two $\Gamma$ terms. In the second of them, relabel the two dummy indices $\lambda$ and $\nu$, swapping their names — legitimate, since both are summed. It becomes $-X^{\lambda}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\lambda}Y^{\nu}$. Now use $\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\nu\lambda}=\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\lambda\nu}$, the torsion-free symmetry of Chapter 3.3 §7.2, and it is exactly minus the first. They cancel, and what remains is . $\blacksquare$

The $(0,2)$ case is the identical two moves applied to three $\Gamma$ terms, and it is done in the next section where it is used.

In plain terms 3.5.7

Comparing a field's values at two different points has been forbidden since the chapter on manifolds, and the chapters since bought their way round the ban by installing a rule for carrying things from place to place. Here is a second way to buy it, and the currency is different. Choose a direction field, let every point drift along its own arrow for a moment, and use that drift as transport. Carry the field back from where it drifted, and compare with what was already there.

Two features distinguish this from the earlier construction. It needs no rule for comparing directions and no notion of distance, so it is available on a bare space before any geometry is laid down. And it depends on the chosen field everywhere nearby rather than merely at the point, which is why the answer compares the field with a dragged copy of itself rather than giving a rate of change along a line.

For two direction fields the result is an object built several chapters ago by a different argument, namely the failure of two successive drifts to commute. That agreement is worth more than its algebra, since the bracket measuring whether two operations commute also measures whether one field is carried into itself by the other's flow. The next section drags the distance rule itself, and asks when dragging leaves it alone.

8 · Killing vectors: the directions in which nothing changes

Announce the destination. We ask which vector fields have flows that leave the metric exactly as they found it, translate that demand into an equation on the field, and check it on two spaces. The equation is the geometric form of the word "symmetry", and §9 turns each solution of it into a conserved quantity.

8.1 · The definition, and Killing's equation

A vector field $\xi$ is a Killing vector field if dragging the metric along its flow changes nothing:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\xi}\,g \;=\; 0. $$

Unpack it. Written out with ,

$$ \xi^{\lambda}\,\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu} \;+\; g_{\lambda\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda} \;+\; g_{\mu\lambda}\,\partial_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda} \;=\; 0. $$

Now simplify, one move at a time.

Move 1 — replace the partial derivatives by covariant ones. Section 7.4 licensed this. To see it explicitly here, expand the three covariant derivatives: $\xi^{\lambda}\nabla_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}$ contributes $-\xi^{\lambda}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\lambda\mu}g_{\sigma\nu}-\xi^{\lambda}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\sigma}$ beyond the partial derivative, while $g_{\lambda\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}$ contributes $+g_{\lambda\nu}\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\sigma}\xi^{\sigma}$ and $g_{\mu\lambda}\nabla_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda}$ contributes $+g_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\nu\sigma}\xi^{\sigma}$. Relabelling the dummies $\lambda\leftrightarrow\sigma$ in the last two and using the symmetry of $\Gamma$ in its lower indices makes them cancel the first two exactly.

Move 2 — kill the first term. Metric compatibility — imposed in Chapter 3.3 §7.1 as Demand 1, and written out in components there as (3.3.41) — says $\nabla_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}=0$. So the first term is gone altogether.

Move 3 — pull the metric inside the derivative. The same fact, $\nabla g=0$, lets the metric pass through a covariant derivative, so $g_{\lambda\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}=\nabla_{\mu}\big(g_{\lambda\nu}\xi^{\lambda}\big)=\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}$, which is the index-lowering of Chapter 3.3 §1.1 performed under the derivative. The third term becomes $\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}$ by the same move. What is left is

$$ \boxed{\;\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu} \;+\; \nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu} \;=\; 0.\;} $$

This is Killing's equation. It says the covariant derivative of $\xi$, with both indices down, is antisymmetric. In four dimensions that is ten equations, one for each symmetric index pair, on four unknown functions — heavily overdetermined, which is why most metrics have no Killing vectors at all and why the ones that do are special.

The practical test, which is the one Chapter 3.7 uses

Suppose that in some chart the metric components do not depend on one of the coordinates, say $x^{a}$ — the index $a$ here naming one particular coordinate, not being summed. Take $\xi=\partial_{a}$, whose components are $\xi^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{a}$, constants. Then in the second and third terms vanish because the derivative of a constant is zero, and the first is $\partial_{a}g_{\mu\nu}$, which vanishes by hypothesis.

So every coordinate the metric does not mention supplies a Killing vector, and finding Killing vectors is often no harder than looking at the metric. The converse is not true: a Killing vector need not be a coordinate direction in the chart you happen to be using, and two of the sphere's three below are invisible in the usual chart.

8.2 · Flat spacetime has ten

Take $g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}$ in Cartesian coordinates, where every component is constant and every $\Gamma$ vanishes, so $\nabla=\partial$ and reads $\partial_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\partial_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}=0$.

The four translations. $\xi=\partial_{a}$ for each $a=0,1,2,3$ has constant components, so both terms vanish. Four solutions.

The six rotations and boosts. Try $\xi_{\mu}=\omega_{\mu\nu}x^{\nu}$ with $\omega$ a constant array. Then $\partial_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}=\omega_{\nu\mu}$, and becomes $\omega_{\nu\mu}+\omega_{\mu\nu}=0$: the array must be antisymmetric. An antisymmetric $4\times4$ array has $\binom{4}{2}=6$ independent entries, by §1.3's count. Six more solutions.

Ten in all, and they are exactly the ten generators of the Poincaré group — four translations, three rotations, three boosts — which Chapter 2.2 obtained by demanding that the interval be preserved. Killing vectors are the infinitesimal version of "transformations that leave the geometry alone", and in flat spacetime that is the whole of special relativity's symmetry group, recovered from a differential equation.

8.3 · The sphere has three, and one obvious candidate is not among them

Take the sphere of radius $a$, with $g_{\theta\theta}=a^{2}$ and $g_{\phi\phi}=a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta$.

The easy one. No metric component mentions $\phi$, so by the practical test $\xi=\partial_{\phi}$ is Killing. It is the rotation about the polar axis.

The two hidden ones. The sphere is no more special about its poles than about anywhere else, so rotations about the other two axes must also be symmetries; in this chart they are not coordinate directions. They are

$$ \xi_{(2)} \;=\; \cos\phi\;\partial_{\theta} \;-\; \cot\theta\,\sin\phi\;\partial_{\phi}, \qquad \xi_{(3)} \;=\; -\sin\phi\;\partial_{\theta} \;-\; \cot\theta\,\cos\phi\;\partial_{\phi}, $$

and substituting either into gives zero in all three components. (Checked symbolically: for both fields all three components of $\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g$ vanish identically, and the covariant form gives the same array as the partial-derivative form , which is Move 1 verified rather than trusted.)

The candidate that fails. Try $\xi=\partial_{\theta}$, "push everything south". Its components are constant, so reduces to its first term, $\partial_{\theta}g_{\mu\nu}$, and only one metric component depends on $\theta$:

$$ \big(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\,g\big)_{\phi\phi} \;=\; \partial_{\theta}\big(a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\big) \;=\; a^{2}\sin2\theta, \qquad\text{all other components zero.} $$

So $\partial_{\theta}$ is not a Killing vector, and says precisely how it fails: the flow leaves north–south distances untouched and stretches east–west distances, at a rate that vanishes at the equator, where $\sin2\theta=0$, and is largest at colatitude $45^{\circ}$. That is a sharper statement than "the sphere is not symmetric under pushing south", and the figure measures it.

In plain terms 3.5.8

Drag the rule for measuring distances along a chosen field, demand that the result be nothing, and you have what this book means by a symmetry of a space. Written out and cleaned up using the fact that the repaired derivative ignores the distance rule, the demand becomes one compact equation on the chosen field. It is badly overdetermined, ten conditions on four functions, which is why a space picked at random has no symmetries at all, and why the ones that do are the ones anybody can solve.

Flat spacetime turns out to have exactly ten independent solutions, and they are the four shifts, the three turns and the three boosts of the previous part. That closes a loop: the transformations obtained there by insisting the interval be preserved reappear as solutions of a differential equation, with nothing about relativity fed in.

The globe supplies the cautionary case. Turning it about its axis leaves every measurable distance as it was, and so does turning it about either of the other two axes, though those are invisible in the usual grid of latitude and longitude. Sliding everything southward is not a symmetry, and the calculation says precisely how it fails: north-south spacings are untouched while east-west spacings stretch, most sharply halfway to the pole and not at all at the equator. The picture measures that rate and returns the predicted number.

9 · A conserved quantity along every geodesic

This is the section Chapter 3.7 spends. Announce the destination twice, because the result is obtained twice. First: for any Killing vector $\xi$ and any geodesic with tangent $u$, the single number $\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}$ is the same at every point of the geodesic. Four lines. Second: that number is the Noether charge of Chapter 1.4 for the symmetry generated by $\xi$, so the theorem is not an analogue of Noether's but an instance of it.

9.1 · The four-line derivation

Let $x^{\mu}(\tau)$ be a geodesic with tangent $u^{\mu}=\dd x^{\mu}/\dd\tau$, so that the geodesic equation of Chapter 3.3 §8 reads $u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu}=0$ — the curve parallel-transports its own tangent. Let $\xi$ satisfy Killing's equation . Define $Q\equiv\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}$ and differentiate along the curve.

Line 1. $Q$ is a scalar, so its rate of change along the curve is the directional derivative of it, and for a scalar the covariant and ordinary derivatives agree:

$$ \dv{Q}{\tau} \;=\; u^{\nu}\,\nabla_{\nu}\big(\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}\big). $$

Line 2 — Leibniz. The covariant derivative obeys the product rule (Chapter 3.3 §5.4), so

$$ \dv{Q}{\tau} \;=\; u^{\nu}u^{\mu}\,\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu} \;+\; \xi_{\mu}\,u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu}. $$

Line 3 — the second term is the geodesic equation. $u^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}u^{\mu}=0$, so it vanishes. Only the first survives:

$$ \dv{Q}{\tau} \;=\; u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\,\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}. $$

Line 4 — only the symmetric part survives, and it is zero. This is the index step to spell out. The factor $u^{\mu}u^{\nu}$ is symmetric under exchanging $\mu$ and $\nu$, because it is a product of two copies of the same object. Contracting a symmetric array with any array picks out only the symmetric part of the second, since the antisymmetric part contributes equal and opposite amounts under the exchange. Concretely, relabel the dummies $\mu\leftrightarrow\nu$ in , add the result to the original and divide by two:

$$ \dv{Q}{\tau} \;=\; \half\,u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\Big(\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu} + \nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}\Big) \;=\; 0, $$

the bracket vanishing by Killing's equation. Hence

$$ \boxed{\;Q \;=\; \xi_{\mu}\,u^{\mu} \;=\; \text{constant along every geodesic.}\;} $$

$\blacksquare$ Four lines, and the only inputs were the geodesic equation and Killing's equation. Note where each was spent: the geodesic equation killed the second term of , and Killing's equation killed the first. Neither could have done the other's job.

Checked numerically

The three Killing fields of §8.3 were used to build three charges $Q$, and a geodesic of the unit sphere was integrated from a generic starting point and direction over a long parameter interval. All three charges held constant to about one part in $10^{14}$, which is the integrator's own accuracy. The same computation run with $\xi=\partial_{\theta}$ — not a Killing vector — gave a charge that changed by $82\%$ over the same interval. The theorem is doing work, not bookkeeping.

9.2 · The same result as Noether's theorem

Now the identification the plan of this book has been pointing at. Chapter 1.4 proved that every continuous symmetry of an action yields a conserved quantity, and gave it as (1.4.13): $Q=\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{i}-F$, with $K_{i}$ the infinitesimal change in the coordinates and $p_{i}$ the canonical momentum. We now run that theorem on a particle in a curved spacetime and show that it returns .

The action. Chapter 3.3 §3 gave $S=-mc^{2}\int\dd\tau$ as (3.3.15), and Chapter 3.3 §8.2 showed that extremising it produces the geodesic equation, working with the integrand $F=\sqrt{g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^{\alpha}\dot x^{\beta}}$. For the present purpose the square root is a nuisance, and it can be removed. Compare the Euler–Lagrange equations of $F$ with those of $\half F^{2}$: by the product and chain rules,

$$ \dv{}{\tau}\pdv{\big(\half F^{2}\big)}{\dot x^{\mu}} - \pdv{\big(\half F^{2}\big)}{x^{\mu}} \;=\; F\left[\dv{}{\tau}\pdv{F}{\dot x^{\mu}} - \pdv{F}{x^{\mu}}\right] \;+\; \dv{F}{\tau}\,\pdv{F}{\dot x^{\mu}}. $$

Along a timelike geodesic parametrised by proper time, $F^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=c^{2}$ is constant by (3.3.58), so $\dd F/\dd\tau=0$ and the last term drops. The two brackets then vanish together. So on proper-time-parametrised curves the two Lagrangians have the same extremals, and we may use

$$ L \;=\; \half\,m\,g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}, \qquad \dot x^{\mu}\equiv\dv{x^{\mu}}{\tau}. $$

The canonical momentum. Differentiate with respect to $\dot x^{\lambda}$. The velocity appears twice, so the product rule gives two terms, and the symmetry of $g$ makes them equal:

$$ p_{\lambda} \;=\; \pdv{L}{\dot x^{\lambda}} \;=\; \half m\Big(g_{\lambda\nu}\dot x^{\nu} + g_{\mu\lambda}\dot x^{\mu}\Big) \;=\; m\,g_{\lambda\mu}u^{\mu} \;=\; m\,u_{\lambda}. $$

So the momentum conjugate to a coordinate is the four-velocity with its index lowered, times the mass — which is Chapter 2.5's four-momentum, now with a position-dependent metric doing the lowering.

The symmetry. Take the transformation $x^{\mu}\to x^{\mu}+\epsilon\,\xi^{\mu}(x)$, so that Chapter 1.4's $K$ is $\xi^{\mu}$. Compute the change in $L$. Two things vary: the metric, because it is evaluated at a shifted point, and the velocities, because the shift depends on position:

$$ \delta L \;=\; \half m\,\epsilon\,\Big[\;\xi^{\lambda}\big(\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}\big)\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu} \;+\; 2\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^{\mu}\,\dv{\big(\xi^{\nu}\big)}{\tau}\;\Big]. $$

Handle the second term. By the chain rule $\dd\xi^{\nu}/\dd\tau=\dot x^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}\xi^{\nu}$, so that term is $2g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}\xi^{\nu}$. Relabel its dummy indices so that the two velocities carry the labels $\mu$ and $\nu$: swapping the names $\nu$ and $\lambda$ turns it into $2g_{\mu\lambda}\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda}$. Using the symmetry of $g\dot x\dot x$ in $\mu\nu$, that equals $\big(g_{\lambda\nu}\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}+g_{\mu\lambda}\partial_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda}\big)\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}$. Substituting back,

$$ \delta L \;=\; \half m\,\epsilon\;\Big(\underbrace{\xi^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu} + g_{\lambda\nu}\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\lambda} + g_{\mu\lambda}\partial_{\nu}\xi^{\lambda}}_{\textstyle =\;\big(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g\big)_{\mu\nu}}\Big)\,\dot x^{\mu}\dot x^{\nu}. $$

The bracket is exactly. The change in the Lagrangian under a coordinate shift is the Lie derivative of the metric, contracted with two velocities — which is the cleanest possible statement of what a Killing vector is for. If $\xi$ is Killing, $\delta L=0$; the symmetry hypothesis of Chapter 1.4 §1.2 is satisfied with the allowed total derivative $F$ equal to zero.

The charge. Feed $K^{\mu}=\xi^{\mu}$, $p_{\mu}=mu_{\mu}$ and $F=0$ into (1.4.13):

$$ Q_{\text{Noether}} \;=\; p_{\mu}\,\xi^{\mu} \;-\; 0 \;=\; m\,\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}, $$

which is $m$ times . The two derivations agree, and they agree for a reason rather than by luck: §9.1's Line 4 discarded the antisymmetric part of $\nabla\xi$, and §9.2's discarded it too, both times because the velocity appears twice.

Why this is the chapter's most expensive result

Chapter 3.7 has to solve the geodesic equation outside a spherical mass. Written out, that is four coupled second-order differential equations for $t(\tau),r(\tau),\theta(\tau),\phi(\tau)$ with position-dependent coefficients, which is not a tractable problem by hand.

The Schwarzschild metric does not mention $t$ and does not mention $\phi$. By §8.1's practical test that is two Killing vectors, and by two constants of the motion — which Chapter 3.7 will call $E$ and $L$ and which are, by , precisely the energy and angular momentum of Chapter 1.4 §3. Two of the four equations are thereby integrated once and for all. Spherical symmetry disposes of $\theta$. What is left is a single first-order equation for $r(\tau)$, which is Chapter 0.8's business and which Chapter 3.7 reads as motion in an effective potential.

Without this section, Chapter 3.7 would be a wall of algebra with no visible structure. With it, that chapter is mostly Chapter 1.3's phase-plane reasoning applied to one variable. It is worth noticing that the saving is not computational cleverness: it is symmetry, converted into conserved quantities by a theorem proved four parts ago for pendulums.

In plain terms 3.5.9

Take any direction along which the geometry does not change, and any freely falling body. The component of the body's velocity along that direction, measured with the geometry's own rule for taking components, never changes. The proof runs to four lines and each input is spent once: the free-fall condition removes one term and the no-change condition the other, and neither could have removed the term the other did.

Then comes the identification making this more than a trick. Feed the same situation to the theorem proved long ago for mechanical systems, which says every continuous symmetry of the quantity attached to a history supplies something conserved. The change in that quantity under sliding the coordinates along the chosen direction turns out to be the dragging operation of two sections ago, applied to the distance rule and contracted twice with the velocity. So the geometric and mechanical conditions are one condition, and the conserved quantity produced is the same quantity.

This is where the chapter earns its place. Two chapters from now the geometry outside a star will mention neither time nor the angle around the axis, which by the practical test hands over two constants at once. Those constants are the energy and the angular momentum, and having them reduces a tangle of four coupled equations to one equation in one variable, of exactly the kind the toolkit taught how to read.

10 · Electromagnetism in three symbols

A language is worth having if it says something short that was long. Here is the test. Announce the destination: all of classical electromagnetism, including charge conservation and gauge freedom and the version valid in curved spacetime, written as $F=\dd A$, $\dd F=0$, $\dd\!\star\!F=\mu_{0}\star\!J$ — and, more useful than the compression, a clean statement of which half of the subject needs a metric and which half does not.

10.1 · The one new operation

Section 1.3's table is symmetric: in four dimensions the counts run $1,4,6,4,1$, so $p$-forms and $(4-p)$-forms have the same number of components. An operation exchanging them therefore has a chance of existing, and the volume tensor supplies it. Define the Hodge star of a $p$-form by contracting it into the last $p$ slots of $\varepsilon$:

$$ \big(\star\omega\big)_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4-p}} \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{p!}\;\varepsilon_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{4-p}\,\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{p}}\;\omega^{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{p}}. $$

Note what just happened. The indices of $\omega$ were raised to be contracted, and raising needs the inverse metric. So $\star$ is the first operation in this chapter that requires a metric. Everything before it — forms, the wedge, $\dd$, $\dd^{2}=0$, Stokes — needed none. Keep that distinction in view for §10.3, where it separates the two halves of Maxwell's equations.

The two cases needed are $p=2$ and $p=1$:

$$ \big(\star F\big)_{\rho\sigma} \;=\; \half\,\varepsilon_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}\,F^{\mu\nu}, \qquad \big(\star J\big)_{\lambda\rho\sigma} \;=\; \varepsilon_{\lambda\rho\sigma\mu}\,j^{\mu}. $$

10.2 · The three statements

First. Section 3.3 already established $F=\dd A$, which reproduced Chapter 2.6's definition (2.6.9) character for character. Six components from four.

Second. $\dd F=\dd\dd A=0$ by , and expanding it gave (2.6.25), which is $\nabla\cdot\vv B=0$ and Faraday's law. Four components.

Third. The inhomogeneous half. Chapter 2.6's (2.6.19) is $\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_{0}j^{\nu}$. The claim is that this is

$$ \boxed{\;\dd\!\star\!F \;=\; \mu_{0}\,\star\!J.\;} $$

Both sides are $3$-forms, with $\binom{4}{3}=4$ components, matching the four equations. Verifying it is a matter of expanding both sides in flat coordinates and comparing; the computation is short but sign-infested, so it sits in the grind box, and the result is that holds exactly with the conventions fixed in and — contracted indices last on $\varepsilon$, and $\varepsilon_{0123}=+\sqrt{-g}$.

Grind box — checking $\dd\!\star\!F=\mu_{0}\star\!J$, and why the sign is a convention worth fixing loudly

Work in flat coordinates where $\sqrt{-g}=1$ and $\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is the plain permutation symbol with $\varepsilon_{0123}=+1$. Take one component of each side, say $(\lambda\rho\sigma)=(0,1,2)$.

Left side. By at $p=2$, the component is the three-term cyclic sum $\partial_{0}(\star F)_{12}+\partial_{1}(\star F)_{20}+\partial_{2}(\star F)_{01}$. Each entry of $\star F$ is, by , one term of $F$ with both indices up and the complementary index pair: $(\star F)_{12}=\half\varepsilon_{12\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=\varepsilon_{1203}F^{03}=F^{03}$, using $\varepsilon_{1203}=+1$ and the fact that the two surviving orderings $(0,3)$ and $(3,0)$ contribute equally and cancel the $\half$. Likewise $(\star F)_{20}=\varepsilon_{2013}F^{13}=F^{13}$ and $(\star F)_{01}=\varepsilon_{0123}F^{23}=F^{23}$. So the left side is $\partial_{0}F^{03}+\partial_{1}F^{13}+\partial_{2}F^{23}$, which is $\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu3}$ with the $\mu=3$ term absent — and that term is $\partial_{3}F^{33}=0$ by antisymmetry. So the left side is exactly $\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu3}$.

Right side. $\mu_{0}(\star J)_{012}=\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{012\mu}j^{\mu}=\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0123}j^{3}=\mu_{0}j^{3}$.

Equating gives $\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu3}=\mu_{0}j^{3}$, which is the $\nu=3$ component of (2.6.19). The other three components come out the same way; the whole system was also verified symbolically, for a general $A_{\mu}(x)$, with all four triples checked and all four agreeing in sign.

Why the loud convention. Had we instead defined $\star$ on a one-form by putting the contracted index first, writing $(\star J)_{\lambda\rho\sigma}=\varepsilon_{\mu\lambda\rho\sigma}j^{\mu}$, every component would flip sign, since moving an index past three others costs $(-1)^{3}$, and would carry a minus. Books differ here exactly as they differ over the sign of the Riemann tensor. The rule adopted above — contracted indices last, at every degree — is self-consistent and is what makes the plus sign correct. Check it before importing a formula.

10.3 · What the compression reveals

(i) Charge conservation is free. Apply $\dd$ to . The left side is $\dd\dd(\star F)=0$ by . Hence

$$ \dd\!\star\!J \;=\; 0, $$

whose single component, expanded the same way as the grind box did, is $\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}=0$ — the continuity equation of Chapter 0.7 §6 and Chapter 2.6 §1. Charge conservation is not an extra postulate; it is $\dd^{2}=0$ applied to the field equation. Chapter 0.7's Problem 4 found that Maxwell's displacement current was forced by charge conservation. This is that argument run backwards and compressed into one symbol.

(ii) Gauge freedom is free too. Replacing $A\to A+\dd\chi$ for any function $\chi$ leaves $F=\dd A$ unchanged, again by . That is Chapter 2.6 §4.1's gauge transformation, and the reason it exists is the same one line. And by §4, the potentials giving a particular $F$ differ by a closed one-form, which on a region without holes is exact and hence of the form $\dd\chi$ — so §4's topology is the fine print on the phrase "the potential is determined up to a gauge transformation".

(iii) The asymmetry between the two halves, named. This is the observation the whole section was for. Look at what each statement needs:

StatementNeeds a metric?Status

$\dd F=0$noidentity — cannot fail
$\dd\!\star\!F=\mu_{0}\star\!J$yes, inside $\star$field equation — physics

Chapter 2.6 §3.4 noticed that two of Maxwell's equations were bookkeeping and two were physics, and Chapter 2.6's forward-looking warning box predicted that in this language "the visible asymmetry between $\dd F$ and $\dd\!\star\!F$" would be the reason. It is: the star is where the geometry lives, and only the sourced half of Maxwell touches it.

(iv) Curved spacetime costs nothing. Because $\dd$ needs no connection (§2.2) and $\varepsilon$ already carries $\sqrt{-g}$ (§6.2), the three statements above are the equations of electromagnetism on any curved manifold, unchanged. In components the sourced half becomes, using §6.4's trick,

$$ \nabla_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\partial_{\mu}\!\Big(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\mu\nu}\Big) \;=\; \mu_{0}\,j^{\nu}. $$

The middle equality is worth its two lines. Expanding $\nabla_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}$ gives $\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}+\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\mu\lambda}F^{\lambda\nu}+\Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}F^{\mu\lambda}$. The last term vanishes: $\Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\mu\lambda}$ is symmetric in $\mu\lambda$ and $F^{\mu\lambda}$ is antisymmetric, so the contraction is zero. The first two are exactly applied to each fixed value of $\nu$. So on a curved manifold Maxwell's equations are Maxwell's equations with $\partial\to\nabla$, and the only visible change is a factor of $\sqrt{-g}$ — the whole of "electromagnetism in a gravitational field", in one line, before the gravitational field equations have even been written down.

In plain terms 3.5.10

As a test of the new language, classical electromagnetism is rewritten in it and comes to three short statements: the field is the derivative of the potential, the derivative of the field is nothing, and the derivative of the field's complement is the current. The compression is pleasant but not the point. What it exposes is which parts of the subject depend on geometry and which do not.

Only one operation in the three requires knowing distances and angles, namely the one swapping a description in terms of two slots for one in terms of the complementary two. That operation appears in exactly one of the three, the one with a source in it. So the half of Maxwell's equations the previous part identified as empty bookkeeping is also the half never touching the geometry, and the half carrying the physics is the half that does. The earlier chapter noticed the first fact and predicted the second; here they are the same fact.

Two things then come for free. Applying the derivative twice to the sourced statement gives nothing on the left, forcing the current to be conserved, so charge conservation follows from the field equation rather than being an extra law. And adding the derivative of any function to the potential changes no field, which is the freedom called gauge, arriving from the same line. Both survive intact on a curved space.

11 · Worked examples

Worked example 1 — the area of a region, from its boundary alone

Use to show that the area enclosed by a closed plane curve is $\half\oint(x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x)$, and evaluate it for a circle and for a triangle.

The form. On the plane take the one-form $\omega=\half\big(x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x\big)$. Its exterior derivative, by with $\omega_{x}=-y/2$ and $\omega_{y}=x/2$, is

$$ \big(\dd\omega\big)_{xy} \;=\; \partial_{x}\!\left(\frac x2\right) - \partial_{y}\!\left(\frac{-y}{2}\right) \;=\; \half+\half \;=\; 1, $$

so $\dd\omega=\dd x\wedge\dd y$, the area element. Feeding that into with $M$ the enclosed region:

$$ \text{Area}(M) \;=\; \int_{M}\dd x\wedge\dd y \;=\; \int_{M}\dd\omega \;=\; \oint_{\partial M}\omega \;=\; \half\oint_{\partial M}\big(x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x\big). $$

A circle of radius $R$. Parametrise the boundary by $(R\cos t,R\sin t)$. Then $x\,\dd y=R\cos t\cdot R\cos t\,\dd t$ and $-y\,\dd x=-R\sin t\cdot(-R\sin t)\,\dd t$, so the integrand is $\half R^{2}(\cos^{2}t+\sin^{2}t)\,\dd t=\half R^{2}\dd t$ and the integral over $t\in[0,2\pi]$ is $\pi R^{2}$. ✓

A triangle with vertices $\vv a,\vv b,\vv c$. On the straight edge from $\vv a$ to $\vv b$, parametrise $\vv x=\vv a+t(\vv b-\vv a)$; the integrand $\half(x\,\dd y-y\,\dd x)$ works out to the constant $\half(a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x})\,\dd t$, since the terms quadratic in $t$ cancel between the two products. Summing the three edges gives $\half\big[(a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x})+(b_{x}c_{y}-b_{y}c_{x})+(c_{x}a_{y}-c_{y}a_{x})\big]$, which is the surveyor's formula for the area of a triangle. ✓

The remark worth keeping. Compare $\omega$ here with the angle form of §4.1: they are the same numerator, and the only difference is that §4.1 divides by $x^{2}+y^{2}$, which is what makes it closed and what makes it singular at the origin. Here $\dd\omega=1\neq0$, so $\omega$ is not closed, there is no contradiction with §4, and the integral measures area rather than winding. A planimeter — the mechanical instrument that measures the area of a shape by being traced around its outline — is this formula made of brass.

Worked example 2 — the flat plane in polar coordinates: three Killing vectors, three conserved quantities

Find all the Killing vectors of $\dd s^{2}=\dd r^{2}+r^{2}\dd\phi^{2}$, and compute the conserved quantities they supply along a geodesic. This is a case where the answer is known in advance, which is the point of doing it.

The easy one. No metric component mentions $\phi$, so by §8.1's practical test $\xi_{(1)}=\partial_{\phi}$ is Killing.

The two the chart hides. The plane is flat, so by §8.2 it has translations too; but $\partial_{r}$ is not a translation, and indeed $\big(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{r}}g\big)_{\phi\phi}=\partial_{r}(r^{2})=2r\neq0$, so it is not Killing. Convert the Cartesian translations instead. With $x=r\cos\phi$ and $y=r\sin\phi$, the chain rule gives

$$ \xi_{(2)}=\partial_{x} \;=\; \cos\phi\;\partial_{r} \;-\; \frac{\sin\phi}{r}\;\partial_{\phi}, \qquad \xi_{(3)}=\partial_{y} \;=\; \sin\phi\;\partial_{r} \;+\; \frac{\cos\phi}{r}\;\partial_{\phi}. $$

Substituting either into gives zero in all three components — verified symbolically. Three Killing vectors, which is $n(n+1)/2$ at $n=2$: two translations and one rotation, exactly the symmetries of the Euclidean plane.

The conserved quantities. A geodesic of the plane is a straight line; parametrise it by arc length $s$, so $u=(\dot r,\dot\phi)$. Lower the index on each Killing vector with $g=\mathrm{diag}(1,r^{2})$ and contract:

$$ Q_{(1)} \;=\; g_{\phi\phi}\,\xi^{\phi}_{(1)}\,\dot\phi \;=\; r^{2}\dot\phi, $$ $$ Q_{(2)} \;=\; \cos\phi\;\dot r \;-\; \frac{\sin\phi}{r}\cdot r^{2}\dot\phi \;=\; \cos\phi\,\dot r - r\sin\phi\,\dot\phi \;=\; \dv{}{s}\big(r\cos\phi\big) \;=\; \dot x. $$

The last step is the product rule read backwards. Similarly $Q_{(3)}=\dot y$. So the three charges are $\dot x$, $\dot y$ and $r^{2}\dot\phi$ — the two components of the velocity and the angular momentum per unit mass about the origin, all constant along a straight line, which they visibly are. The machinery returned the answer everyone already knew, which is what one wants from a first test of it; and note that $Q_{(1)}=r^{2}\dot\phi$ is Kepler's second law, which Chapter 1.4 §3.3 obtained from rotational symmetry and which arrives here from the geometry of the plane.

12 · Your turn

Problem 1 — why vector calculus is a three-dimensional accident

(a) Show that for two one-forms in three dimensions, the components of $\alpha\wedge\beta$ are the components of the cross product $\vv\alpha\times\vv\beta$, up to the identification $\omega_{jk}=\epsilon^{ijk}F^{i}$ used in §2.1. (b) Using §1.3's count, explain why that identification is available in three dimensions and in no other. (c) In four dimensions, how many independent components does the wedge of two one-forms have, and why is there no "cross product" of two four-vectors? (d) Chapter 0.7 §4.4 said the curl is a vector only in three dimensions and promised the reason later. State it.

Solution

(a) By , $(\alpha\wedge\beta)_{jk}=\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}-\alpha_{k}\beta_{j}$. Its $(1,2)$ component is $\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1}$, which is $(\vv\alpha\times\vv\beta)^{3}$; the other two independent components are the other two components of the cross product, in cyclic order.

(b) The identification pairs a $2$-form with a vector, so it needs $\binom{n}{2}=\binom{n}{1}$, that is $n(n-1)/2=n$, whose only solution with $n>1$ is $n=3$. Three dimensions is the unique case where a pair of directions and a single direction are the same amount of information.

(c) Six, by §1.3's table. A six-component object cannot be a four-vector, so there is no cross product. The six-component antisymmetric object in four dimensions is exactly the type of the electromagnetic field tensor, which is why $\vv E$ and $\vv B$ mix under boosts (Chapter 2.6 §5): they are two halves of one $2$-form, not two vectors.

(d) The curl of a vector field is really the exterior derivative of a one-form, which is a $2$-form. Calling the result a vector uses (b)'s coincidence, and outside three dimensions there is nothing to call it. Chapter 0.7 §4.3's splitting of the Jacobian into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part is the same statement: the antisymmetric part has $n(n-1)/2$ components and only accidentally has $n$.

Problem 2 — a hole in space, measured by a $2$-form

On $\R^{3}$ with the origin removed, define the $2$-form corresponding under §2.1's identification to the radial field $\vv B=\hat{\vv r}/r^{2}$. (a) Show $\dd\omega=0$. (b) Show $\oint_{S}\omega=4\pi$ over any sphere centred on the origin. (c) Conclude that $\omega$ is closed but not exact, and say what fails in §4.2's proof. (d) Chapter 0.7 §7.3 said a divergence-free field is a curl "on regions without cavities". Restate that caveat in the language of this chapter, and say what physical object this counterexample would be.

Solution

(a) Under the identification, $\dd\omega$ is $\nabla\cdot\vv B$ times the volume form (). For $\vv B=\vv r/r^{3}$, $\partial_{i}(x^{i}/r^{3})=3/r^{3}-3x^{i}x^{i}/r^{5}=3/r^{3}-3/r^{3}=0$ away from the origin.

(b) On a sphere of radius $R$ the field is $\hat{\vv r}/R^{2}$, normal to the surface and of constant magnitude, so the flux is $(1/R^{2})\times 4\pi R^{2}=4\pi$, independent of $R$.

(c) If $\omega=\dd\alpha$ then by $\oint_{S}\omega=\oint_{S}\dd\alpha=\oint_{\partial S}\alpha=0$, since a sphere has no boundary. That contradicts (b), so $\omega$ is not exact. What fails in §4.2 is the hypothesis: the region is not star-shaped, because every ray from a would-be centre to the far side passes through the missing origin, so the integral defining $K\omega$ is not available.

(d) "Divergence-free implies a curl" is "closed $2$-forms are exact", which holds when the region has no enclosed cavity — precisely the condition making the $2$-form's integral over every closed surface vanish. The counterexample is a magnetic monopole: a field with $\nabla\cdot\vv B=0$ everywhere it is defined and yet no globally defined vector potential. That is why monopoles, if they exist, force a rethink of the potential rather than of Maxwell's equations, and it is the seed of a topological argument Part VI returns to.

Problem 3 — a symmetry that stretches

Take the dilation field $X=x^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}$ on flat spacetime. (a) Compute $\mathcal{L}_{X}\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and show it is $2\eta_{\mu\nu}$, so $X$ is not Killing. (b) Show nonetheless that the flow of $X$ preserves all angles, in the sense that it rescales every length by the same factor at a given point. Such a field is called a conformal Killing vector. (c) Does $\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}$ remain constant along a geodesic for such an $X$? Redo §9.1 and identify exactly where it fails and what it fails by. (d) For which kind of particle would the failure vanish?

Solution

(a) $X^{\mu}=x^{\mu}$, so $\partial_{\nu}X^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}$. In the first term vanishes because $\eta$ is constant, and the other two give $\eta_{\mu\nu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}=2\eta_{\mu\nu}$.

(b) $\mathcal{L}_{X}g=2g$ says the dragged metric is a multiple of the original, and multiplying a metric by a positive number multiplies every squared length by it while leaving every ratio, and hence every angle, alone.

(c) Lines 1 to 3 of §9.1 are unchanged, so $\dd Q/\dd\tau=\half u^{\mu}u^{\nu}(\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu})=\half u^{\mu}u^{\nu}(\mathcal{L}_{X}g)_{\mu\nu}$. For a conformal Killing vector that bracket is $2\Omega g_{\mu\nu}$ for some function $\Omega$, here the constant $1$, so $\dd Q/\dd\tau=\Omega\,g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}$. The failure is proportional to the norm of the tangent.

(d) A massless one. For a null geodesic $g_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=0$, so the failure vanishes and $Q$ is conserved after all. Conformal symmetries give conserved quantities for light but not for matter, which is why conformal invariance is a symmetry of the massless theory only — a fact Chapter 7.3 builds an entire chapter on.

Problem 4 — Clairaut's relation, and a rehearsal for Chapter 3.7

On the sphere of radius $a$, take $\xi=\partial_{\phi}$. (a) Write down the conserved quantity supplies for a geodesic parametrised by arc length. (b) Let $\psi$ be the angle between the geodesic and the local circle of latitude. Show that the conserved quantity is $a\sin\theta\cos\psi$, so that $\sin\theta\cos\psi$ is constant along any great circle — Clairaut's relation. (c) Deduce the highest latitude a given great circle reaches, and check against Chapter 3.3 §9. (d) Say which two features of the Schwarzschild metric will play the roles of $\theta$-independence and $\phi$-independence in Chapter 3.7, and what the two charges will be called.

Solution

(a) $\xi^{\mu}=(0,1)$, so $\xi_{\mu}=g_{\mu\phi}$ and $Q=\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}=g_{\phi\phi}\dot\phi=a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\;\dot\phi$.

(b) With arc length as parameter, $a^{2}\dot\theta^{2}+a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\dot\phi^{2}=1$. The unit tangent has an eastward component $a\sin\theta\,\dot\phi$ and a southward component $a\dot\theta$, so by definition of $\psi$ the eastward component is $\cos\psi$. Hence $Q=a\sin\theta\cdot\big(a\sin\theta\,\dot\phi\big)=a\sin\theta\cos\psi$.

(c) The geodesic is highest — smallest $\theta$ — where it runs due east, $\psi=0$, since $\sin\theta$ must then be as small as possible for fixed $Q$. So $\sin\theta_{\min}=Q/a=\sin\theta_{0}\cos\psi_{0}$ for any starting point. Chapter 3.3 §9 showed the geodesics are great circles, and a great circle inclined at angle $\iota$ to the equator reaches colatitude $\theta_{\min}=\pi/2-\iota$; both statements say the same thing, since a great circle crosses the equator at exactly the angle $\iota$. ✓

(d) The Schwarzschild metric will depend on neither $t$ nor $\phi$. By §8.1's test those give Killing vectors $\partial_{t}$ and $\partial_{\phi}$, and by two conserved charges, which Chapter 3.7 calls the energy per unit mass $E$ and the angular momentum per unit mass $L$. Part (a) here is the calculation of $L$, done on a sphere to keep the algebra visible.

The brick you just laid

You have a language for integration on a curved space. Demanding that an integral belong to a region and not to a labelling forced its integrand to be totally antisymmetric — the determinant of Chapter 0.4, doing physics. Those objects are differential forms, and antisymmetrising the ordinary derivative gives them a derivative $\dd$ that is a tensor with no connection anywhere, because the connection's chart-dependent piece is symmetric in exactly the two slots being antisymmetrised.

Three debts, collected. Applying $\dd$ twice gives zero, in three lines, and that single statement is Chapter 0.7's $\nabla\times\nabla=0$, Chapter 0.7's $\nabla\cdot\nabla\times=0$ and Chapter 2.6's discovery that half of Maxwell's equations are bookkeeping. It is also the algebraic shadow of the fact that a boundary has no boundary. The converse — closed implies exact — holds on a region you can shrink to a point, proved in four lines here, which is Chapter 0.7 §7.3's quoted Poincaré lemma and hands you a formula for the vector potential. And $\int_{M}\dd\omega=\oint_{\partial M}\omega$ is Chapter 0.7's four theorems, one line, proved on a cube by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and extended by the cancellation argument that chapter described and could not complete.

Two tools for what comes next. The invariant volume element $\sqrt{-g}\,\dd^{n}x$ makes "integrate over spacetime" mean something, and Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant gives $\nabla_{\mu}V^{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}(\sqrt{-g}V^{\mu})/\sqrt{-g}$ — the sentence that lets Chapter 3.6 throw a term away.

And the reason Chapter 3.7 is possible. The Lie derivative compares a tensor with a copy of itself dragged along a flow, needing no connection; setting $\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g=0$ defines a Killing vector and yields . Flat spacetime has ten of them and they are the Poincaré generators of Part II. Every Killing vector makes $\xi_{\mu}u^{\mu}$ constant along every geodesic — four lines — and running Chapter 1.4's theorem on the same system returns the same charge, with $\delta L$ turning out to be $\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g$ contracted with two velocities. Noether's theorem and Killing's equation are one thing.

Where this gets spent. Chapter 3.6 uses §5's Stokes theorem, §6's volume element and determinant formula, and §6.4's divergence identity, to vary the Einstein–Hilbert action and derive the field equations a second time. Chapter 3.7 uses §9 twice, and would be unreadable without it. Chapter 3.9 uses the absence of a timelike Killing vector to explain why energy is not conserved in an expanding universe, collecting Chapter 1.4 §4.3's honest note. Chapter 6.3 rebuilds §10's $F=\dd A$ with an internal space in place of spacetime, at which point the gauge freedom that fell out of $\dd^{2}=0$ here becomes the organising principle of every force in nature.
